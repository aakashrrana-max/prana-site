What All-on-X means

A full arch is every tooth on the top or the bottom. With All-on-X, dental implants are placed in the jaw and one custom bridge of teeth is screwed onto them. The bridge doesn't come out at night and you don't use adhesive. Only your dentist takes it off, usually for a professional cleaning or a repair.

The "X" stands for the number of implants in each arch. Four is the minimum for most fixed full-arch plans. Many people end up with five or six, and some need more. The number is a clinical choice, not an upgrade tier. It comes from how much bone you have, where that bone sits, how hard you bite, and whether we are working on the upper or the lower jaw.

You may also see the terms full-arch implants, fixed implant bridge, hybrid denture or teeth in a day. They usually describe the same basic idea. The real differences are the number and angle of the implants, the timing, and the material of the final teeth, which is why a good quote spells out all three.

How the number of implants is chosen

Implants need enough healthy bone to hold firmly. When teeth have been missing or loose for a while, the bone that held them tends to shrink, and that changes what is possible. Planning starts at your first visit, which is free: an exam, a digital 3D scan of your teeth and bite, and a smile preview that shows what new teeth could look like.

To measure the bone itself, a full-arch plan also needs a CBCT, which is a 3D X-ray of the jaw. It is a separate step, and its cost is confirmed with you before it is taken. The CBCT shows the height, width and density of your bone, plus where the nerve in the lower jaw and the sinuses above the upper teeth sit. Dr. Rana then decides how many implants each arch needs, based on a few things:

  • Bone volume and density. Thinner or softer bone often calls for more implants to share the load.
  • Upper or lower arch. Upper bone is often softer, and the sinuses can limit space at the back, so upper arches frequently get more implants than lower ones.
  • Bite force. People who grind or clench, or who simply have a strong bite, may do better with more support points.
  • Arch shape and size. A wide arch can need extra implants to support the bridge evenly from end to end.
  • Health and budget. Every implant adds cost and treatment time, so we don't add one without a reason we can show you on your images.

All-on-X vs All-on-4®: what's the difference?

All-on-4 is one specific way to restore a full arch. It uses four implants, with the two back implants tilted so they can reach denser bone toward the front of the jaw. That angle often lets people with some bone loss avoid a graft. It is a well-established approach, and for many people four implants is enough. Our All-on-4 page covers that option in detail.

All-on-X is the wider family. It includes four-implant plans as well as plans with five, six or more. Extra implants spread chewing force across more points and give the bridge some backup if one implant runs into trouble years later. They also cost more and need enough bone in more places. More is not automatically better: four well-placed implants in good bone can do just as well as six. Our guide to All-on-4 vs All-on-6 goes through that tradeoff.

So the useful question isn't which name to buy. It is how many implants your jaw needs to hold a fixed bridge reliably, and your images answer that.

All-on-X vs dentures

A conventional denture rests on your gums and is held by suction or adhesive. It is the lowest-cost way to replace a full arch, and it needs no surgery. The tradeoffs are well known. Dentures can slip when you eat or talk, a full upper denture covers the roof of your mouth, and they come out every night. Because nothing loads the bone underneath, the jaw usually keeps shrinking, so dentures tend to loosen and need relining or remaking over the years.

Fixed full-arch teeth stay put, leave the palate open, and let many people chew a wider range of foods. The implants also load the jawbone, which helps slow that shrinking. In exchange, the cost is higher, there is surgery and a healing period, and cleaning takes a different routine.

There is a middle option too: an implant overdenture, which snaps onto two or more implants but still comes out for cleaning. It usually costs less than fixed teeth and holds much better than a regular denture. For a longer side-by-side, see full-arch implants vs dentures.

Who All-on-X suits

Fixed full-arch teeth are usually considered when most or all of the teeth in an arch are missing, failing or loose, or when someone has worn a denture for years and wants teeth that stay in place. Common situations include:

  • Several teeth that are broken, badly decayed or loose from advanced gum disease
  • A denture that slips, rubs sore spots or makes eating a chore
  • A mouth full of old crowns, bridges and root canals that keep failing one after another
  • Wanting teeth you never take out at night

When we may suggest waiting, or another option

Implants need to heal into bone, so general health matters. Uncontrolled diabetes, smoking or vaping, some bone medications and past radiation to the jaw can raise the risk of implant problems, and some of these need your doctor's input first. Jaw growth also has to be finished, so this isn't a treatment for children or young teens.

If some of your own teeth can be saved, we will tell you. Taking out healthy teeth to fit a package is not something we do, and sometimes a partial plan, a few single dental implants or an overdenture is the more sensible answer.

When we refer

The All-on-X process, visit by visit

1. Free first visit

An exam, a digital 3D scan and a smile preview. We listen to what bothers you, look at your teeth and gums, and tell you honestly whether full-arch treatment makes sense or whether a smaller fix would do. This first visit doesn't include a cleaning, X-rays or a written treatment plan, so you won't leave with a final price that day.

2. Imaging and a written plan

Next comes the CBCT to measure your bone, plus any X-rays needed. Dr. Rana plans the implant positions digitally and sets the number for each arch. You then get an itemized written quote covering implants, abutments, extractions, any grafting, sedation, the teeth you wear while healing and the final teeth. Nothing is scheduled until you have it and have had time to think.

3. Treatment day

Any remaining failing teeth are removed, the implants are placed and, in many cases, a fixed provisional bridge is attached the same day. That depends on the implants being stable enough when they go in. If they aren't, we protect them while they heal and attach fixed teeth a little later. Local anesthetic numbs the area, and oral sedation is available if you are anxious.

4. Healing

Over the next few months, commonly about three to six depending on your bone, the implants fuse with the jaw. You eat softer foods during this time and come in for checks. Swelling and soreness for the first several days are common. Our implant recovery timeline explains what each stage usually feels like.

5. Final teeth

Once the implants have healed, we take new scans and records and make your final bridge in our in-house lab. You try it in, we fine-tune the bite, shape and shade, and then it is fixed in place.

6. Maintenance

Regular cleanings and checks keep the implants and gums healthy. From time to time we take the bridge off so the implants and its underside can be cleaned properly, then put it back.

Full mouth dental implants: upper and lower

Full mouth dental implants means replacing the teeth in both jaws with fixed bridges on implants: one All-on-X arch on top and one on the bottom. Some people need both arches done; others need only one, with natural teeth, a partial or a denture on the other side.

Doing both arches is usually planned as one treatment, often with both arches placed on the same day, so you heal once. The upper jaw tends to have softer bone and often needs more implants than the lower jaw, so the two arches don't always get the same number. Each arch is priced separately on your written quote, starting from $9,999 per arch.

If only one arch is failing now and the other is likely to follow, we talk through the timing honestly: doing both together can be simpler, but there is no reason to remove teeth that still have years of life in them.

The honest downsides

Fixed full-arch teeth change lives for many people, but they are not the right choice for everyone. Before you decide, weigh these:

  • It is surgery. Expect swelling, soreness and a soft-food period. Many people take a few days off work, depending on the job and how much is done.
  • It is a big cost. Even with financing it is a major commitment, and treating both arches roughly doubles it.
  • Cleaning takes a new routine. Food can collect under the bridge. A water flosser, special floss and regular professional cleanings become part of life.
  • The teeth can wear or chip. Acrylic in particular wears and can chip or stain over the years, so repairs or a remake may be needed down the line.
  • Implants can have problems. Most implants heal into the bone well, but some don't, and gum infection around implants (peri-implantitis) can develop, especially with smoking, uncontrolled diabetes or skipped cleanings.
  • Speech takes adjusting. Some people notice a slight lisp or extra saliva for the first days or weeks.
  • It isn't reversible. Once teeth are removed and implants placed, there is no going back, which is why we never rush the decision.

Care and how long it lasts

With good home care and regular checkups, dental implants often last many years, and for many people decades. That depends on your health, your bite, whether you smoke and how well the area is kept clean. Nobody can honestly promise you a lifespan.

The bridge on top usually needs attention sooner than the implants underneath. Acrylic teeth commonly need repair or replacement after several years, while zirconia tends to hold up longer, though it can still chip. If you grind your teeth, a night guard helps protect the bridge. Daily care looks like this:

  • Brush the bridge twice a day, especially where it meets the gum
  • Clean under the bridge every day with a water flosser or a floss threader
  • Keep regular professional cleanings, often every three to six months for full-arch patients
  • Tell us early if anything feels loose, clicks, smells or bleeds

Acrylic or zirconia teeth

The final bridge is usually made from one of two materials. Acrylic (often PMMA, sometimes over a metal frame) is lighter, costs less and is easier to repair or adjust. It can wear, stain and chip over time. Zirconia is a very strong ceramic that resists staining and wear and tends to keep a natural look for longer. It costs more, and if it does chip, a repair can be harder.

The teeth you wear while healing are usually acrylic. For the final set, we help you choose based on your bite, whether you grind, how you want the teeth to look and your budget. Both are made in our in-house lab, and the price of each is in writing before you decide.

Questions, answered

What is All-on-X?

All-on-X is a fixed full arch of teeth attached to four, five, six or more dental implants in one jaw. The X is the number of implants, which is chosen from your scans, your bone and your bite. The bridge stays in at night and only your dentist removes it.

Is All-on-X better than All-on-4?

Not automatically. All-on-4 uses four implants, and for many people with good bone that is enough. All-on-X can add implants when bone is softer, the bite is heavy or the arch needs more support. The right number depends on your images, not on which option sounds bigger.

How long does All-on-X last?

The implants often last many years, and for many people decades, with good cleaning and regular checkups. The bridge on top usually needs repair or replacement sooner, especially acrylic. Smoking, grinding, uncontrolled diabetes and skipped cleanings can shorten that.

Does insurance cover All-on-X?

All-on-4 vs All-on-X vs dentures: which is right for me?

Dentures cost the least and need no surgery, but they come out daily and the jaw tends to keep shrinking under them. All-on-4 and All-on-X both give you fixed teeth on implants; the difference is how many implants your jaw needs. Your exam, scans and goals decide which fits, and we will tell you plainly if a denture or overdenture makes more sense.

How many implants do I need for a full arch?

Usually four to six per arch, and sometimes more. Upper arches and softer bone often need more implants than a lower arch with dense bone. Dr. Rana sets the number after measuring your bone on a CBCT and explains the reason for it.

Can I get fixed teeth if I was told I don't have enough bone?

Often, yes. Tilted implants, extra implants or a bone graft can make fixed teeth possible for many people with bone loss. If your case needs major grafting or sinus work, we refer you to an oral surgeon and tell you why.

Can you get dental implants for your whole mouth at once?

Often, yes. Many people have both arches treated in one visit under sedation and wear fixed healing teeth while the implants heal. Whether that is right for you depends on your bone, your health and how many teeth need to come out, which your exam and scans show.

What are zirconia teeth?

Zirconia teeth are a full-arch bridge milled from a strong, tooth-colored ceramic and screwed onto implants. They resist stains and wear better than acrylic but cost more. See our zirconia teeth page for a full comparison.
