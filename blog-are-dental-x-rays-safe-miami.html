Are dental X-rays safe?
Yes, when they are taken for a reason.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Are dental X-rays safe? For most people, yes: the radiation from dental imaging is low, and dentists are trained to use as little as they can while still getting a useful picture. The real question is whether each image is needed, and that depends on your own risk, not a calendar. This guide explains the main types, how often they tend to be taken, and what changes if you are pregnant or asking for your child.

Short answer: dental X-rays use a small amount of radiation, and dentists follow an "as low as reasonably achievable" approach. How often you need them depends on your cavity and gum history. If you are pregnant, tell the dentist: routine images are often postponed, while X-rays needed for a dental problem can be taken with appropriate precautions.

Why the dose from dental X-rays is low

Dental offices work by a principle called ALARA, short for "as low as reasonably achievable." In plain terms: don't take an image unless it will change a decision, and when you do take one, use the smallest exposure that still shows what the dentist needs to see.

Modern equipment helps with that. Digital sensors need less exposure than old film did, the beam is narrowed to the area being imaged, and each exposure lasts a fraction of a second. Small, targeted images of a few teeth involve far less than a medical CT of the head.

Protective measures are used as appropriate for each patient. Professional guidance on shielding has shifted over the years, with more emphasis on a tightly focused beam and fast digital sensors, so practices vary in when they use a lead apron or collar. If a shield would make you more comfortable, just ask.

The main types of dental X-rays and what each one is for

Not every X-ray answers the same question. Knowing which one is being taken, and why, makes it easier to ask good questions.

How often you need them depends on you

There is no single schedule that suits everyone. Following ADA guidance, a dentist sets the interval from your history and what the exam shows. Two adults the same age can have very different needs:

  • Often fewer images: no new cavities in recent years, healthy gums, few fillings, good home care.
  • Often more frequent images: new cavities at recent visits, active gum disease, a dry mouth from medication, lots of crowns or large fillings, or ongoing treatment such as a root canal or an implant.
  • Images taken because of a symptom: pain, swelling, a cracked tooth or an injury, whatever the date of your last set.

One simple way to avoid repeat exposure: if you are changing dentists, ask your previous office to send your most recent X-rays. If they are recent and clear enough, a new dentist can often use them instead of retaking everything. That is also a good habit for anyone moving to Miami from another city or country.

Dental X-rays during pregnancy

Tell the dentist if you are pregnant or might be, even early on. Routine check-up images are often postponed until after the baby arrives, simply because they can wait.

Necessary X-rays are a different matter. If you have a toothache, swelling or an infection, an image may be needed to diagnose it, and dental X-rays can be taken during pregnancy with appropriate precautions. Leaving a dental infection untreated is generally considered a bigger concern than a small, targeted image of the mouth. Your dentist can also talk with your obstetric provider if you or they have questions.

Pregnancy is also a good time for a cleaning and an exam, since hormone changes can make gums puffy and prone to bleeding.

X-rays for children

Children are more sensitive to radiation than adults, so dentists are especially selective with them. Many young children with healthy teeth and spaces between their baby teeth need few or no X-rays at first. Once the back baby teeth touch, bitewings become useful, because decay can spread quickly in thin baby-tooth enamel.

Other reasons to image a child include an injury to a front tooth, a baby tooth that won't loosen, or checking that adult teeth are developing where they should. Child-sized sensors are used. If your child has had X-rays elsewhere recently, bring the details. Our kids' dentist page explains how visits for younger patients work.

Questions you can always ask, and how we handle X-rays

You are entitled to understand any image before it is taken. Reasonable questions include: which type is this, what are you looking for, and could my old X-rays answer that instead? A good dentist will answer without hesitation.

Dental X-ray questions

How often should you get dental X-rays?

It depends on your risk. Adults with healthy teeth and gums and no recent cavities usually need them less often, while people with frequent cavities, gum disease or ongoing treatment may need them more often. Your dentist should explain the interval they suggest and why.

Can I refuse dental X-rays?

Yes, you can decline. Your dentist should explain what they may miss without the images, such as decay between teeth or an infection at a root tip, and may ask you to sign a note that you declined. Some treatments, like a root canal or an implant, can't be done safely without imaging.

Are dental X-rays safe during pregnancy?

X-rays needed to diagnose a dental problem can be taken during pregnancy with appropriate precautions. Routine images are often postponed until after delivery. Always tell your dentist if you are pregnant or might be.

Why does my new dentist want new X-rays?

A new dentist needs a current picture to diagnose accurately, and older images may be out of date or unclear. Ask your previous office to send your most recent X-rays. If they are recent and of good quality, they can often be used instead of retaking them.

Is a 3D intraoral scan the same as an X-ray?

No. An intraoral scan uses light to record the surface of your teeth and gums, so it involves no radiation. It can't see inside teeth or bone, which is why X-rays are still needed for some diagnoses.
