Bad breath that won't go away?
Find the source, then fix it.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Bad breath that won't go away almost always has a source you can find, and most of the time it is in the mouth: a coated tongue, inflamed gums, or food trapped somewhere a toothbrush can't reach. Less often it comes from the tonsils, the sinuses, the stomach or a medical condition. Mouthwash covers the smell for an hour or so but doesn't remove the cause. Here is how to narrow it down and what actually helps.

Short answer: persistent bad breath (halitosis) usually comes from bacteria on the back of the tongue, gum disease, food traps or a dry mouth. Cleaning your tongue, cleaning between your teeth every day and drinking enough water fix many cases. If your mouth checks out healthy and the smell continues, see your physician about tonsils, sinuses, reflux or other medical causes.

First, check whether it is really there

You can't reliably smell your own breath, because your nose gets used to it. Breathing into your cupped hands doesn't work well either. Two simple checks give a better idea:

  • Lick the inside of your clean wrist, let it dry for a few seconds and smell it. That gives a rough sense of the front of your tongue.
  • Gently wipe the back of your tongue with a piece of gauze or a spoon and smell it. A yellowish or whitish film with a strong odor points to the tongue as a source.
  • Ask someone you trust. It is awkward, but an honest answer from a partner or family member beats guessing.

Morning breath that fades after breakfast and brushing is normal: saliva flow drops during sleep and bacteria multiply. The kind worth tracking down is the smell that hangs on through the day.

The usual causes, and the clues for each

Most bad breath comes from sulfur compounds that bacteria release as they break down food debris, dead cells and proteins. Where those bacteria are living is what you need to figure out.

Dental work can be part of the story too. Partial dentures and full dentures collect a film that smells if they aren't cleaned and soaked as directed. The space under a bridge and the edge of an old crown are classic food traps.

Diet, habits and Miami heat

Garlic and onions are the famous ones, and they work differently from everything above. Their odor compounds are absorbed into the bloodstream and breathed out through the lungs, which is why brushing doesn't help much until your body clears them.

Coffee, including a few cafecitos a day, and alcohol tend to dry the mouth and leave a sour note behind. Low-carb diets and long fasts can produce a distinct fruity or acetone smell as the body burns fat. Smoking and vaping add their own odor and also dry the mouth and harm the gums.

Then there is the weather. A long day at the beach, a run along the bay or an afternoon working outside in Miami humidity can leave you more dehydrated than you realize. Less saliva means more bacteria and stronger breath by evening. Keeping a water bottle with you is a simple, real fix.

What actually helps, step by step

  • Clean your tongue once a day. Use a tongue scraper or your toothbrush. Start as far back as is comfortable and pull forward gently, a few strokes, rinsing in between. Pressure doesn't help; consistency does.
  • Clean between your teeth daily. Floss, a water flosser or small interdental brushes all remove the debris that feeds odor-producing bacteria.
  • Brush twice a day for two minutes with a fluoride toothpaste, paying attention to the gumline.
  • Drink water through the day, especially in the heat and after coffee or alcohol. Sugar-free gum can help keep saliva flowing between meals.
  • Clean dental appliances properly. Dentures, retainers, aligners and night guards need their own daily cleaning.
  • Keep up with cleanings. Tartar under the gumline harbors bacteria that no home routine can reach.

Give the routine about two weeks. If the smell is clearly better, you have found your main cause. If nothing changes, it is time for a dental exam.

Why mouthwash alone doesn't solve it

Most cosmetic mouthwashes add a strong flavor that hides the odor for a short time. The bacteria and the debris they feed on are still there, so the smell comes back, often within an hour or two. Rinses that contain alcohol can dry the mouth, which can make the problem worse later in the day.

Some antibacterial rinses can lower the bacteria count and are a reasonable add-on, but they work only alongside tongue cleaning, flossing and treatment of any gum disease. If you find yourself reaching for mouthwash or mints many times a day, take that as a sign the source still needs finding.

When the dentist or your physician should look

Book a dental visit if the smell lasts beyond a couple of weeks of good home care, or if it comes with bleeding gums, a bad taste or a tooth that catches food. At Prana Dental, Dr. Rana checks the gums, looks for food traps around old fillings and crowns, and examines the tongue and tonsil area. Very often the answer is a professional cleaning plus treatment of early gum disease. If dryness seems to be a factor, our post on dry mouth explains common causes.

If your mouth is healthy and the smell persists, see your physician. Sinus problems, reflux, tonsil issues and some medical conditions can cause breath odor. In someone with diabetes, a sweet or fruity smell together with feeling unwell needs prompt medical attention. Don't stop or change a prescribed medication on your own because of breath or dry mouth; ask the doctor or pharmacist who manages it.

Bad breath questions

Why does my breath still smell after I brush my teeth?

Brushing cleans the teeth but often misses the back of the tongue and the spaces between teeth, where most odor-causing bacteria live. Gum disease and food traps under old dental work are other common reasons. Add tongue cleaning and daily flossing, and see a dentist if the smell continues.

Can bad breath come from the stomach?

Occasionally. Reflux can cause a sour taste and odor, but most persistent bad breath starts in the mouth. If a dentist finds your teeth, gums and tongue healthy, talk to your physician about reflux and other medical causes.

Can tonsil stones cause bad breath?

Yes. Tonsil stones are small, whitish clumps of debris and bacteria that collect in the folds of the tonsils, and they can smell strong. Gargling can help loosen them. Avoid poking at them with sharp objects, and see a physician if they keep coming back or your throat hurts.

Does mouthwash get rid of bad breath?

Mostly it masks it for a short time. Some antibacterial rinses help as an add-on, but the smell returns unless the source, such as a coated tongue or gum disease, is dealt with. Alcohol-based rinses can also dry your mouth.

Can a dentist tell what is causing my bad breath?

Often, yes. An exam can show gum inflammation, deep pockets, tartar, food traps around fillings or crowns, and tongue coating. If the mouth looks healthy, the dentist can tell you that it is worth checking other causes with your physician.
