cavities between teeth: why x-rays catch what you can't see

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Cavities that form between teeth are hidden from a mirror, a flashlight, and even a dentist's naked eye in many cases, because the contact point where two teeth touch blocks the view entirely. Dental X-rays see straight through that contact point, which is why they're a standard part of routine exams even when nothing hurts.

It's one of the more frustrating things patients hear: "you have a cavity" with zero warning it was ever coming at all. Cavities between teeth are notorious for this exact experience, and it comes down to simple geometry, not bad luck or careless brushing on anyone's part. Here's why X-rays matter so much specifically for this spot, what tends to happen if that checkpoint gets skipped for too long, and what to actually expect once one is finally found and addressed properly.

Why the space between teeth is a blind spot

Where two teeth touch, called the contact point, is tight, curved, and genuinely impossible to see directly even with a mirror and good lighting pointed right at it from several angles. Floss can physically reach it, which is exactly why flossing matters so much even when brushing already feels thorough, but by the time decay there becomes visible from the front or actually starts causing pain, it's often already progressed considerably further than a cavity you might have caught early on a front tooth's clearly visible surface. This is genuinely one of the most common places cavities start in adults with otherwise solid brushing habits, precisely because the geometry works against you rather than any lapse in effort.

How an X-ray sees what your eyes can't

A dental X-ray passes straight through the tooth and shows up differences in density on the resulting image, and a cavity is simply less dense than the healthy enamel surrounding it on either side. That density contrast shows up clearly on an X-ray as a small dark shadow right between two teeth, even at a stage far too early to be visible to the eye, felt with a dental tool, or noticed by you at home no matter how carefully and regularly you check things yourself. The image essentially lets Dr. Rana see one step ahead of where symptoms would eventually show up, which is the entire point of taking it before anything hurts.

Why these cavities often cause no symptoms early on

Enamel itself has no nerve endings at all, so decay sitting in that outer enamel layer between two teeth typically causes zero sensitivity or pain of any kind, even as it slowly spreads across the surface. It's only once decay reaches the dentin layer underneath the enamel, or gets closer to the nerve itself, that most people finally start feeling anything at all — which by that point is well past the stage where a small, simple, inexpensive filling would have handled the entire problem easily and quickly without any lasting consequence.

How often X-rays are actually needed

This varies quite a bit from patient to patient and by individual cavity history, and Dr. Rana will tell you what interval genuinely makes sense for your own mouth rather than applying some blanket rule to every single patient regardless of their history. Patients with a documented history of cavities between teeth typically benefit from noticeably more frequent checks than someone with a longer track record of a clean bill of dental health at every single visit over the years. Age, diet, and how tightly your teeth are naturally spaced all factor into that individual recommendation too, along with how your last few visits actually went.

What happens if one is caught early vs. late

A small cavity between teeth caught early on an X-ray usually just means a straightforward tooth-colored filling starting from $199, done in a single short visit with minimal fuss involved. Left unchecked for a long stretch of time instead, it can progress to needing a larger filling, a full crown, or in more advanced cases even a root canal — all of which are considerably more involved, more time-consuming, and more expensive than simply catching it at the small-shadow-on-an-X-ray stage would have been from the very start of the process. The math genuinely favors catching it early almost every single time.
