Zirconia vs porcelain vs metal crowns,
compared in plain words.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Choosing between zirconia vs porcelain vs metal crowns comes down to three questions: how much force the tooth takes, how much it shows when you smile, and how much natural tooth can be saved. Each material wins on one of those and gives a little on another. Below we compare the four materials dentists use most often, with a side-by-side table and the situations where each one tends to make sense.

Short answer: zirconia is the strongest tooth-colored option and suits back teeth and grinders. Lithium disilicate ceramic usually looks the most natural and suits front teeth and premolars. Porcelain fused to metal has a long track record but can show a grey edge over time. Gold alloy needs the least tooth removed and is very durable, but it looks like metal.

The four materials in plain words

Zirconia

A very tough white ceramic. Solid (monolithic) zirconia is milled from one block and resists chipping and cracking well, which is why it is popular for molars. It can look quite natural, though it tends to be a little more opaque than glass ceramics. Some zirconia crowns get a thin layer of porcelain on the front for extra lifelike color, which looks lovely but brings back a small risk of chipping in that layer.

Lithium disilicate ceramic

A glass ceramic that lets light pass through it much like enamel does, so it blends in beautifully next to natural front teeth. It is bonded to the tooth, which adds strength to the whole unit. It is strong enough for most front teeth and premolars and some molars, but on the back teeth of a heavy grinder, zirconia or gold is usually the safer pick.

Porcelain fused to metal

A metal shell underneath with porcelain baked over it. It has been used for decades and works well, especially for bridges. The tradeoffs: it needs extra room for both layers, the porcelain can chip off the metal, and if the gum recedes, a dark metal edge can show at the gumline. Many older crowns you see in people's mouths are this type.

Gold alloy

Gold mixed with other metals for strength. It rarely breaks, it is gentle on the opposing teeth, and it can be made very thin, so less of your tooth has to be shaved down. The obvious downside is the color, so it is usually reserved for back molars that do not show. The metal content also makes its cost move with metal prices.

Front teeth, back teeth and grinders

Here is how the choice often plays out in real situations:

  • One front tooth next to natural teeth. This is the hardest match in dentistry. Lithium disilicate or layered zirconia usually gives the most lifelike result, and shade matching in good light matters more than the material name.
  • A front tooth that has turned dark (often after a root canal). A very translucent ceramic can let that darkness show through, so the dentist may choose a more opaque material or mask the color underneath first.
  • A lower molar on someone who clenches. Solid zirconia or gold alloy is usually the safer choice, ideally with a night guard.
  • A short tooth with little room to spare. Materials that work in thin sections, such as gold alloy or solid zirconia, can save tooth structure.
  • Replacing an old crown with a grey line at the gum. A metal-free crown avoids that edge if the gum recedes again.
  • A bridge. Zirconia and porcelain fused to metal are both common, because the connectors between teeth need strength. Our dental bridges page explains how bridges are planned.

If grinding is part of your story, protecting the new crown matters as much as choosing it. Our page on custom night guards explains how they are made.

Why the amount of tooth removed matters

Every crown needs room, so the tooth is shaped down to make space for the material. The thinner a material can safely be, the more natural tooth you keep. That matters because healthy tooth structure cannot grow back, and the more that is removed, the closer the work gets to the nerve.

Porcelain fused to metal typically needs the most room, because two layers have to fit. Gold alloy needs the least. Zirconia and lithium disilicate sit in between, and the exact amount depends on the tooth and how much strength that spot requires. When a tooth only has damage on part of its surface, a partial crown (onlay) may save even more tooth, which is worth asking about.

Crown materials: common questions

Should I get a zirconia or a porcelain crown?

It depends on the tooth. Zirconia is stronger and usually preferred for molars and people who grind, while glass ceramics such as lithium disilicate tend to look more natural on front teeth. Many people end up with different materials in different parts of the mouth.

Do zirconia crowns look fake?

Modern zirconia can look very natural, especially on back teeth and in layered form. Next to natural front teeth it can look a little more opaque than glass ceramic. Good shade matching and shaping make a bigger difference than the material alone.

Why do some old crowns have a black line at the gum?

That line is usually the metal edge of a porcelain fused to metal crown showing after the gum has receded. It is not always a health problem, but it can be a cosmetic concern, and it is worth checking for decay at that edge. A metal-free crown avoids it.

Are gold crowns still used?

Yes. Gold alloy crowns are very durable, need the least tooth removed and are gentle on the opposing teeth, so they remain a solid choice for back molars, especially for heavy grinders who don't mind the look.

Which crown material lasts the longest?

Gold alloy and solid zirconia are the most resistant to breaking, but how long any crown lasts depends more on the tooth underneath, your gums, grinding habits and cleaning at the crown edge than on material alone.
