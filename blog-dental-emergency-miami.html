If you're in pain or you've just damaged a tooth, you need clear steps, not a long article. This guide covers what actually counts as a dental emergency in Miami, exactly what to do in the first few minutes, and when to go to the dentist versus the ER.

What actually counts as a dental emergency?

Not every dental problem needs same-day care, but several do. These generally count as emergencies that shouldn't wait:

  • A tooth that's been knocked out completely
  • A tooth that's cracked, chipped, or broken with pain or a sharp edge
  • Severe, persistent toothache, especially if it's throbbing or keeping you up
  • A filling or crown that's fallen out and is causing pain or sensitivity
  • Facial or gum swelling, especially if it's getting worse
  • Bleeding from the mouth that won't stop with gentle pressure
  • A dental injury from a fall, sports, or an accident

What do you do right now for a knocked-out tooth?

Time matters here more than almost any other dental emergency. Pick the tooth up by the crown (the chewing surface), not the root, and avoid scrubbing or scraping it. If it's clean, try gently placing it back in the socket and biting down softly on gauze to hold it in place; if that's not possible, keep it moist in a cup of milk or your own saliva — never let it dry out. Then get to a dentist as quickly as you can; the sooner it's addressed, the better the chances of saving the tooth.

What do you do for a severe toothache?

Rinse your mouth gently with warm water, and use dental floss carefully to check if something is caught between teeth that might be causing pain. A cold compress on the outside of your cheek can help with swelling and discomfort. Over-the-counter pain relief can help in the short term if you don't have a medical reason to avoid it, but a lasting toothache — especially one that throbs or wakes you up — needs a dentist to find the actual cause, not just manage the pain.

What do you do for a broken or chipped tooth?

Rinse your mouth with warm water and, if you can find the broken piece, save it and bring it with you. Apply a cold compress to the outside of your face near the area to reduce swelling, and if there's a sharp edge, dental wax or even sugar-free gum can protect your tongue and cheek until you're seen. Avoid chewing on that side of your mouth in the meantime. Depending on how much of the tooth is affected, treatment afterward can range from a simple repair to a crown — your dentist will confirm the right fix once they can actually see it.

When is it an ER visit instead of a dentist visit?

This is the most important distinction to get right. If you have facial swelling accompanied by a fever, or any trouble breathing or swallowing, go to the emergency room or call 911 immediately — that combination can signal a spreading infection that needs to be treated as a medical emergency, not something a dental office can manage safely. Uncontrolled bleeding after a significant injury is another reason to go straight to the ER. For pain, a broken tooth, a lost filling, or a knocked-out tooth without those warning signs, a same-day dental visit is the right call.

How can you avoid a dental emergency in the first place?

Most emergencies are either accidents or problems that built up quietly. Regular checkups catch small issues, like a weakening filling or an early cavity, before they turn into a Saturday-night emergency. If you play contact sports, a mouthguard is one of the simplest ways to prevent a knocked-out or broken tooth. And if something feels off — a twinge, a sensitivity, a small chip — it's worth getting it looked at through general dentistry before it becomes urgent, or booking a free consult if you're not yet an established patient.

Questions, answered

What should I do if my tooth gets knocked out?

Pick it up by the crown, not the root, and avoid scrubbing it. Try to place it back in the socket, or keep it in milk or your own saliva if that's not possible, and get to a dentist right away — acting quickly gives the best chance of saving the tooth.

Is a toothache a dental emergency?

A severe, persistent, or throbbing toothache is worth same-day attention, since it usually means something needs treatment rather than just pain relief. Rinse gently and use a cold compress in the meantime, and see a dentist as soon as you can.

When should I go to the ER instead of a dentist for a dental issue?

Go to the ER or call 911 if you have facial swelling with a fever, or any trouble breathing or swallowing. That combination can point to a spreading infection that needs emergency medical care, not a dental office visit.

What if my crown or filling falls out?
