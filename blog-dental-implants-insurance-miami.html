Does insurance cover dental implants in Florida?

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Most dental insurance plans cover diagnostics (exam, X-rays) and sometimes part of the crown, but rarely the implant itself, which many plans still classify as a major or excluded procedure. Prana accepts most major insurance and offers 0% financing from $167/month to cover whatever insurance doesn't.

This is one of the first questions almost every implant patient asks, and the honest answer is more nuanced than a flat yes or no. Here's what's usually covered, what isn't, and how to find out exactly where your plan lands before you commit to treatment.

Why implants are often excluded or limited

Many dental insurance plans were written before implants became a standard of care, and some still classify implant placement as a major or even elective procedure rather than a medically necessary tooth replacement. That's a plan design choice, not a reflection of whether you actually need the treatment.

What's commonly covered

Diagnostics like your exam, X-rays and sometimes a 3D scan are more often covered or discounted than the implant itself. Some plans cover part of the crown that attaches to the implant, even when they exclude the implant post. The two are frequently coded and covered separately, which is where a lot of patient confusion comes from.

A note on PPO vs. HMO dental plans

PPO plans generally give you more flexibility to use any dentist and get partial reimbursement, even for procedures with limited coverage. HMO/DHMO dental plans are more restrictive about providers and often cover even less of major work like implants. If you're choosing a plan specifically with implants in mind, this distinction matters more than the monthly premium.

The short answer for Florida

Sometimes, and usually only in part. Whether a dental plan pays toward implants in Florida depends far more on that plan's own rules than on the state you live in, so two neighbors in Miami with different employers can get very different answers. Below is how the main types of coverage tend to work, and how to get the most out of the one you have.

Dental PPO vs dental HMO: how each treats implants

Dental PPO

A PPO has a network of dentists who agree to set fees, and it usually lets you see dentists outside that network as well, though it may pay less when you do. Services are sorted into tiers such as preventive, basic and major. When implants are covered at all, they normally sit in the major tier, which pays a smaller share than exams and cleanings. The list of exclusions in your plan booklet is where implants most often turn up.

Dental HMO (DHMO)

A DHMO usually assigns you to one primary dental office and pays according to a fixed copay schedule. You generally need to stay with that office, and care elsewhere may not be covered. Some DHMO schedules list implant services and many do not. Where they do, the copay normally applies only at the assigned office.

Annual maximums: the number that matters most

Your annual maximum is the most your plan will pay in one benefit year. For many dental plans it is often in the $1,000 to $2,000 range. Once the plan has paid that amount, you cover the rest until the next benefit year starts, which is why even a plan that includes implants may pay for only part of the treatment.

Cleanings, fillings and any other work earlier in the year draw from that same pool. If an implant is on your list, it helps to know how much of this year's maximum is still unused before you pick a date.

Waiting periods and missing-tooth clauses

Many plans make you wait a set time after enrolling before they pay for major services. The length is written into the plan and can be long, so if you bought coverage recently, check when major benefits begin.

A missing-tooth clause says the plan won't pay to replace a tooth that was already gone before your coverage started. If you lost the tooth years ago and enrolled last month, the plan may decline the implant even when implants are otherwise a covered benefit. Not every plan has this clause, so ask about it by name.

Medicare and Medicare Advantage

Original Medicare (Part A and Part B) generally doesn't cover dental implants. Some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits, but what those benefits include varies widely from one plan to the next, and some come with their own yearly limits and network rules. If you have a Medicare Advantage plan, your plan documents or member services line are the place to confirm whether implants are included.

Florida Medicaid for adults

Florida Medicaid dental coverage for adults is limited, and it generally doesn't include dental implants. Adults on Medicaid may still have some dental services through their plan, so it is worth asking what yours offers, but implants are usually not on the list. Coverage for children works differently and is a separate topic.

Medical insurance: only in rare cases

Medical insurance usually treats implants as dental care and doesn't pay for them. In uncommon situations, such as teeth lost in an accident or as part of treatment for another medical condition, a medical plan might consider part of the cost. It isn't something to count on: it depends entirely on the plan, and it typically takes paperwork from your medical providers. If you think your situation could qualify, ask your medical insurer before treatment starts.

Using benefits across two calendar years

Implant treatment usually happens in stages. The implant is placed, the bone heals around it for a few months, and then the crown or final arch goes on. That timeline can work in your favor: if your plan covers part of implant treatment and your benefit year resets on January 1, starting in the fall can mean one stage draws on this year's maximum and the next stage on next year's.

A few cautions apply. Not every plan runs on the calendar year, so confirm when yours resets. The schedule should follow what is right for healing, not the other way around. And splitting the work only helps if both stages are covered services under your plan.

HSA and FSA funds

If you have a health savings account (HSA) or a flexible spending account (FSA), dental treatment such as implants is often an eligible expense. The exact rules come from your account and its administrator, and FSA money often has to be used within the plan year, so check your deadline. Paying with pre-tax dollars can stretch your budget, and those funds can be combined with insurance and financing.

Questions, answered

Does Medicare cover dental implants in Florida?

Original Medicare generally doesn't cover dental implants. Some Medicare Advantage plans in Florida include dental benefits, and a few may help with part of implant treatment, but it varies widely by plan. Check your plan documents, or bring your card and we check your benefits for you.

Does Florida Medicaid cover dental implants for adults?

Generally, no. Florida Medicaid dental coverage for adults is limited and usually doesn't include implants. If you have Medicaid, ask your plan which dental services it does include. Many people look at financing for the cost of implants instead.

Is there a waiting period for dental implants with insurance?

Often, yes. Many dental plans have a waiting period before they pay for major services, and implants usually fall into that group when they are covered at all. The length is set by your plan, and we check your benefits for you so you know where you stand.

Can I use my HSA or FSA for dental implants?

In many cases, yes. Dental implants are often an eligible expense for HSA and FSA accounts, but the rules come from your account and its administrator. FSA funds often have a use-by date, so check yours. You can combine those funds with insurance and financing.

What is a missing tooth clause in dental insurance?

It is a rule in some dental plans that they won't pay to replace a tooth that was already missing before your coverage began. If your plan has one, it may decline an implant for that tooth even when implants are otherwise covered. Not every plan includes it, so ask, or let us do it: we check your benefits for you.
