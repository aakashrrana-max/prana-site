Can smokers get dental implants?
Often yes, with honest odds and a plan.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Smokers can often get dental implants, but smoking raises the chance that something goes wrong, both while the implant heals and in the years after. That is not a lecture; it is the single most useful thing to know before you decide. This guide explains why tobacco and nicotine matter, where vaping fits in, and what you can do to tilt the odds back in your favor.

Short answer: smoking does not automatically rule out implants, but it is a well-known risk factor for slower healing, implants that do not bond to the bone, and infection around implants later. Vaping is not risk-free either. Your dentist will advise on stopping before and after treatment; any reduction helps, and quitting helps most.

What smoking does to a healing implant

An implant succeeds when bone grows snugly against it over a few months. That process needs good blood flow, oxygen and a calm immune response at the site. Tobacco works against all three:

  • Nicotine narrows blood vessels. Less blood reaches the gum and bone, so healing is slower and the tissues are less able to fight bacteria.
  • Smoke brings heat and chemicals. Carbon monoxide reduces the oxygen the blood can carry, and hot smoke irritates the wound directly.
  • The suction of drawing on a cigarette can disturb a fresh blood clot after an extraction or implant placement.
  • It hides warning signs. Smokers' gums often bleed less even when they are inflamed, so gum disease and implant infections can go unnoticed for longer.

The result is a higher risk of two problems. Early on, an implant may fail to integrate and need to be removed. Later, smokers are more prone to peri-implantitis, an infection that destroys bone around the implant. Our post on implant failure signs lists what to watch for.

Is vaping safer for implants?

Vaping removes the burning tobacco, but it is not risk-free for implants. Most vapes still deliver nicotine, which constricts blood vessels just as it does from a cigarette. The aerosol can dry the mouth, and a dry mouth makes it harder to keep plaque under control around teeth and implants. Research on vaping and implants is newer and still developing, so nobody can honestly tell you it is safe.

The same caution applies to cigars, hookah, chewing tobacco, nicotine pouches and smoked cannabis. If you use any of them, say so at your consultation. Your dentist is not there to judge; the answer changes how the treatment is planned and timed.

Quitting or cutting down: what helps and when

Your dentist will advise on how long to stop before the procedure and how long to stay off cigarettes or vapes afterward, based on your case. In general, the longer the break around surgery and the early healing weeks, the better the conditions for the bone.

A few practical points patients often find helpful:

  • Set your quit or cut-down date before the treatment date, not the night before.
  • Talk with your physician or pharmacist about nicotine replacement or other quit aids, and tell your dentist what you plan to use.
  • Plan for the first days after surgery: the urge often hits hardest when you are home, bored and eating soft food.
  • Keep your hands busy and your mouth moist: water, sugar-free gum only if your dentist says chewing is fine, and a cool drink instead of a cafecito break that usually comes with a cigarette.
  • If you slip, tell your dentist. It is better to watch the site closely than to hide it.

Even if you do not quit for good, a break around treatment and a lower daily amount afterward can make a real difference to how the gums behave.

How the plan may change for a smoker

Smoking rarely changes whether an implant is possible as much as how it is approached. Depending on your health and bone, a dentist may suggest:

  • Treating any gum disease first, since smokers often have more of it than their gums show
  • A staged approach, letting an extraction site or graft heal before the implant goes in
  • Holding off on attaching a temporary tooth to the implant on the same day
  • More frequent maintenance cleanings afterward, with the gum around the implant measured each time
  • A frank conversation about alternatives, such as a bridge or a denture, if the risk looks too high

Heavy smoking combined with other risk factors, such as uncontrolled diabetes or very thin bone, can tip the balance toward waiting. That is a discussion, not a refusal.

Smoking, vaping and implants: common questions

Will a dentist do implants if you smoke?

Many will, after explaining the higher risks and planning around them. Some cases with heavy smoking plus other risk factors may be advised to wait. An open conversation about how much you smoke helps the dentist give you an honest answer.

How long should I stop smoking before a dental implant?

Your dentist will advise a time frame based on your case, both before and after the procedure. In general, a longer break around surgery and early healing gives the bone better conditions. Ask your physician about quit aids if you need support.

Can I vape after a dental implant?

Try not to vape while the site heals, and your dentist will tell you how long. Nicotine still reduces blood flow, and drawing on a vape can disturb the healing area. Vaping is not considered risk-free for implants.

Do smokers' implants fail more often?

Smoking is a recognized risk factor for both early implant failure and infection around implants later. That does not mean a smoker's implant will fail. Quitting or cutting down, good cleaning and regular checkups lower the risk.

Can I use nicotine patches after an implant?

Patches avoid smoke and heat but still contain nicotine, which affects blood flow. Some people use them to get through healing without smoking. Discuss it with your physician and your dentist before you start.
