Dental implants with gum disease:
treat the gums first, then build.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

You can often get dental implants with gum disease in your history, but not while the disease is active. The same bacteria that destroy bone around natural teeth can attack the bone around an implant, so the gums need to be under control before anything is placed. That usually means a deep cleaning, a re-check, and a maintenance plan you can keep up for life.

Short answer: active gum disease is a reason to wait, not a permanent no. Once the infection is controlled with scaling and root planing (and, for advanced cases, care from a periodontist), many people go on to have implants. Because a past of gum disease raises the risk of infection around implants later, regular maintenance visits matter more than usual.

Why active gum disease and implants do not mix

Gum disease (periodontitis) is a chronic infection under the gum line. Bacteria live in pockets between the gum and the root, the body reacts with inflammation, and over time the bone that holds the teeth wears away. An implant depends on that same bone.

If an implant goes into a mouth where the infection is still active, the bacteria from nearby pockets can settle around it. The result can be inflammation around the implant and, in time, bone loss there too. Treating the gums first lowers that risk and gives the implant a healthier neighborhood to heal in.

Not sure whether you have early or advanced disease? Our post on gingivitis vs periodontitis explains the difference and the warning signs.

The usual order of treatment

Every plan is personal, but most follow a sequence like this:

  • Measure. The gum pockets around each tooth are measured, bleeding spots are noted, and X-rays and a 3D scan show how much bone remains.
  • Remove what cannot be saved. Teeth with too little bone left may be taken out, sometimes with a graft placed in the socket to protect the ridge.
  • Deep clean. Scaling and root planing removes hardened deposits and bacteria from the root surfaces below the gum line, usually one section of the mouth at a time under local anesthetic. Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS performs this deep cleaning in our office.
  • Re-check. After several weeks of healing, the pockets are measured again. Shallower pockets and less bleeding mean the gums are responding.
  • Refer when needed. Deep pockets that do not respond, significant bone defects or gum surgery are referred to a periodontist, the dentist who focuses on gums and the bone around teeth.
  • Then plan the implant. Once the gums are stable, the implant can be planned on fresh scans.

For more on the deep cleaning itself, see deep cleaning vs regular cleaning and our gum disease treatment page.

Lost teeth to gum disease? Implants are still often possible

Many people considering implants lost their teeth to gum disease in the first place, and that does not shut the door. What it often means is less bone where the teeth used to be, which can call for grafting or a different implant layout. Our guide to bone grafting before implants explains when that is needed.

When most of the teeth in an arch are loose or failing, replacing them one by one rarely makes sense. A history of periodontitis is one of the recognized risk factors for problems around implants. There are two stages, and catching the first one early is the goal:

Smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, skipped maintenance and a cement or crown edge that traps plaque all add to the risk. For a full list of warning signs, read implant failure signs.

Protecting the implant for the long run

People with a past of gum disease usually do better with maintenance cleanings more often than the standard twice a year. Your dentist sets the interval from how your gums behave; many people with this history are seen every few months. At those visits the pockets around both teeth and implants are measured, so changes are found while they are small.

At home, the routine that protects natural teeth also protects implants: a soft brush angled at the gum line twice a day, plus floss, interdental brushes or a water flosser to clean where the implant crown meets the gum. If you smoke, cutting down or quitting helps both your gums and the implant; your physician can help with a plan.

Implants and gum disease: common questions

Can I get dental implants if I have periodontal disease?

Usually not while it is active. The infection is treated first, typically with scaling and root planing and a re-check. Once the gums are stable, many people can have implants, with closer maintenance afterward.

How long after a deep cleaning can I get an implant?

The gums are usually re-checked several weeks after a deep cleaning to see how they responded. If pockets have improved and bleeding has settled, implant planning can go ahead. Advanced cases may take longer or need care from a periodontist first.

Can implants get gum disease?

Implants cannot decay, but the gum and bone around them can become inflamed and infected, which is called peri-implantitis. It can cause bone loss and, if not treated, the implant can loosen. Daily cleaning and regular checkups are the main protection.

Are implants better than keeping teeth with gum disease?

Not automatically. A tooth with gum disease that responds well to treatment is often worth keeping. Implants make sense when a tooth has lost too much support to save, and the decision should be made tooth by tooth after an exam.

Does gum disease make implants fail?

A history of gum disease raises the risk of problems around implants, but it does not mean an implant will fail. Controlling the disease first, not smoking and keeping up maintenance visits lower the risk considerably for many people.
