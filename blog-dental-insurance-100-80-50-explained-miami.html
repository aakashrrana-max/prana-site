Dental insurance 100-80-50, explained
in plain English, tier by tier.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

In dental insurance, 100-80-50 is shorthand for how much the plan pays for three tiers of care: preventive, basic and major. Read it as 100 percent of preventive care, 80 percent of basic care and 50 percent of major care, after any deductible and only up to your annual maximum. The numbers are a starting point, though. Which tier a service falls into, and the rules around each, decide what you actually owe.

Short answer: 100-80-50 means the plan pays the full allowed fee for preventive care, most of it for basic care and about half for major care, and you pay the rest. Deductibles, frequency limits, waiting periods, the annual maximum and whether your dentist is in network all change the final number. Plans differ, so check yours.

What the three numbers mean

Each number is the plan's share of the allowed fee for a service, which is the amount the plan recognizes for that procedure. Your share, called coinsurance, is whatever is left. So in a 100-80-50 plan, you would typically owe nothing for a covered cleaning, a fifth of the allowed fee for a covered filling, and half of it for a covered crown, once any deductible has been met.

Some plans use other splits, such as 100-70-50 or 90-80-50, and some managed-care plans use fixed copays instead of percentages. The idea is the same: routine care is encouraged, larger work is shared more evenly.

Which services usually fall in which tier

Plans write their own lists, so the same procedure can sit in a different tier on two plans. This is the common pattern:

The fine print that changes the math

  • Deductible. A set amount you pay each benefit year before the plan shares costs on basic and major work. Many plans waive it for preventive care.
  • Annual maximum. The most the plan pays in a benefit year, often in the $1,000 to $2,000 range. Once it is used up, the tier percentages stop mattering until the plan resets.
  • Frequency limits. Rules such as two cleanings per benefit year, full-mouth X-rays every few years, or one crown on the same tooth only after a set number of years.
  • Waiting periods. New members may wait months before basic or major services are covered, while preventive care often starts right away.
  • Missing-tooth clause. Some plans will not pay to replace a tooth that was lost before coverage began.
  • Alternate benefit. Some plans pay only what a simpler option would cost, for example the fee for a silver filling on a back tooth even when you choose tooth-colored, and you pay the difference.

These rules explain most "but my plan says 80" surprises. A filling may be in the 80 tier, but if the deductible has not been met, the visit counts it first.

In network vs out of network

An in-network dentist has agreed to the plan's fee schedule. The percentages apply to that agreed fee, and the dentist does not bill you above it for covered services.

Out of network, the plan applies its percentage to its own allowed amount, which may be lower than the office's fee. Depending on the plan, you may owe the coinsurance plus the difference between the two, often called balance billing. Some plans, especially managed-care ones, pay nothing outside their network except in emergencies. Before your first visit anywhere, ask whether the office is in network with your specific plan, not just the insurance company.

Predeterminations: an estimate before you commit

For larger treatment, the office can send the plan a predetermination (also called a pre-treatment estimate). The plan replies with what it expects to pay, based on your benefits at that moment. It is not a promise of payment: if you use part of your maximum elsewhere, change plans or the treatment changes, the final payment can differ. It can also take a few weeks to come back, so ask early if timing matters.

After any claim, the plan sends an Explanation of Benefits (EOB). It is not a bill. It shows the allowed fee, what the plan paid, what went to the deductible and what remains your share. Comparing it with the office's statement is the easiest way to catch a mismatch.

Dental insurance tiers: common questions

What does 100-80-50 mean in dental insurance?

It describes how much the plan pays for preventive, basic and major care: the full allowed fee for preventive, most of it for basic and about half for major. You pay the remainder, after any deductible and up to the annual maximum.

Is a root canal basic or major on dental insurance?

It depends on the plan. Many plans list root canals as basic services, while others place them under major. Check your plan's benefit summary or ask the office to look it up before treatment.

Is a crown considered major dental work?

On many plans, yes: crowns, bridges and dentures are usually major services, paid at the lowest percentage and often subject to waiting periods and replacement limits. Some plans cover them differently, so confirm with yours.

Do I still pay a deductible for cleanings?

Often not. Many plans waive the deductible for preventive care such as exams and cleanings. Basic and major services usually require the deductible to be met first.

Does 100 percent coverage mean my cleaning is always covered?

Only within the plan's rules. If you go more often than the frequency limit, need a different type of cleaning, or see an out-of-network dentist, you may owe part of the fee. Check your limits before booking an extra visit.
