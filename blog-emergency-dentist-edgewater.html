What counts as a dental emergency

Situations that generally warrant an emergency visit include a knocked-out or badly loosened tooth, a broken tooth with pain or a sharp edge, a lost crown or filling causing pain or sensitivity, facial or gum swelling, or pain severe enough to disrupt sleep or daily function. Ongoing mild sensitivity or a chipped tooth with no pain can usually wait for a regular appointment, but when in doubt, it's better to call and describe the situation to someone who can actually assess it than to guess and wait it out at home hoping it resolves on its own, since some problems get harder and more expensive to treat the longer they're left alone.

What to do before you get to the office

For a knocked-out tooth, handle it by the crown (not the root, which is more fragile) and keep it moist — in milk or held gently in the cheek — and get to the dentist as quickly as possible, since timing matters a great deal for whether it can be saved. For a broken tooth, rinse with warm water and avoid chewing on that side until you're seen. For swelling or significant pain, a cold compress on the outside of the face can help temporarily. None of this replaces being seen — it's just what to do in the time between noticing the problem and getting to your appointment, so the situation doesn't get worse in the meantime.

What happens during an emergency visit

An emergency visit starts with an exam and an X-ray to see exactly what's going on — a cracked root, an infection, or a tooth that's more or less stable than it feels from the outside. From there, your dentist explains the options plainly, including what needs to happen immediately versus what can be scheduled shortly after, before any treatment begins, so you fully understand the plan and the reasoning behind it before agreeing to anything. In most cases, this same visit is also where any pain relief or urgent stabilization happens right away, even if the full course of treatment ends up being scheduled for a separate follow-up visit slightly later on.

When pain doesn't mean the worst-case scenario

Dental pain can feel alarming, but it doesn't always mean a tooth has to be pulled — sometimes it's a deep cavity that needs a filling, a cracked filling that needs replacing, or an infection that responds well to prompt treatment without anything more drastic. An accurate diagnosis from an exam and X-ray is what determines the real severity, which is exactly why getting seen quickly matters more than trying to guess at the outcome or the treatment beforehand on your own, without an X-ray to confirm what's actually happening.
