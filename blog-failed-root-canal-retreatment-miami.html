Can a root canal fail?
Yes, sometimes. Here is what happens next.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

A root canal can fail, though most treated teeth stay comfortable and healthy for many years. When one does run into trouble, it usually shows up as pain on biting, swelling, or a small bump on the gum near the tooth, sometimes years after treatment. The good news is that a failed root canal does not always mean losing the tooth. This guide covers the signs, the usual causes and the realistic options, including when an endodontist should take over.

Short answer: yes, a root canal can fail, either because infection never fully cleared or because bacteria got back in later through a leaking crown, new decay or a crack. Options include retreatment, a small procedure at the root tip done by an endodontist, or extraction and replacement. An exam and X-ray show which one fits your tooth.

What a failed root canal actually means

A root canal removes the inflamed or infected pulp, cleans the canals and seals them. The bone around the root tip then has a chance to heal. When dentists say a root canal has failed, they usually mean infection is still present or has come back around the root, even though the tooth was treated.

That can happen on two timelines. Some teeth never fully settle: the soreness that should fade after a few days lingers for weeks, or a shadow at the root tip on the X-ray does not shrink at follow-up. Other teeth are fine for years and then flare up, usually because something new let bacteria back inside.

One point that confuses people: a treated tooth has no living nerve inside, yet it can still hurt. The ligament and bone around the root have their own nerves, and they react to pressure and inflammation. So pain in a root-canal-treated tooth is real and worth checking.

Signs a treated tooth may be in trouble

Some tenderness for a few days after a root canal is expected. These signs are different, and they deserve an exam:

  • Pain when you bite or chew on the tooth that does not fade, or returns after months of comfort
  • A tooth that feels tender when you press it with a finger or tap it
  • Swelling of the gum near the tooth, or along the jaw or cheek
  • A small pimple-like bump on the gum that comes and goes, sometimes releasing a salty or bad taste; this is often a drainage path from an infection at the root
  • A deep ache that is hard to pin to one tooth
  • No symptoms at all, but a dark area at the root tip on a routine X-ray

That last one surprises people. Infection at the root can be quiet for a long time, which is why dentists sometimes recommend treating a tooth that does not hurt. Go to the ER right away if swelling is spreading toward your eye or down your neck, or comes with fever or trouble breathing or swallowing. If you are not sure whether the problem is this tooth or another one, our page on dental abscess treatment explains how infections are tracked down.

Why root canals fail

There is rarely one simple culprit, but these are the usual reasons:

  • A missed or hidden canal. Some teeth have an extra canal, or a branch too small to see on a flat X-ray. Bacteria left there can keep an infection alive.
  • Canals that could not be fully cleaned. Very curved, narrow or calcified canals are hard to reach to the tip.
  • A leaking crown or filling. If the top of the tooth is not sealed, saliva seeps down into the canals. A temporary filling left in place for months is a common source.
  • New decay. A cavity at the edge of the crown can reach the canal filling and reinfect it.
  • A crack. A crack running down the root lets bacteria in and often cannot be repaired. This is the outcome most likely to mean the tooth has to come out.

Delay plays a part too. A treated back tooth that waits a long time for its crown is more likely to leak or crack, which is one reason dentists push to finish the final restoration promptly.

For most of these cases, he refers patients to an endodontist (a root canal specialist), because that is usually the most predictable path for the tooth.

Failed root canals: common questions

How long after a root canal can it fail?

It can happen within the first months if the infection never fully cleared, or many years later if bacteria get back in through a leaking crown, new decay or a crack. Most treated teeth do well for a long time. A follow-up X-ray helps confirm the area is healing.

Is a failed root canal an emergency?

It is urgent if you have swelling, especially swelling that is spreading, fever, or trouble breathing or swallowing; go to the ER for those. Pain on biting or a small gum bump without those signs should still be seen soon, ideally within days, not months.

Can antibiotics fix a failed root canal?

Antibiotics may be prescribed when an infection is spreading, but they cannot clean the inside of the root. Once the medicine stops, the infection usually returns unless the source is treated with retreatment, a root-tip procedure or extraction.

Is it better to redo a root canal or get an implant?

If the tooth is solid and has no crack in the root, keeping it with retreatment is often worth trying. If the root is cracked or little tooth is left, extraction and an implant may be more predictable. The X-rays and an endodontist's opinion help decide.

Does a failed root canal mean the dentist did something wrong?

Not necessarily. Teeth can have hidden canals, and new decay, a leaking crown or a crack can cause problems years after good treatment. What matters now is finding the cause and choosing the most predictable fix.
