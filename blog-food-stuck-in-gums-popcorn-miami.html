Popcorn stuck in your gum?
How to get it out without making it worse.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

A popcorn hull stuck in your gum is one of the most common small dental emergencies, and in most cases you can get it out at home with floss, warm salt water and a little patience. The thin, curved hull slips under the gum edge like a splinter, and the gum swells around it, which is why it feels so stubborn. This guide covers safe ways to remove it, what not to poke with, the signs that it has turned into a gum abscess, and why food keeps getting trapped in the same spot.

Short answer: swish warm salt water, then floss gently on both sides of the tooth, curving the floss under the gum and pulling it out sideways. A water flosser on a low setting can help. Never dig with pins, knives or a toothpick forced deep. If it won't come out within a day or two, or the gum swells, throbs or tastes bad, see a dentist.

How to get it out, gently

Go slowly. The gum is already irritated, and force tends to push the hull deeper. Try these steps in order, and repeat them a few times through the day:

  • Rinse first. Stir about half a teaspoon of salt into a cup of warm water and swish hard around the area for 30 seconds. This can loosen the hull and calm the gum.
  • Floss with a curve. Slide the floss down between the teeth, wrap it in a C shape against the side of the tooth and ease it just under the gum. Then pull the floss out sideways through the gap instead of snapping it back up. Do both neighboring teeth.
  • Try a knot. Tying a small knot in the middle of the floss gives it something to catch the edge of the hull as you pass it through.
  • Use water. A water flosser on a low setting, aimed along the gum line rather than straight into it, can flush the hull out.
  • Brush the gum line. A soft toothbrush angled toward the gum, with small gentle strokes, sometimes lifts it free.
  • Give it a few hours. As the swelling settles, the gum often loosens its grip and the hull works its way out.

If it hurts, a cold compress on the outside of the cheek and an over-the-counter pain reliever taken as the label directs, if it is safe for you, can help while you keep trying.

What not to use

When something is stuck, it is tempting to grab whatever is closest. These do more harm than good:

  • Pins, needles, paper clips, the tip of a knife or a fork tine: they can cut the gum and carry bacteria in
  • A wooden toothpick or metal pick pushed deep under the gum, which can drive the hull farther down
  • Fingernails, which can tear the gum and aren't clean
  • Aspirin or any tablet placed on the gum, which can burn the tissue
  • Heat packs on the face if the area is swelling
  • Checking it every few minutes with your tongue or a finger, which keeps the gum inflamed

A toothpick used lightly along the surface of the tooth is fine. The problem is forcing anything into the pocket under the gum.

When it turns into a gum abscess

A hull left under the gum for days can trap bacteria and set off a gum abscess, a pocket of infection next to the tooth. It usually develops over a few days, not hours. Watch for:

  • Throbbing pain that is getting worse rather than better
  • A swollen, shiny or red bump on the gum beside the tooth
  • A bad taste, or pus when you press near the area
  • The tooth feeling tender or slightly raised when you bite

Those signs need a dentist within a day, because an abscess generally doesn't clear without the cause being removed and the area drained. Our dental abscess treatment page explains what that involves. Go to the ER now if swelling spreads to the face, toward the eye or down the neck, or comes with a fever or trouble swallowing or breathing.

Why food keeps getting stuck in the same spot

If one spot catches popcorn, mango strings or shredded beef every time, it is probably a food trap. Healthy neighboring teeth touch firmly, and that tight contact keeps food out. Common reasons the contact fails:

  • A filling or crown with a broken, worn or rough edge
  • A small gap that opened between two teeth as they shifted, often after a nearby tooth was lost
  • A cavity between the teeth that has started to change the tooth's shape
  • A crown or filling that was shaped without a tight contact against its neighbor
  • Gum recession that leaves a small triangle-shaped space near the gum line
  • A deep gum pocket, where food slips under the gum more easily
  • A wisdom tooth that is only partly through the gum

Food packing into the same place day after day is worth mentioning at your next visit even when it comes out easily. Over time it raises the risk of decay between the teeth and of gum inflammation, and fixing the contact is often simple. Our post on cavities between teeth explains how those are found.

What a dentist does

If you can't get it out, a dentist usually can in a few minutes. The area is numbed if needed, the hull is lifted out with fine instruments, and the pocket is flushed clean. If an abscess has formed, it is drained and the cause is treated.

Then the real fix: repairing a broken or rough filling with a new tooth-colored filling, treating a cavity between the teeth, reshaping a contact, or cleaning out a deep pocket. If the gum around that tooth keeps catching food because of gum disease, a deep cleaning may be recommended, as explained on our gum disease treatment page.

Food stuck in your gums: common questions

How do you get a popcorn kernel out of your gums?

Rinse with warm salt water, then floss gently on both sides of the tooth, curving the floss under the gum line and pulling it out sideways. A water flosser on a low setting can also help. If it is still stuck after a day or two, or the gum is swelling, see a dentist.

Will a popcorn hull come out on its own?

Sometimes, as the gum swelling goes down over a few hours to a day. Don't count on it if the area keeps hurting. A hull left under the gum for several days can lead to a gum abscess.

Can food stuck in your gums cause an infection?

Yes. Food trapped under the gum feeds bacteria and can cause a painful gum abscess, usually over a few days. Throbbing pain, a swollen bump, a bad taste or pus are signs to see a dentist promptly.

Why does food always get stuck between the same two teeth?

Usually because the contact between those teeth is no longer tight. A broken filling, a cavity, a gap from shifting teeth, gum recession or a crown without a firm contact are common reasons. A dentist can often fix the contact so the trap closes.

Is it okay to use a toothpick to get food out of my gums?

Lightly along the tooth surface, yes. Forcing a toothpick, pin or metal pick deep under the gum can tear the tissue and push the food farther in. Floss, salt water rinses and a water flosser are safer.
