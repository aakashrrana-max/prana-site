how to get kids to actually brush their teeth

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: Kids resist brushing for predictable reasons — it's boring, rushed, or feels out of their control. Small changes to timing, tools, and routine usually work better than reminders or bribery.

If getting your child to brush feels like a nightly negotiation, you're not doing anything wrong — this is one of the most common frustrations parents bring up at checkups, at every age from toddler to tween. A few practical adjustments tend to make a much bigger difference than repeating 'go brush your teeth' louder or more often, and most of them take less effort than the daily argument itself.

Understand Why Kids Resist in the First Place

Brushing asks a young child to stand still, hold a tool correctly, and do something they can't really see the result of — that's genuinely a lot to ask, especially when they're tired and want to be done at bedtime. For toddlers, it can also just be a control issue: brushing is one of the few things a parent still does to them rather than with them, which naturally invites pushback. Knowing this helps reframe the goal — it's not about forcing compliance through repetition, it's about making the routine something the child can actually participate in and feel some ownership over.

Make the Tools Age-Appropriate

A toothbrush with a small head, soft bristles, and a grip sized for small hands makes a real, measurable difference, as does a toothpaste flavor your child doesn't actively hate. For kids under three, a rice-grain-sized smear of fluoride toothpaste is typically enough; for ages three to six, a pea-sized amount is standard — check with your dentist if you're ever unsure what's right for your child's specific age and needs. Letting your child pick their own toothbrush color or a favorite character on it can also lower resistance considerably without changing anything meaningful about the actual routine itself.

Turn It Into a Routine, Not a Request

Brushing at the same two points every day — right after breakfast and right before bed — tends to work better than brushing 'whenever there's time,' because it removes the moment-to-moment negotiation entirely from the equation. A two-minute timer, a favorite song, or a sticker chart for consistency (not perfection) can help younger kids stay engaged without turning it into a nightly power struggle. Brushing alongside your child, so they can watch and copy your motion and technique, also works especially well for toddlers and preschoolers who learn by imitation.

Know When to Step In and Finish the Job

Most children don't have the fine motor control to brush thoroughly on their own until around age six to eight, even if they insist loudly on doing it entirely themselves. A reasonable approach is to let them brush first for independence, then do a quick follow-up pass yourself, especially along the back molars where most cavities actually start and where kids tend to miss spots. This isn't about distrust or taking over — it's simply a physical skill that takes years to fully develop, not unlike tying shoes or learning handwriting. Framing the follow-up pass as a normal, expected part of the routine, rather than a correction, tends to keep kids from feeling like their effort wasn't good enough.

When Brushing Struggles Might Mean Something Else

If your child consistently avoids brushing because it seems to hurt, or cries specifically when one area is touched, that's worth mentioning at their next visit rather than assuming it's just typical resistance — sensitivity or an early cavity can genuinely make brushing uncomfortable in a way a young child can't always articulate. Regular checkups and cleanings also help reinforce good habits over time, since kids often listen to a dentist's advice in a way they don't always take from a parent at home. If it's been a while since your child's last visit, a free consult is a good, low-pressure place to start.

Flossing: The Step That Usually Gets Skipped

Once brushing is a manageable routine, flossing tends to be the next thing parents give up on, mostly because it's fiddly with small hands and small mouths. Floss picks with a small handle are often easier for parents to manage than string floss when helping a child, and starting the habit as soon as two teeth touch each other helps normalize it early rather than introducing it as a new, unfamiliar chore later on. Most kids need help flossing well into elementary school, and that's completely normal rather than a sign anything is being done wrong. A dentist can show you the right technique at a checkup if it's not clicking at home. Establishing the habit early, even if it's just one or two teeth at first, makes it much easier to expand into a full daily routine as your child gets older and more coordinated.
