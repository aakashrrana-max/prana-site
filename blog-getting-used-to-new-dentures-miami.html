Getting used to new dentures,
one week at a time.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Getting used to new dentures usually takes a few weeks, and the first days are the hardest. Your tongue, cheeks and lips have to learn to hold something new in place, your gums have to toughen up where the denture rests, and your brain has to relearn speaking and chewing with it. This guide walks through what is normal, practical drills for speech and eating, and the problems that should not be waited out.

Short answer: most people feel noticeably more comfortable within a few weeks, though lower dentures and first-ever dentures often take longer. Read aloud to retrain speech, start with soft food cut small and chew on both sides at once. Expect extra saliva and a few sore spots, and get sore spots adjusted rather than living with them.

What the first weeks usually feel like

Everyone adapts at a different pace, and this is a rough pattern, not a promise. Someone replacing an old denture usually adapts faster than someone wearing one for the first time.

A typical adjustment period with new dentures
TimeWhat many people notice
First few daysThe mouth feels full, lips may look pushed out, extra saliva, some words sound slurred, eating is slow and careful
First two weeksSore spots appear where the denture presses; speech improves day by day; soft foods become easier
Weeks three and fourSaliva settles, most sounds are back, chewing a wider range of foods, fewer adjustments needed
After about a monthMany people stop thinking about the denture most of the day; lowers may still feel less stable than uppers

If you had teeth removed the same day the denture went in (an immediate denture), the timeline is different. The gums are still healing and shrinking, so expect more adjustment visits and, later, a reline once healing is done. Our dentures page explains immediate and conventional dentures.

Speaking clearly again

Your tongue is used to touching teeth and gums in exact spots to make each sound. A denture moves those landmarks a few millimeters, and the tongue needs practice to find them again. Practice speeds it up a lot:

  • Read aloud for a few minutes, several times a day: a newspaper, a book, the back of a cereal box. Bilingual households can practice in both languages, since each has its own tricky sounds.
  • Repeat the words that trip you up slowly, then at normal speed. Sounds made with the tip of the tongue and teeth, like s, z, f, v and th, are usually the slowest to come back.
  • Count out loud from sixty to seventy; the run of s sounds is a good workout.
  • If the denture clicks, slow down a little. If it keeps clicking after the first couple of weeks, it may be moving and should be checked.
  • If the denture lifts when you laugh, cough or yawn, bite gently and swallow to reseat it.

Talking to yourself in the car or on a walk counts. The more you speak with the denture in, the faster it stops sounding like you are speaking with one.

Eating: a step-by-step progression

Chewing with a denture is a new skill. Build up to it rather than starting with a steak:

  • Days one to three: soft foods that need little chewing, such as yogurt, eggs, mashed potatoes, soups, oatmeal, ripe banana and well-cooked rice and beans.
  • The rest of the first week: add tender fish, ground meat, soft pasta and cooked vegetables, cut into small pieces.
  • Weeks two and three: try firmer foods, still in small pieces. Put food on both sides of your mouth and chew on both sides at the same time, which keeps the denture balanced.
  • After that: slowly add crunchier foods. Bite with the side teeth, not the front teeth, and cut corn off the cob and slice apples rather than biting into them whole.

Be careful with very hot drinks and soups at first. The denture covers part of your mouth and changes how heat feels, so a cafecito can be hotter than it seems. Sticky foods like caramel and chewing gum, and very hard foods like nuts and hard bread crusts, stay tricky for many denture wearers.

Saliva, gagging and taste

Extra saliva is normal for the first days. Your mouth treats the denture like food and responds the only way it knows. Swallow more often than usual, and it usually fades within a week or two.

Some people gag at first, especially with an upper denture that covers the palate. It often eases as the mouth gets used to the feel. Breathing slowly through your nose, keeping your mind busy, and wearing the denture for longer stretches each day can help. If gagging does not improve, tell your dentist, since the back edge of the denture can sometimes be adjusted.

Food may taste a little different at first, partly because the palate is covered and partly because your mouth is concentrating on the new denture. Taste usually feels more normal as you adapt.

Sore spots, adjustments and adhesive

Sore spots are the most common problem in the first weeks, even with a well-made denture. Gums under a denture are being pressed in a new way and some areas take more load than others. Rinsing with warm salt water can soothe them.

Do not file, sand or bend your denture at home to relieve a sore spot. It is easy to remove too much and ruin the fit. Instead, call for an adjustment. If you can, wear the denture for a few hours before the visit so the dentist can see the red pressure mark and know exactly where to relieve it. If a spot is very painful, it is fine to take the denture out to let it rest, but put it back in before the appointment.

Adhesive can add confidence while you learn, especially for a lower denture. Use the least amount that works: a few small dots or thin strips on a clean, dry denture, never a thick layer. Clean all of it off the denture and your gums every day. If you need more and more adhesive to keep the denture in place, that is a sign the fit should be checked, not a reason to use more.

When to call, and how we help

Call or text your dentist if you notice any of these:

  • A sore spot that is not improving after a couple of days, or an ulcer that does not heal within about two weeks
  • The denture rocks, drops often or clicks after the first couple of weeks
  • Your bite feels uneven, or you bite your cheek or tongue repeatedly
  • Burning, white patches or redness under the denture
  • Pain on chewing that keeps you from eating enough

New dentures: common questions

How long does it take to get used to new dentures?

Many people feel noticeably more comfortable within a few weeks, though it varies. First-time wearers, lower dentures and immediate dentures placed after extractions often take longer. Daily practice speaking and eating speeds things up.

Should I sleep with my new dentures in?

Usually not. Most dentists recommend taking dentures out at night so your gums can rest, and keeping them in water or a soaking solution. With an immediate denture, you may be told to keep it in for the first day or so after extractions, so follow your dentist's instructions.

Why do my new dentures make me talk funny?

Your tongue is used to touching your teeth and gums in specific places, and the denture changes those spots slightly. Reading aloud and repeating difficult words helps it relearn. Most speech changes improve within a couple of weeks.

Is it normal for new dentures to hurt?

Some tenderness and a few sore spots in the first weeks are common. Sharp pain, a sore that keeps getting worse, or an ulcer that does not heal are not normal and should be adjusted. Do not try to grind down the denture yourself.

Do I need to use denture adhesive?

Not always. A well-fitting upper denture often holds without it, while a lower denture may feel more secure with a small amount. Use only a little, clean it off daily, and see your dentist if you need more and more over time.
