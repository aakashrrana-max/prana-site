Gum pocket numbers explained:
how to read your periodontal chart.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Gum pocket numbers are measurements, in millimeters, of how deep the groove between each tooth and its gum is. In general, 1 to 3 mm is considered healthy, 4 mm is a warning zone, and 5 mm or more usually means bacteria and tartar are sitting deeper than brushing or a regular cleaning can reach. But the pocket depth is only one line on a periodontal chart. This guide decodes the rest of it, from bleeding points and recession to mobility and furcation, and explains why your numbers change.

Short answer: 1 to 3 mm without bleeding is usually healthy. 4 mm, especially with bleeding, suggests inflammation or early disease. 5 mm and deeper usually points to periodontitis and often leads to a deep cleaning. Bleeding, recession, loose teeth and bone loss on X-rays matter as much as the depth, so read the numbers together rather than one at a time.

How the chart is recorded

A periodontal probe is a thin, blunt instrument marked in millimeters. It is slid gently into the groove where the gum meets the tooth until it meets light resistance. Each tooth is measured at six spots: three on the cheek side (front corner, middle, back corner) and three on the tongue or palate side.

That is why you hear a rhythm like "three, two, three... four, three, five" called out during the exam. Each group of three numbers belongs to one side of one tooth. A full chart for a mouth with all its teeth has well over a hundred readings, and the pattern across them tells more than any single number.

What the pocket numbers usually mean

These ranges are general guidance. A dentist reads them alongside your X-rays, your bleeding points and your history, so the same number can mean different things in two different mouths.

Pocket depths and how they are usually read
DepthWhat it usually meansWhat often follows
1 to 3 mmA healthy groove, especially if it doesn't bleedRegular cleanings and good home care
4 mmA warning sign: swollen gums from gingivitis, or early loss of attachmentCloser attention, better cleaning between teeth, a recheck; sometimes a deep cleaning of that area
5 mmA true pocket in many cases, with tartar and bacteria below the gumOften scaling and root planing (deep cleaning) for the affected teeth
6 mm and deeperMore advanced periodontitis, usually with bone loss visible on X-raysDeep cleaning and re-evaluation; persistent deep pockets may lead to referral to a periodontist

One caution: swollen gums can make a pocket read deeper even when no bone has been lost. When the swelling goes down after a cleaning, that number can shrink. That is the difference between gingivitis and periodontitis, explained in more detail in our post on gingivitis vs periodontitis.

The other marks on your chart

Most charts carry more than pocket depths. Here is what the other notations usually mean:

  • Bleeding points: often shown as red dots or marks over a reading. Bleeding when the probe is placed is a sign of active inflammation at that spot. A 4 mm site that doesn't bleed is usually less concerning than one that does.
  • Recession: how far the gum edge has moved down from where it should sit, exposing root. It is often recorded on a separate row.
  • Attachment level: pocket depth plus recession. This shows how much support the tooth has actually lost. A 3 mm pocket can look healthy on its own, but with 3 mm of recession, the tooth has lost 6 mm of attachment.
  • Mobility: how much a tooth moves, often graded from 0 (normal) to 3 (moves a lot, sometimes up and down).
  • Furcation: on back teeth with two or three roots, the area where the roots split. If bone has been lost there, the probe can enter between the roots. It is usually graded from I (just detectable) to III (passes through).
  • Pus: sometimes noted when it appears from a pocket, a sign of active infection.
  • Plaque or tartar marks: some offices note where plaque is found, which shows which areas home care is missing.

Why your numbers change from visit to visit

Small changes are normal. Probe angle, pressure and swelling vary, so a reading can shift by about a millimeter without anything real happening. Dentists usually pay attention when the same spot changes by 2 mm or more, or when a pattern repeats across several teeth.

Numbers usually get smaller after a deep cleaning because the gum tightens as inflammation settles, though recession may increase slightly at the same time. They can get larger with active gum disease, during pregnancy when gums swell more easily, or when diabetes is poorly controlled. Smoking deserves a special mention: it narrows blood vessels in the gums, so smokers may bleed less on probing even when disease is present, which can make a chart look better than the gums really are.

What usually comes next

Your chart, plus X-rays that show the bone, lets a dentist decide between a regular cleaning and a deep one. Our post on deep cleaning vs regular cleaning walks through that decision. If periodontitis is diagnosed, it is often described by stage (I to IV, how severe) and grade (A to C, how fast it seems to be progressing), which guides how closely it needs to be watched.

After a deep cleaning, the gums are measured again, typically several weeks later, to see which spots responded. Areas that stay deep and keep bleeding may need more treatment. Questions worth asking about your chart

  • Which areas are deepest, and do they bleed?
  • Do my X-rays show bone loss, and where?
  • Is this gingivitis or periodontitis, and if periodontitis, what stage?
  • How do today's numbers compare with my last chart?
  • Which spots should I focus on at home, and with what: floss, small brushes between teeth or a water flosser?
  • When will you measure again?

Gum pocket numbers: common questions

Is a 4 mm gum pocket bad?

Not necessarily, but it is worth watching. A 4 mm pocket can be swollen gum from gingivitis that shrinks with better cleaning, or early periodontitis. Bleeding at that spot and bone loss on X-rays help tell which.

What gum pocket depth is considered normal?

Readings of 1 to 3 mm without bleeding are generally considered healthy. Deeper readings, or shallow ones that bleed, suggest inflammation that needs attention.

Can gum pockets get smaller?

Yes. When inflammation goes down after a professional cleaning or deep cleaning and better home care, pocket readings often shrink. Bone that has been lost doesn't usually grow back on its own, which is why the attachment level and X-rays matter too.

Do 5 mm pockets always need a deep cleaning?

Often, but not always. It depends on whether the site bleeds, whether there is bone loss and tartar below the gum, and how many teeth are involved. A single 5 mm reading may be watched or treated locally, while several usually point toward a deep cleaning.

What does furcation mean on my dental chart?

Furcation is the area where the roots of a back tooth split apart. A furcation note means bone has been lost in that area, which makes it harder to clean and can affect the long-term outlook for that tooth.
