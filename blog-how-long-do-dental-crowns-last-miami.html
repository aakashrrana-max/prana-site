How long do dental crowns last?
Often many years. The tooth underneath decides.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

How long do dental crowns last? A well-made crown on a healthy tooth often lasts many years, frequently a decade or more, but there is no fixed expiration date. The crown material is rarely what gives out first. Far more often, the problem starts with the tooth or gum underneath it, so how you bite, clean and live with your crown matters as much as how it was made.

Short answer: many crowns last a decade or more, and some last much longer. Lifespan depends on the tooth under the crown, your gums, whether you grind or clench, the material, and how well the edge of the crown is kept clean. A crown should be checked, not replaced on a schedule.

Why there is no single number

Ask five dentists how long a crown lasts and you will hear a range, because two crowns made the same week can have very different lives. A crown on a front tooth of someone with healthy gums and a light bite carries a small load. A crown on a lower molar of someone who clenches through stressful workdays takes heavy force thousands of times a day.

It also helps to know what a crown actually is: a cap cemented over a shaped tooth. The cap itself is very hard. The weak point is the margin, the thin seam where crown and natural tooth meet near the gum. Bacteria can work at that seam, gums can pull away from it, and cement can wash out over time. Most crowns that need replacing fail at that edge or underneath, not because the cap wore out. Dentists check the seam, the gum and the bite at every visit, plus an X-ray when one is due.

What shortens a crown's life

Most of the things that cut a crown's life short are habits or conditions you can do something about.

Grinding and clenching

Night grinding puts sideways force on teeth that chewing never does. On a crown, that can chip porcelain, crack the tooth under it or loosen the cement. Our guide to teeth grinding at night explains the signs, and a custom night guard is one of the simplest ways to protect crown work.

Decay at the margin

A crown cannot get a cavity, but the tooth right at its edge can. Plaque left along the gumline, frequent sugary sips through the day and a dry mouth all raise the risk. Decay there often grows quietly under the crown before it hurts.

Gum recession

When gums pull back, the root surface below the crown edge is exposed. Root surfaces are softer than enamel and decay more easily, and on some older crowns a dark line appears where gum used to cover the seam. Our post on receding gums covers why it happens.

Chewing ice and using teeth as tools

Crunching the ice left in a cold drink is a common habit in Miami heat, and it is hard on crowns. So are hard candy, popcorn kernels, pen caps, and tearing open packages with your teeth. Each can chip porcelain or crack the tooth beneath it.

A weak tooth to start with

A crown is only as strong as what it sits on. A tooth with very little structure left, a deep crack or a borderline root may not last as long, even under an excellent crown. You should hear about that before treatment, not after.

Signs a crown needs attention

None of these automatically means replacement, but each is a reason to call rather than wait for your next routine visit:

  • The crown feels loose, moves, or clicks when you bite or press it with your tongue
  • A bad smell or taste near one tooth that brushing does not fix, which can mean a gap at the seam
  • Floss catches or shreds at the edge of the crown every time
  • Pain when biting or a new ache in a crowned tooth, which can point to decay, a crack or a nerve problem
  • A dark line or grey shadow at the gumline, especially on older crowns with a metal layer
  • Puffy or bleeding gum around just that tooth
  • A chip in the porcelain or a rough edge your tongue keeps finding
  • Food packing between the crown and the tooth next to it

A small chip can sometimes be polished, and a loose crown on a healthy tooth can sometimes be recemented. If a crown has already come off, see our steps for a lost crown or filling until you are seen.

How a crown is replaced

Replacing a crown looks a lot like getting the first one, with one extra step at the start: the old crown has to come off. Most crowns are cemented too firmly to lift off whole, so the old one is usually cut away carefully with the tooth numbed. Here is the usual order:

  • Diagnosis first. An exam and an X-ray show whether there is decay under the crown, a problem at the root tip, or bone loss around the tooth.
  • Removing the old crown under local anesthetic.
  • Cleaning up the tooth. Any decay is removed and the remaining tooth is checked for cracks.
  • Rebuilding if needed. A core buildup restores missing structure so the new crown has something solid to hold.
  • Scanning and a temporary. A digital scan replaces messy impressions, and you wear a temporary while the new crown is made.
  • Fitting the new crown, checking the seam, contacts with neighboring teeth, the bite and the shade, then cementing it.

One honest caveat: nobody can see under a crown completely until it is off. Sometimes the tooth is in good shape and the new crown goes right back on. Sometimes decay has reached the nerve and the tooth needs a root canal first. Occasionally the tooth is cracked or too weak to keep, and the conversation turns to extraction and replacement. Dr. Rana explains those possibilities before starting, so a surprise does not turn into a shock.

Helping your next crown last longer

Daily care around a crown is the same as for natural teeth, with a little extra attention at the seam. Angle the toothbrush bristles toward the gumline, floss with a C-shape around the crowned tooth, and use fluoride toothpaste. If you sip coffee or sweet drinks through the day, try to finish them in one sitting rather than over hours. Wear a night guard if you grind, and keep regular checkups so small problems at the edge are caught while they are small.

Material choice matters too: zirconia suits back teeth and heavy biters, while layered ceramics look most lifelike up front. Our dental crowns page explains materials and each visit, and our in-house lab makes it easier to fine-tune the fit at that critical seam.

Crown lifespan: common questions

Can a dental crown last a lifetime?

Some do, especially on teeth with healthy gums and a gentle bite, but nobody can promise it. Lifespan depends on the tooth underneath, your gums, grinding habits and home care. Regular checkups help a crown last as long as it can.

Do crowns need to be replaced every 10 years?

No. There is no set replacement schedule. A crown that fits well, has a sealed edge and sits on a healthy tooth can stay in place as long as it keeps doing its job. It is replaced when there is a problem, such as decay at the edge, a crack or a poor fit.

Why does my crown smell bad?

A bad smell around one crown often means food and bacteria are collecting at a gap along its edge, or that there is decay or gum inflammation underneath. Better flossing may help a little, but it is worth having the crown checked with an X-ray soon.

Can a chipped crown be repaired instead of replaced?

Sometimes. A small chip can often be smoothed and polished, and some can be patched with tooth-colored material. A large chip, a crack through the crown or a chip that exposes the metal layer underneath usually means a new crown is the more reliable fix.

Does insurance cover replacing an old crown?

Many plans do cover replacement as a major service, but often only after a certain amount of time has passed since the last crown on that tooth. Deductibles, waiting periods and annual maximums also apply. We check your benefits for you before any treatment.
