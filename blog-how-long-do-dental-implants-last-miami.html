how long do dental implants last?

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: The titanium implant post itself is designed to last decades, often a lifetime, while the crown attached to it typically has a shorter lifespan and may need replacement after years of normal wear.

"How long will this last?" is one of the first questions most patients ask before committing to a dental implant, and it's a fair one given the investment of time and money involved. The honest answer has two parts: the implant post and the crown on top age differently, and mixing them up is where a lot of the confusion about implant lifespan comes from. Here's what actually determines how long each one holds up, and what you can do to get the most out of yours.

Two Parts, Two Different Lifespans

A dental implant has two components: the titanium post placed into the jawbone, and the crown attached on top that looks and functions like a tooth. The post fuses with your bone through a process called osseointegration, and once that's complete, it becomes a stable, long-term fixture, many patients keep the same implant post for the rest of their life without ever needing it touched again. The crown, however, is exposed to the same daily biting, grinding, and wear as a natural tooth, so it's the part more likely to need attention, a repair, or eventual replacement over the years, the same way a natural tooth's enamel wears down over decades of use.

What Determines How Long Your Implant Holds Up

Bone quality and density at the placement site matter a great deal, which is why a thorough exam and 3D scan happen before any implant is planned, rather than deciding on the spot. Your oral hygiene routine afterward matters just as much: an implant doesn't get cavities, but the gum and bone around it can still develop infection if plaque builds up along the gumline, the same way untreated plaque causes problems around natural teeth covered in our general dentistry care. Bite force, grinding habits, and whether you keep up with regular cleanings all factor into the long-term picture too, since each one puts a different kind of stress on the implant and the tissue around it.

What Can Shorten an Implant's Lifespan

Smoking is one of the biggest risk factors, since it slows healing and reduces blood flow to the gum tissue around the implant, which makes it harder for the body to fend off infection if one starts. Untreated gum disease, chronic teeth grinding without a nightguard, and skipping regular dental visits can also put an implant at risk over time, often quietly, without obvious symptoms until the problem has progressed. None of these make an implant unsuitable for you, they're simply things worth managing so your investment holds up the way it's meant to for as many years as possible.

Simple Habits That Protect Your Investment

Brushing and flossing around an implant the same way you would a natural tooth, keeping up with routine cleanings, and mentioning any grinding or clenching to your dentist all go a long way toward protecting your implant for the long haul. A nightguard is a simple, low-effort step if grinding is an issue. If you ever notice looseness, swelling, or discomfort around an implant, don't wait it out hoping it resolves on its own, book an exam so it can be checked early, when problems are almost always easier and less expensive to address.

How Implant Longevity Compares to Other Options

It helps to see implant lifespan in context. A traditional bridge relies on two natural teeth to support it, and those teeth can wear out or need retreatment well before a well-cared-for implant would need attention. Removable dentures typically need relining or eventual replacement as your jawbone and gum tissue change shape over time, since nothing is anchoring them into the bone the way an implant is. None of this means a bridge or denture is a poor choice for every patient, but when longevity itself is the main priority, it's worth understanding what each option is realistically built to do over the years, not just what it costs on day one.
