How long do dentures last?
Longer when the fit is kept up.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

How long do dentures last? With good care, a denture can serve you for several years, but its fit usually changes well before the denture itself wears out, because your jaw keeps changing underneath it. That is why most people need a reline or two along the way and, eventually, a new set. Knowing the signs helps you act before a loose denture causes sore spots, chewing trouble or changes to your face.

Short answer: many people wear the same denture for several years, with relines to refit it as the gums and bone shrink. A new denture is usually needed when the teeth have worn flat, the base is cracked or stained, or relines no longer give a stable fit. A yearly checkup, even with no natural teeth, catches problems early.

Two things age: the denture and your mouth

A denture is made to fit your mouth on one particular day. From then on, both sides of that fit slowly change.

Your mouth changes

After teeth are lost, the bone that used to hold them gradually shrinks, because nothing inside it is being loaded the way roots load it. The gum on top follows. This happens faster in the first months after extractions and continues more slowly for years, and the lower jaw often changes more than the upper. Weight changes, some medicines and dry mouth can affect the fit too. For someone with a partial denture, losing another tooth or a change in a clasp tooth also shifts things.

The denture wears

Acrylic denture teeth are softer than natural enamel, so they slowly wear flat with chewing. The base can pick up stains and fine scratches that hold bacteria and odor. Repeated flexing can create stress cracks, especially in a lower denture or a thin area of an upper one. On partials, clasps can loosen with years of taking the denture in and out.

Reline, rebase or new denture?

These three fixes are often confused. They solve different problems:

A word of caution about drugstore reline kits: they can change your bite, trap bacteria and irritate the gums, and they are hard to remove cleanly if a professional reline is needed later. They may get you through a weekend, but they are not a substitute for a proper refit.

Signs your denture needs attention

  • It feels loose or drops when you talk or laugh, and you need more adhesive than you used to
  • Sore spots that keep coming back, or new ones after years of comfort
  • Food slips under it at almost every meal
  • Clicking when you speak, which often means the denture is moving
  • The teeth look flat or short, and chewing takes more effort than it did
  • Your face looks shorter: lips thinner, chin closer to the nose, or deeper folds at the corners of the mouth
  • Cracks at the corners of the mouth, which can come with a bite that has closed down
  • Biting your cheek or tongue more often
  • Stains or an odor that cleaning no longer removes
  • A visible crack or a tooth that has come loose from the base

The facial changes deserve a special mention. As denture teeth wear and the jaw shrinks, the distance between your nose and chin can gradually close, and the lower face can look older or more collapsed. A new denture with properly restored height often helps support the lips again. If a denture actually breaks, avoid gluing it at home and see our emergency dental care page for next steps.

Why checkups matter even with no teeth

It is easy to think a dental visit is pointless once every tooth is gone. In fact, a denture checkup looks at more than the denture.

The dentist examines your gums, the ridges the denture rests on, the roof of your mouth, your tongue, cheeks and lips. Redness under an upper denture can be a sign of irritation or a yeast overgrowth, often linked to wearing the denture overnight. Sore spots that do not heal within about two weeks, white or red patches, and lumps should be checked, because a denture can hide changes you would otherwise notice. The denture is also checked for fit and bite, and cleaned professionally.

Once a year is a common recommendation for people with full dentures, and more often if you wear a partial or have sore spots. Bring your denture with you, and mention any new medicines, since many cause dry mouth, which makes dentures less comfortable.

Getting more years from your denture

Clean it daily with a soft brush and a denture cleaner rather than regular toothpaste, keep it moist when it is out, never use hot water, and handle it over a towel or a sink of water. Take it out at night unless you have been told otherwise. Do not bend clasps or file down sore spots yourself: a small adjustment in the office is quick and keeps the fit intact.

Denture lifespan: common questions

How often should dentures be relined?

There is no fixed schedule. A reline is done when the denture has become loose but is otherwise in good shape. Many people need one every couple of years, and someone with recent extractions may need one sooner, after the gums have healed.

Can I keep using old dentures if they still feel okay?

Possibly, but comfort can hide problems. Worn teeth can let your bite close down, and a loose fit can irritate the gums without much pain. A checkup can tell you whether a reline, a rebase or a new denture makes sense.

Why do my dentures suddenly feel loose?

It is usually gradual change in the gums and bone that you notice all at once. Weight loss, dry mouth from a new medicine, or a recent extraction under a partial can also change the fit. A reline often restores it if the denture is otherwise sound.

Do I still need to see a dentist if I have no teeth?

Yes. Checkups look at your gums, palate, tongue and cheeks for sore spots, irritation or changes that should be watched, and they check the fit and bite of your denture. Many dentists recommend at least one visit a year.

What is the difference between a reline and a rebase?

A reline adds new material to the inside of the denture so it fits your gums again. A rebase replaces the entire pink base while keeping the existing teeth, which helps when the base is cracked or worn but the teeth are still good.
