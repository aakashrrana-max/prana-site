how long do porcelain veneers last?

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Porcelain veneers typically last 10 to 15 years, sometimes longer, when they're well made and well cared for. Composite veneers wear out sooner, usually in the 4 to 8 year range. How long yours actually last comes down to bite forces, daily habits, and how well the edges and gumline are maintained.

One of the first questions we get about veneers is simple: how long will they hold up? The honest answer depends on the material and on you. Here's what actually determines a veneer's lifespan, and what you can do to get the most out of yours.

What "Lasting" Actually Means

A veneer doesn't have an expiration date stamped on it. "Lasting" means the porcelain stays intact, the edge stays sealed against the tooth, and the color still matches your other teeth. Veneers can fail in small ways long before they fall off: a hairline chip at the edge, a dark line creeping in at the gumline, or a shade shift compared to the teeth next to it. Any of those is a sign the veneer is aging out, even if it's technically still attached. That's why a lifespan estimate is really a range, not a guarantee, and why regular checkups matter more than people expect. Catching an early chip or a loosening margin means a simple fix; ignoring it usually means the tooth underneath is now exposed to decay or the veneer needs to be replaced sooner than it should. It's also worth remembering that the natural tooth underneath a veneer is still a living tooth that can develop a cavity at the margin, so a veneer doesn't take that tooth out of the normal maintenance conversation the way people sometimes assume.

Porcelain vs. Composite Veneers: Different Timelines

Material matters. Porcelain is a ceramic that's fired and glazed, so it resists staining and holds its polish for years, which is why porcelain veneers commonly reach the 10 to 15 year mark. Composite veneers are built from the same resin used in tooth-colored fillings, sculpted directly onto the tooth. They cost less up front and can be done in a single visit, but the resin is softer, picks up stain faster, and typically needs touch-ups or replacement in the 4 to 8 year range. Neither material is wrong — they just suit different budgets and timelines, which is why we walk through both options at a free consult instead of assuming one fits everyone.

What Shortens a Veneer's Lifespan

Veneers are strong, but they're not indestructible. Grinding or clenching at night puts repeated force on the thin edges and is one of the most common reasons a veneer chips early. Using your teeth as tools — opening packages, biting fingernails, tearing tape — does the same thing. Chronic gum inflammation can also shorten a veneer's life, because healthy gum tissue is what holds the margin looking clean; if the gums recede or stay inflamed, the edge of the veneer becomes visible or vulnerable to decay underneath. Even something as simple as skipping regular cleanings lets plaque build up right at that seam, which is exactly where problems start. Diet plays a role too — frequent exposure to very acidic drinks can gradually affect the bonding line over years, the same way it affects natural enamel, so the habits that protect your teeth generally protect your veneers as well.

Habits That Help Veneers Go the Distance

The good news is that most of what protects a veneer is the same as what protects a natural tooth. Brush twice daily and floss once daily, keep up with professional cleanings on schedule, and don't skip checkups just because the tooth looks fine on the surface at a glance. If you grind your teeth, a nightguard is one of the simplest ways to add years to a veneer's life. Avoid using your front teeth to open things, and treat very hard foods (ice, hard candy, unpopped popcorn kernels) with the same caution you'd give a natural tooth. None of this requires special products — it's ordinary dental hygiene applied consistently, day after day, without needing to overthink it. Patients who stay on top of these basics are usually the ones who get the upper end of that 10 to 15 year range, or beyond it, while patients who treat veneers as maintenance-free tend to run into problems earlier than expected.

Signs It's Time to Replace a Veneer

Watch for a rough or chipped edge you can feel with your tongue, a gap or dark line forming at the gumline, or a veneer that no longer matches the shade of teeth around it. Sensitivity to hot or cold on a veneered tooth is also worth mentioning at your next visit. None of these mean something urgent is wrong, but they're exactly the kind of thing a dentist should look at rather than something to self-diagnose.
