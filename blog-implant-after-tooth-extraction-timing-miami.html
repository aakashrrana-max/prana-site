How long after an extraction can I get an implant?
Same day, a few weeks, or a few months.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

How long after an extraction you can get an implant depends on the socket the tooth leaves behind. Sometimes the implant goes in at the same visit. More often the gum is allowed to heal for several weeks, or the bone for a few months, before the implant is placed. Infection, the shape of the bone walls and whether it is a front or back tooth usually decide which path is safest.

Short answer: there are three common timings. Immediate placement puts the implant in the fresh socket the same day. Early placement waits roughly several weeks for the gum to close. Delayed placement waits a few months for bone to fill in, often after a graft. A clean socket with strong walls opens the door to faster options; infection or thin bone usually means waiting.

The three timings, explained

Dentists usually describe implant timing in three broad groups. The exact number of weeks varies from case to case, so treat these as typical ranges, not fixed rules.

Immediate placement does not always mean an immediate tooth. Even when the implant goes in the same day, the crown is often added months later, once the bone has bonded to the implant. Our post on same-day teeth explains when a temporary tooth can be attached right away.

What decides which timing you get

Nobody can promise a timeline before seeing the tooth, the X-rays and a 3D scan. These are the factors that weigh most:

  • Infection. An abscess, a draining bump on the gum or a large area of bone loss at the root tip usually means the site needs time to heal first.
  • The bone walls. A socket is like a cup. If all its walls are intact after the tooth comes out, an implant can sometimes be wedged in firmly. If a wall cracked or was already lost to infection, waiting and grafting is usually wiser.
  • Front or back. Upper front teeth often have a very thin outer wall of bone. If it shrinks after placement, the gum can recede and show a gray edge, so appearance drives careful planning. Molars leave wide sockets with several roots, which an implant rarely fills well on day one.
  • Gum thickness. Thin gum tissue tends to recede more, which can tip the balance toward waiting in a visible area.
  • Your health and habits. Smoking, uncontrolled diabetes and some medications slow healing and may favor a staged approach.
  • Nearby structures. In the lower jaw a nerve runs below the back teeth, and in the upper jaw the sinus sits above them; their position can limit what is possible right away.

Socket grafting: keeping the bone for later

After a tooth comes out, the bone around the socket naturally shrinks, especially in the first months. If an implant is planned but cannot go in the same day, the dentist may fill the socket with graft material, often covered with a thin membrane, to help hold the shape of the ridge. This is called socket preservation or ridge preservation.

A socket graft does not speed things up. It adds a healing period, commonly a few months, but it can mean a better base for the implant and less need for a bigger graft later. It is not always necessary: some sockets heal well on their own, and a graft adds a line to the quote. If you are unsure whether you need one, ask what would happen to the bone without it. Our guide to bone grafting before implants covers the types of grafts in plain terms.

What you wear while you wait

For a back tooth, many people simply leave the space empty for a while and chew on the other side. Nobody sees the gap, and an empty site heals without pressure on it.

For a front tooth, going without is rarely acceptable, so a temporary replacement is planned before the extraction. The usual choices are a small removable plastic tooth on a plate, a clear retainer-style tray with a tooth in it, or a temporary bridge bonded to the neighboring teeth. Whatever you wear, it should not press on the healing socket or the new implant. People who are losing many teeth may wear an immediate denture during healing.

The first days after the extraction matter too. Protecting the blood clot lowers the chance of a dry socket; see how to prevent dry socket.

Planning the extraction with the implant in mind

Ideally, the implant is planned before the tooth comes out. A gentle extraction that protects the bone walls keeps more options open, and the scan taken beforehand shows whether an immediate implant is realistic. Implant timing after an extraction: common questions

Can I get an implant the same day as my extraction?

Sometimes. It is most likely when the socket shows no infection, the bone walls are intact and the implant can be held firmly in place. Many cases still wait, especially molars and infected teeth. A scan and exam before the extraction show which applies to you.

Is it bad to wait too long after an extraction to get an implant?

Waiting years usually means more bone has shrunk, which can make a graft necessary and the case more involved. Waiting a few weeks or months on purpose, as part of a plan, is normal. If you already had a tooth out long ago, an implant is often still possible after a 3D scan of the bone.

Do I need a bone graft after a tooth extraction?

Not always. A socket graft is often suggested when an implant is planned later and the bone walls are thin or damaged. Some sockets heal well without one. Your dentist should explain why a graft is or is not recommended in your case.

Can an implant go in an infected socket?

Active infection usually needs to be cleared and the site allowed to heal before an implant goes in. In selected cases a dentist may clean the site thoroughly and still place the implant, but waiting is the more common and cautious approach. Fever or spreading swelling needs urgent care right away.

Is immediate implant placement less successful?

In well-chosen cases, immediate implants can do well. The key words are well chosen: the bone, gum and infection status have to suit it. When they do not, a short wait often gives a more predictable result.
