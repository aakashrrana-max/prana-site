when should kids get their first cavity filled?

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: Baby teeth get cavities just like adult teeth, and once decay reaches the dentin layer, a filling is usually the right call rather than 'waiting it out.' The earlier it's caught, the smaller and simpler the fix.

It's a common question from parents at their child's first few checkups: does a cavity in a baby tooth actually need to be filled, or can it just be watched until the tooth falls out on its own? The honest answer depends on how deep the decay already is, but timing matters far more than most parents expect, and waiting rarely makes the decision easier.

Baby Teeth Decay the Same Way — Just Faster

Cavities form the same way in a five-year-old's mouth as they do in an adult's: bacteria feed on leftover sugar and starch, produce acid, and that acid slowly dissolves the tooth's outer layer. The difference is that baby teeth have thinner enamel and a larger, softer inner layer (dentin), so decay tends to move through them more quickly once it starts. A spot that looks small on the surface can already be more advanced underneath, which is part of why baby teeth can go from a tiny dark speck to a deep cavity in what feels like a short window of time. That's one reason routine exams at a pediatric-friendly dental visit matter even before you think there's a problem — catching decay early keeps the treatment small, quick, and far less stressful for everyone involved.

Why 'It's Just a Baby Tooth' Isn't a Reason to Wait

Baby teeth hold space for the permanent teeth underneath them and guide how those adult teeth come in later. A cavity left untreated can grow into the nerve, cause a painful infection or abscess, and in some cases lead to the tooth being lost earlier than it should be — which can shift neighboring teeth out of position and crowd the space meant for the permanent tooth. An infected baby tooth can also make it uncomfortable for a child to chew, which affects eating habits and nutrition at an age when both matter a great deal for growth. None of this requires alarm if it's addressed reasonably promptly, but it is a real reason not to leave a known cavity unaddressed for months while hoping it resolves itself.

When Watching Is Reasonable — and When a Filling Is Needed

Not every early sign needs immediate drilling. A very early white or chalky spot that hasn't broken through the enamel can sometimes be managed with fluoride, better brushing, and monitoring at regular checkups, since that stage can occasionally be arrested before it becomes a true cavity. But once decay has broken through into the dentin — visible as a brown or dark spot, or picked up clearly on an X-ray — it typically needs a filling. Left alone, that stage of decay doesn't reverse itself; it only gets bigger, moves closer to the nerve, and eventually becomes a bigger, more involved procedure than it needed to be. The distinction between 'watch it' and 'fill it now' really does come down to depth, which is why an exam matters more than a visual guess at home.

What a Filling Visit Actually Looks Like for a Kid

Most children's fillings today use a tooth-colored composite material placed after the area is numbed with a local anesthetic. For a small, single cavity, the appointment is usually quick and similar in feel to what an adult would experience during a routine filling. For younger children, multiple cavities in one visit, or kids who feel real anxiety about dental visits, sedation options can make the appointment calmer and considerably easier for both the child and the parent waiting in the lobby. There's no one-size-fits-all approach here — some kids do perfectly fine with numbing alone, and others benefit from a gentler, more supported experience, which is worth discussing openly at the exam.

Signs It's Already Time to Get Checked

If your child complains of pain when chewing, reacts noticeably to cold or sweet foods, or you notice a dark spot on a tooth, don't wait for the next scheduled cleaning — those are signs decay may already be past the early stage and progressing. A free consult exam with X-rays can tell you exactly what's going on and what it will take to fix it, with no guessing involved and no pressure to commit to anything on the spot. If your child is in obvious pain or has visible swelling, treat it like a dental emergency rather than something to schedule around for later in the week.

What Regular Checkups Do to Prevent This Whole Conversation

The easiest way to avoid the 'does my child need a filling' question altogether is simply keeping up with checkups every six months, whether or not anything seems wrong at home. Decay that's caught at the very earliest stage often needs nothing more than a fluoride treatment and closer attention to brushing, avoiding a filling entirely. Between visits, limiting sugary snacks and drinks, especially sipping on juice or milk throughout the day, and making sure your child is brushing twice daily with the right amount of fluoride toothpaste, does more to prevent cavities than almost anything done after the fact. Consistent preventive visits also mean your child gets comfortable with the dental office long before any actual treatment is needed, which tends to make future appointments easier for everyone.
