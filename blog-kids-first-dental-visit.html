Kids' first dental visit in Miami:
when to go and what to expect.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: Most dental guidance recommends a child's first dental visit by their first birthday, or within about six months of their first tooth appearing. The first visit is short and gentle — a look at the teeth and gums, guidance for parents on brushing and diet, and a chance for your child to get comfortable in the chair.

Planning your child's first dental visit in Miami usually raises the same two questions: when, and what actually happens. The general guidance is simple — by their first birthday or within six months of their first tooth coming in, whichever comes first — and the visit itself is much gentler than most parents expect.

When should a child have their first dental visit?

General dental guidance recommends a first visit by a child's first birthday, or within about six months of their first tooth coming in — whichever happens first. That might sound early for a baby with just a few teeth, but the point of that first visit isn't a deep cleaning; it's catching any early issues and giving parents a foundation for care before problems have a chance to start.

Why so early, if there are only a few teeth?

Baby teeth matter more than people often assume — they hold space for adult teeth, help with speech development, and let kids chew properly while their jaws grow. Early visits let a dentist catch things like baby bottle tooth decay, teething concerns, or bite issues while they're small and simple to manage, and they build a habit of regular checkups long before your child is old enough to be anxious about it.

There's also a habit-forming benefit that's easy to overlook: a child whose first dental memory is calm and low-pressure tends to carry that comfort into future visits. Starting later, once a child already has a full set of baby teeth and stronger opinions about strangers, can sometimes mean the first visit is also the first time they're asked to sit still for someone new — which is a harder starting point than it needs to be.

What actually happens at a first dental visit?

The first visit is short and low-key by design. Typically it includes a gentle look at the teeth, gums, and bite, often with your child in your lap for a baby or toddler, and a conversation with you about brushing technique, diet, teething, thumb-sucking or pacifier habits, and fluoride. The goal is comfort and information, not a long procedure.

Most first visits don't involve any cleaning tools touching your child's teeth at all — it's closer to a friendly look-and-count than a full appointment. If your child is a little older and has more teeth, the dentist may also do a light polish, but nothing is done that requires your child to sit still for long.

How can you prepare your child (and yourself)?

A little preparation goes a long way for a first visit. A few things that help:

  • Keep your own language neutral and positive — avoid words like "hurt" or "shot," even reassuringly, since it can plant a worry that wasn't there.
  • Bring a favorite small toy or comfort item if your child has one.
  • Schedule for a time of day when your child is usually rested and calm, not right before a nap.
  • Let your child know it's just a "tooth checkup" and that the dentist will count their teeth.
  • Bring a list of any questions about brushing, diet, or habits like thumb-sucking so you remember to ask.

Does a family dentist see kids, or do you need a pediatric dentist?

Many family dental practices, including Prana, see kids as part of regular care for the whole family, from toddlers through teens and adults, which can be simpler than juggling separate practices for parents and children. That said, some children — particularly those with special healthcare needs, significant anxiety, or complex dental issues — are better served by a pediatric dentist, who has additional specialized training for those situations. A good family practice will tell you plainly when a referral makes sense rather than trying to manage every case in-house. What should kids' checkups look like after the first visit?

Once your child has had their first visit, most guidance points to regular checkups roughly every six months going forward, similar to adults, though your dentist may suggest a different interval based on what they see. These follow-up visits are where cleanings, cavity checks, and fluoride guidance become a more routine part of the appointment as your child gets older and more teeth come in. Regular visits from an early age are one of the simplest ways to keep small issues from turning into bigger, more stressful ones later.

Questions, answered

When should a child's first dental visit be?

General guidance recommends a first visit by a child's first birthday, or within about six months of their first tooth coming in, whichever comes first. It's an early, low-key visit meant to catch issues early and get your child comfortable with the dentist.

What happens at a baby's first dental checkup?

It's typically brief and gentle: a look at the teeth and gums, often with your child on your lap, and a conversation with parents about brushing, diet, teething, and habits like thumb-sucking. It's more about information and comfort than treatment.

Is it normal for a baby to have no teeth yet at their first dental visit?

Yes, that's common and expected. The first visit is timed around the first birthday regardless of exactly how many teeth have come in, since the goal includes guidance for parents as much as an exam of teeth.

Do I need a pediatric dentist, or can a family dentist see my child?

Many family dental practices see kids as part of routine care. Children with special healthcare needs, significant anxiety, or more complex dental issues are sometimes better served by a pediatric dentist, and a good family practice will refer you when that's the case.

How can I prepare my toddler for their first dental visit?

Keep your language neutral and positive, avoid words that suggest pain, schedule the visit for a calm time of day, and let them know it's just a checkup where the dentist counts their teeth. Bringing a comfort item can help too.
