knocked-out tooth: what to do in the first 30 minutes

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: Pick the tooth up by the crown, never the root, rinse it gently without scrubbing, and try to reinsert it into the socket immediately if possible. If that's not possible, keep it moist in milk or saliva and get to an emergency dentist within 30 minutes to give it the best possible chance of being saved.

A knocked-out tooth is one of the true dental emergencies where speed genuinely changes the eventual outcome more than almost anything else you do. This isn't a moment to start searching around online while precious minutes tick by — here's the exact sequence to follow right now, why every minute genuinely counts, and it's genuinely worth knowing ahead of time if you have kids playing contact sports.

First: find the tooth and handle it correctly

Pick the tooth up by the crown, the white chewing surface on top, and never touch the root portion at all, which is exactly where the delicate cells needed for successful reattachment actually live and can easily be damaged. If it's visibly dirty, rinse it gently with plain water for no more than a few seconds — do not scrub it with anything, use any soap on it, or wrap it in a towel to try drying it off completely, since all of that damages the surface further and lowers the odds of success. Try to stay calm, since a clear head genuinely helps you handle the next few steps correctly, and it helps the injured person stay calm too, especially if it's a child who's already scared and looking to you for reassurance.

Try to put it back in the socket if you can

If the person is calm enough to manage it and it's an adult permanent tooth, gently try to reinsert it into its own socket facing the correct direction and have them bite down softly on gauze or a clean cloth to hold it steadily in place while you head to the dentist right away. This gives the tooth its very best possible chance overall, since staying inside the socket keeps the delicate root cells alive and properly moist the entire time on the way in. Don't force it if it doesn't slide in easily — move on to keeping it moist instead rather than fighting to make it fit.

If it can't go back in, keep it moist

If reinsertion genuinely isn't possible for whatever reason in the moment, place the tooth in a small container of milk instead, which is gentle on the delicate root surface, or have the person hold it carefully in their own mouth against the inner cheek if they're old enough not to accidentally swallow it. Do not let the tooth dry out completely at any point or sit in plain tap water for any extended period, since both of those situations damage the surviving root cells badly and quickly. A store-bought tooth-preservation solution works too, if you happen to have one on hand from a sports first-aid kit, and it's worth keeping one in your bag if your kids play contact sports regularly, right alongside a mouthguard for practice and games.

Time is the single biggest factor

The chances of successfully saving and reattaching a knocked-out tooth drop significantly the longer it stays outside the mouth without proper care, which is exactly why getting to an emergency dentist within roughly 30 minutes matters so much more in this specific situation than with most other dental injuries you might encounter. Call ahead as you're heading over so the team already knows you're coming and can prepare the room in advance for your arrival, cutting down on time spent waiting once you get there.

What happens when you arrive

The dentist will clean and carefully evaluate the socket itself, reposition and splint the tooth in place if it's still genuinely viable, and check for any additional injury to the jaw or surrounding teeth nearby that might have happened at the same time as the impact. Follow-up care and ongoing monitoring over the following weeks is genuinely part of giving the tooth its best realistic chance of staying in place successfully for the long term ahead, so don't be surprised at all if a short-term check-in visit is recommended a week or two later on.

If the tooth can't be saved

Sometimes, despite genuinely fast action on your part and doing everything right, a tooth still can't be saved in the end — and that's not any kind of failure on your part at all.
