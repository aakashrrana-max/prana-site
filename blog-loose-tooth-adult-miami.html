A loose tooth as an adult?
Why it happens, and how it can often be treated.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

A loose tooth as an adult is never part of normal aging, and it always deserves a dental visit. The most common cause is bone loss from gum disease, which often develops without pain. Grinding or a bite that overloads one tooth, an injury and an infection at the root can also loosen a tooth, and some of those causes can be reversed if they are found early. This guide covers what is urgent, how a dentist works out the cause and the treatment paths that follow.

Short answer: call the same day if the tooth loosened after a blow, has shifted position, or comes with swelling, pus or pain. A tooth that has slowly become loose over months is usually a gum disease problem and needs a visit within a week or two. Depending on the cause, treatment ranges from a deep cleaning, a bite adjustment or a night guard to splinting, or extraction and replacement.

Is a loose adult tooth an emergency?

It depends on how it happened and what comes with it. Use this as a rough guide:

  • ER now: a loose tooth after a hard blow to the face with a possible broken jaw, a head injury, heavy bleeding, or swelling with fever, trouble breathing or trouble swallowing.
  • Same day: a tooth loosened by a fall or a hit, a tooth that has moved and changed your bite, or a loose tooth with swelling, a pimple on the gum, pus or throbbing pain.
  • Within a week or two: a tooth that has gradually become loose with no pain, especially with gums that bleed, have pulled back, or show new gaps between teeth.
  • Mention at your next visit: a very slight movement you only notice when you push it with your tongue, with no other symptoms. Even then, don't wait months.

Why adult teeth get loose

Gum disease and bone loss

Periodontitis slowly destroys the bone and fibers that hold teeth in place. It often causes little pain, so the first thing many people notice is a tooth that moves, food catching in a new gap, or front teeth that seem to be fanning out. Bone lost to gum disease usually doesn't grow back on its own, so the earlier it is treated, the more support is left.

Grinding, clenching and bite overload

Heavy force can stretch the ligament around a root and make a tooth move, even with healthy bone. It often happens to a tooth that hits first when you close, or one carrying extra load after nearby teeth were lost. The good news is that this kind of looseness can improve once the force is reduced.

Injury

A fall, a sports hit or biting something very hard can stretch the ligament or crack the root. A root fracture can make a tooth feel loose even though the gum looks normal.

Infection

An abscess at the root tip or in the gum can make a tooth feel loose and raised. Once the infection is treated, the tooth often firms up again.

Less common causes

Hormonal changes in pregnancy can make teeth slightly more mobile. Teeth also move a little during orthodontic treatment, which is expected. Rarely, a cyst or growth in the jaw, or a medical condition affecting bone, is the cause, which is one more reason not to ignore a loose tooth.

How a dentist works out the cause

Finding the reason matters more than the looseness itself, because the treatment is completely different for each cause. At the exam, Dr. Rana usually:

  • Grades how much the tooth moves, side to side and up and down
  • Measures the gum pockets around it and checks for bleeding or pus
  • Takes X-rays to see the bone level, the ligament space around the root, any infection and any sign of a root fracture
  • Checks your bite with marking paper to see whether that tooth takes too much force, and looks for wear from grinding
  • Tests whether the nerve inside the tooth is still alive
  • Asks about recent injuries, pregnancy, medicines and health conditions

Treatment paths

Many loose teeth can be kept, especially when the cause is caught early. The usual options, by cause:

A splint is a thin wire or fiber strip bonded to the backs of the loose tooth and its neighbors, so they share the load while the area heals. Splinting is also sometimes used to stabilize teeth loosened by gum disease, once the disease itself is treated. For grinding, see our night guards page. What to do until your visit

  • Leave it alone. Wiggling it with your tongue or fingers keeps the ligament irritated.
  • Chew on the other side and choose softer foods. Skip ice, nuts, hard bread and sticky candy.
  • Keep brushing and cleaning gently around it. Plaque around a loose tooth makes the gum problem worse.
  • If you already have a night guard, wear it.
  • Watch for swelling, pus, fever or the tooth moving more, and call sooner if any appear.

Loose adult teeth: common questions

Can a loose adult tooth tighten back up?

Sometimes. Teeth loosened by an injury, an infection or heavy bite force often firm up once the cause is treated. Teeth loosened by gum disease may tighten somewhat as inflammation settles, but bone that was lost usually doesn't grow back.

What should I do if my adult tooth is loose but doesn't hurt?

See a dentist within a week or two. Looseness without pain is often a sign of gum disease, which can progress quietly. Until then, avoid wiggling it and chew on the other side.

Can stress make your teeth loose?

Indirectly, yes. Stress often goes along with clenching and grinding, and heavy force over time can loosen teeth. Stress can also make gum disease harder to control. A night guard and gum treatment address the dental side.

Is a loose tooth after a fall an emergency?

It should be seen the same day. The tooth may need to be repositioned and splinted, and an X-ray checks for a root fracture. Go to the ER first if there is a head injury, a possible broken jaw or bleeding that won't stop.

Will a loose tooth from gum disease fall out?

Without treatment, it may. With a deep cleaning, regular maintenance and good home care, many loose teeth stay in place for years. Very loose teeth with little bone left sometimes need to be removed and replaced.
