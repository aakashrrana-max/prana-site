Oral cancer screening at the dentist,
a few quiet minutes that matter.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

An oral cancer screening is part of a thorough dental exam: the dentist looks at and feels the soft tissues of your mouth, lips and neck for changes that shouldn't be there. It takes a few minutes, it doesn't hurt, and most people don't even notice it is happening. Most spots turn out to be harmless, but finding the rare serious one early can make a big difference. Here is what the dentist checks, who has higher risk, and how to check yourself between visits.

Short answer: during an exam, the dentist checks your lips, cheeks, gums, tongue, floor of the mouth, palate, throat and neck by sight and touch. Risk is higher with tobacco, heavy alcohol use, HPV and years of sun on the lips. A sore, patch or lump that lasts more than two weeks should be looked at, and anything suspicious is referred for evaluation and, if needed, a biopsy.

What happens during the screening

There is no special machine involved in a standard screening. It is a careful, systematic look and feel, done in the same order every time so nothing gets skipped. A typical sequence:

  • Face and neck: the dentist checks for swelling or asymmetry, then feels along the jaw, under the chin and down the sides of the neck for lumps or tender lymph nodes.
  • Lips: outside and inside, looking for scaly, crusted or color-changed areas, especially on the lower lip.
  • Cheeks and gums: the insides of the cheeks are pulled gently aside to check for patches, ulcers or thickened tissue.
  • Tongue: you stick it out, and the dentist holds the tip with a piece of gauze to see the sides and underneath, the areas where changes are easiest to miss.
  • Floor of the mouth: felt with one finger inside and one outside under the chin.
  • Palate and throat: the roof of the mouth, the tonsil area and as much of the back of the throat as can be seen.

Tell the dentist about anything you have noticed yourself, such as a sore spot, a patch, a change in your voice or trouble swallowing. Your observations help, because you see your own mouth every day.

Who has a higher risk

Anyone can develop oral cancer, including people with no obvious risk factors, which is why the check is part of routine exams for adults. Some things raise the odds:

  • Tobacco in any form: cigarettes, cigars, pipes, hookah and smokeless tobacco. Dip and chew are held against the same spot for long periods.
  • Alcohol, especially heavy drinking, and particularly combined with tobacco.
  • HPV (human papillomavirus): certain types are linked to cancers at the back of the mouth and throat, including the tonsils and the base of the tongue. These areas are hard to see, so symptoms such as a lasting sore throat, ear pain on one side or a lump in the neck deserve attention. Ask your physician about HPV vaccination.
  • Sun on the lips: years of sun exposure raise the risk of lip cancer, usually on the lower lip.
  • Age and health: risk rises with age, and a weakened immune system can raise it too.

Miami sun and your lips

Lips get sunburned just like skin, but they have very little pigment to protect them, and people rarely think to cover them. In Miami that adds up quickly: boating on the bay, fishing, beach volleyball, weekend runs, outdoor jobs and plain daily errands under a strong sun all year round.

Early sun damage on the lip can look like a persistent dry, scaly or rough patch, a blurred border between the lip and the skin, or a sore that crusts, heals and comes back. A lip balm with SPF, reapplied after swimming or eating, and a wide-brimmed hat are easy habits. Point out any lip patch that doesn't clear up at your next exam, or sooner if it bleeds or keeps returning.

Warning signs to check for at home

Once a month, take two minutes in front of a mirror in good light. Pull your lips out and look inside, check both cheeks, lift your tongue and look underneath, then hold it to each side with a clean cloth. Feel your neck on both sides. See a dentist or a physician if you notice any of these lasting more than two weeks:

  • A sore or ulcer that hasn't healed
  • A white or red patch, or a mix of the two
  • A lump, thickening or rough area in the mouth or on the lip
  • Pain, numbness or tingling in the mouth or lips without an obvious cause
  • Trouble chewing or swallowing, or a feeling that something is caught in the throat
  • Hoarseness or a voice change that doesn't go away
  • A lump in the neck, or ear pain on one side
  • A tooth that loosens for no clear reason, or a denture that suddenly stops fitting

Most of these turn out to be something minor, such as a cheek bite, a canker sore or rubbing from a rough tooth or denture. The two-week rule exists because ordinary sores usually heal by then.

If something looks unusual

Finding a spot does not mean finding cancer. When something irritating is the likely cause, such as a sharp tooth edge or a rubbing denture, the dentist usually removes the irritant and rechecks the area in about two weeks. Spots that heal are noted in your chart.

An area that doesn't heal, or that looks suspicious from the start, is referred for further evaluation, often to an oral surgeon or an ear, nose and throat doctor. Only a biopsy, where a small sample of tissue is examined in a laboratory, can confirm what a spot is. Dr. Rana is a general dentist; he doesn't diagnose cancer himself, but he explains what he saw, makes the referral promptly and tells you what to expect.

Oral cancer screening questions

Does an oral cancer screening hurt?

No. The dentist looks at your mouth and gently feels your cheeks, tongue, floor of the mouth and neck. It takes a few minutes and is usually part of a normal exam.

How often should I get an oral cancer screening?

For most adults it happens at each regular dental exam. People with higher risk, such as tobacco users, may be checked more closely. Between visits, a quick monthly self-check helps you notice changes early.

What does early oral cancer look like?

It can look like a sore that doesn't heal, a white or red patch, or a lump or thickened area. Early changes often cause no pain at all, which is why they can go unnoticed. Anything lasting more than two weeks should be checked.

What happens if my dentist finds something suspicious?

If an irritant is the likely cause, the dentist usually removes it and rechecks the spot in about two weeks. If it doesn't heal or looks concerning, you are referred for evaluation, often to an oral surgeon or an ear, nose and throat doctor, and a biopsy may be done. Most spots that are checked turn out not to be cancer.

Can HPV cause mouth cancer?

Certain types of HPV are linked to cancers in the back of the mouth and throat. These areas are hard to see, so mention a lasting sore throat, one-sided ear pain or a neck lump to your dentist or physician. Ask your physician whether HPV vaccination is right for you or your children.
