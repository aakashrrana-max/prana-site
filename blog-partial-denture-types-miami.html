Types of partial dentures,
metal, acrylic and flexible compared.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

There are three main types of partial dentures: a cast metal framework, an all-acrylic partial and a flexible partial made of a nylon-type resin. All three fill gaps when you still have some healthy teeth, and all three come out for cleaning. They differ in how they grip, how they feel, how they look, how easily they can be repaired, and, importantly, how they treat the teeth you still have.

Short answer: a cast metal-framework partial is usually the most stable and durable long-term choice and is gentlest on the remaining teeth when well designed. An acrylic partial is quick and lower cost, and often used as a temporary. A flexible partial can look and feel nicer but is harder to adjust, reline or add teeth to later.

The three main types

Cast metal-framework partial

A thin framework cast in cobalt-chrome metal holds gum-colored acrylic and denture teeth. Small metal arms called clasps hug some of your natural teeth, and small rests sit in shallow seats shaped into those teeth so the partial is supported by the teeth, not only by the gums. Because the frame is rigid and thin, it can cover less of the mouth than an acrylic partial and still be strong. The main drawback is looks: a clasp on a front tooth can show when you smile, though designs try to place clasps where they hide.

Acrylic partial

Made entirely of pink acrylic with denture teeth, usually held by simple bent-wire clasps. A small version that replaces one or two teeth is often called a flipper. It is quicker and less costly to make, and easy to add a tooth to if you lose another one. It is also bulkier, rests mostly on the gums, and can be more prone to breaking. Many people wear one as a temporary while extraction sites heal or while an implant or bridge is planned.

Flexible partial

Made from a bendable nylon-type resin, with gum-colored clasps that blend with the gum instead of metal arms on the teeth. Many people like the look and the softer feel, and it is less likely to snap if dropped. The tradeoffs: it is harder to adjust, reline or add teeth to later, it can be harder to keep clean and stain-free, and because it flexes, it may not distribute biting force as evenly. Some flexible designs add small rests to help with that.

There is no single right answer. A partial that replaces two lower back teeth for a heavy chewer has different needs from one that replaces a front tooth while an implant site heals.

How a partial affects the teeth you still have

This is the part people rarely hear about, and it matters most. A partial is attached to your natural teeth every time you wear it, so the design decides whether those teeth stay healthy.

Teeth that hold clasps carry extra force, especially when the partial rocks. A partial that is supported on the gums only, as many acrylic and flexible partials are, can sink a little into the soft tissue under chewing pressure and twist the clasp teeth over time. A rigid metal framework with rests spreads the load more evenly and keeps the partial from sinking. That is why metal frameworks are often recommended for long-term use.

Clasps and the edges of a partial also trap plaque against the teeth. Clasp teeth are more likely to get decay at the gumline and gum inflammation if they are not cleaned carefully. Brushing those teeth well, taking the partial out to clean it every day, and keeping regular checkups make a real difference.

Before a partial is made, the remaining teeth need to be healthy. Cavities are filled first, gum disease is brought under control (see our gum disease treatment page), and a tooth that will hold a clasp sometimes needs a crown shaped to grip it well. Small rest seats may be smoothed into the enamel for a metal partial. These details are invisible later, but they shape how long both the partial and your teeth last.

Partial, bridge or implants?

A removable partial is not the only way to fill gaps. Each alternative fits a different situation:

  • Fixed bridge. For one or two missing teeth in a row with healthy teeth on both sides, a bridge stays in and needs no clasps. The neighboring teeth are crowned to support it. See dental bridges.
  • Implants. An implant replaces the root as well as the tooth and leaves neighboring teeth untouched. Our post on implant vs bridge cost compares the two fixed options.
  • Implant-assisted partial. One or two implants placed under a partial can stop it from rocking and reduce the load on the clasp teeth.
  • A partial now, something fixed later. Many people start with a partial while they heal, save or plan, then move to a bridge or implants.

Partials tend to make the most sense when several teeth are missing in different spots, when the teeth next to the gaps are not strong enough to support a bridge, or when surgery is not a good option.

Partial dentures: common questions

What is the most comfortable type of partial denture?

Many people find flexible partials comfortable because they are soft and have no metal clasps. A well-designed metal-framework partial can also feel very comfortable because it is thin and stable. Comfort depends heavily on fit and design, not only on the material.

Do partial dentures damage your teeth?

A well-designed, well-fitting partial that you clean daily should not harm your teeth. Problems come from partials that rock, sink into the gums or trap plaque against clasp teeth. Regular checkups help catch decay or gum inflammation around the clasp teeth early.

Can a tooth be added to a partial denture?

Often, yes. Acrylic partials are usually easy to add to, and metal-framework partials often can be modified. Flexible partials are harder to change, which is worth considering if other teeth are at risk.

Is a flipper a partial denture?

Yes. A flipper is a small, simple acrylic partial, usually replacing one or two teeth. It is commonly used as a temporary tooth while an extraction site heals or while an implant or bridge is planned.

How long do partial dentures last?

It varies. Metal-framework partials often last the longest, while acrylic partials are usually seen as shorter-term. How long any partial lasts depends on your remaining teeth, your gums, how well it is cleaned and whether it is adjusted as your mouth changes.
