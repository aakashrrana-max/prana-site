same-day crowns: how in-house milling speeds up treatment

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: A traditional crown usually takes two visits with a temporary in between while an outside lab makes the permanent one. With in-house milling, many crowns are designed and completed in a single visit, from $999.

Getting a crown used to mean two separate appointments: one to prepare the tooth and fit a temporary, and a second, one to two weeks later, to remove the temporary and cement the real crown once it came back from an outside lab. In-house milling changes that timeline for many patients, cutting the whole process down to a single visit in many cases these days. Here's how the same-day process actually works, step by step, and when it genuinely doesn't apply to your situation.

The traditional two-visit crown process

In the conventional process, the dentist prepares the tooth, takes an impression or scan, and places a temporary crown while the case is sent to an outside lab. The permanent crown is milled off-site and shipped back, which typically takes one to two weeks. Only then does the patient return for a second visit to remove the temporary and cement the final crown. Temporaries are functional but not ideal — they can come loose, wear down faster, or feel different from your natural bite while you wait, and a second appointment means blocking out time twice for one tooth, plus the risk of a temporary failing, coming loose, or trapping food against the gum before the real crown arrives — none of it dangerous, but all of it adds friction to what should be a straightforward fix for one tooth.

How the same-day process works instead

At Prana, the process starts the same way — preparing the tooth — but instead of taking a physical impression, the dentist captures a digital scan. That scan becomes a 3D design for the crown right there in the office, which is reviewed and adjusted on screen before anything is manufactured. Once the design is approved, the crown is milled from a solid ceramic block in the in-house lab, checked for fit and bite, and cemented — all in the same visit for many cases, without ever needing a lab-made temporary at all, and without a second trip back weeks later just to swap one temporary piece for the permanent one.

What the appointment actually feels like

From your perspective in the chair, the process looks like a longer single visit rather than a shorter one repeated twice. There's a waiting period while the crown is being milled — often used for other parts of your visit or simply a short break — followed by a fitting where your dentist checks the bite and adjusts as needed before final cementation. Most patients find one longer appointment easier to plan around than two separate ones spread over a couple of weeks, especially when time off work is limited or hard to coordinate with a busy work calendar in advance.

Who's typically a good candidate

Most single-tooth crown cases are well suited to the same-day process, including crowns needed after a large filling fails or a tooth cracks under normal chewing pressure. Your dentist will confirm during your exam whether your specific tooth is a good fit for same-day milling — for example, a tooth needing extensive gum-level work first may require an additional step before the crown itself can be designed and milled.

Why not every case is same-day

Some situations genuinely benefit from more planning time — a case involving multiple teeth, a bite that needs more extensive adjustment, or a restoration bundled with other treatment like a root canal completed first. In those cases, the in-house lab still speeds things up considerably compared to shipping to an outside facility, even if it isn't compressed into a single appointment. Your dentist will walk you through the realistic timeline for your case rather than assuming one approach fits everyone, and explain exactly why extra time is genuinely needed rather than just being cautious for no reason at all, so you know exactly what you're waiting on and why.
