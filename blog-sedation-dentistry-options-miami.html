nervous about the dentist? here are the sedation options available

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: Dental anxiety doesn't mean you have to avoid needed care. Several sedation options exist to help nervous patients get through treatment comfortably, from light options during the visit to deeper approaches for bigger procedures.

If putting off a dental visit because of anxiety sounds familiar, you're far from alone, and it's not something to be embarrassed about. Avoiding care because of fear tends to let small problems turn into bigger, more involved ones down the road. Sedation dentistry exists specifically to make that avoidance unnecessary, whatever the source of your anxiety happens to be, whether it's a bad past experience or simply never having felt comfortable in a dental chair. Here's an overview of what's available and how to think about which option fits you.

Why Dental Anxiety Deserves a Real Solution, Not Just Willpower

Dental anxiety is a well-recognized reason people delay or skip care entirely, and it isn't limited to people with a specific bad memory. Sounds, smells, past experiences, or just a general discomfort with feeling out of control in the chair can all contribute. The problem with avoidance is that dental issues rarely improve on their own; a small cavity left untreated can turn into something that needs a root canal, which is a bigger visit than the one that was being avoided in the first place. Sedation options exist precisely to break that cycle, so anxiety doesn't end up costing you more time in the chair down the line.

Lighter Options for Everyday Anxiety

For patients who feel nervous but don't need to be fully asleep, lighter approaches can take the edge off without a long recovery. Nitrous oxide, sometimes called laughing gas, is inhaled through a small mask and wears off within minutes of removing it, letting most patients drive themselves home afterward. Oral sedation, a pill taken before the appointment, produces a deeper sense of calm and often some drowsiness, and typically requires a ride home since its effects last longer. Both options are commonly used for routine procedures where anxiety, rather than the complexity of the work itself, is the main obstacle. Neither one requires a hospital setting or a specialist beyond your own dental team. We'll cover the specific differences between these two in more detail in a separate post.

Deeper Sedation for More Involved Procedures

For longer or more involved procedures, such as multiple implant placements or extensive work like full-arch restorations, deeper sedation options may be appropriate so the patient is more comfortable through a longer appointment. What's available and appropriate depends on the specific procedure and your health history, which is exactly the kind of thing that gets discussed and planned at your consult rather than decided on the spot. Patients considering a bigger restorative plan sometimes assume sedation isn't an option for them, when in reality it's often built directly into how longer procedures are structured.

What Actually Determines Which Option Is Right for You

The right sedation choice depends on a combination of factors: how anxious you genuinely are, what procedure you're having done, how long the appointment will take, and your overall health history. This isn't a one-size-fits-all decision, and it's not something to pick off a menu without a conversation first. At your visit, you'll go over your medical history and your specific concerns so the plan actually fits you, not a generic patient profile. It's also fine to say you're not sure what you need. Part of the conversation is figuring that out together rather than arriving with a firm answer already.

Telling Your Provider What Actually Triggers Your Anxiety

Not all dental anxiety comes from the same place, and being specific helps more than a general "I don't like the dentist." Some patients are mainly bothered by needles, others by the sound of a drill, others by the feeling of not being in control while lying back in a chair, and others by a genuinely difficult past experience. Naming the specific trigger, rather than describing anxiety in general terms, lets your provider address it directly, whether that means a warmer explanation of each step before it happens, a signal you can give to pause, or a particular sedation option suited to that trigger. None of this is something to feel embarrassed about bringing up, since it's information your provider genuinely needs to make the visit work for you.
