signs you need a filling or a crown

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: A filling repairs a small to moderate area of decay or a minor chip, while a crown covers and protects a tooth that's lost too much structure for a filling to support, such as after a large cavity, a root canal, or a significant crack. Only an exam and X-ray can confirm which one actually applies to your specific tooth.

Patients often ask this before they even sit down in the chair: is this going to be a quick filling, or something bigger and more involved? The honest answer depends on how much healthy tooth structure is actually left underneath the surface, not just on how the tooth currently feels day to day. Here's what tends to point toward each option, why guessing on your own from a mirror rarely works out the way people hope, and what the actual exam is looking for when Dr. Rana makes the call.

Sensitivity to sweets or temperature, without pain otherwise

A tooth that reacts to something sweet, hot, or cold but settles back down quickly once the trigger is gone is a fairly classic early sign of a cavity that's still small enough to be handled with a tooth-colored filling. Catching it at this early stage is exactly why routine exams matter in the first place — waiting around lets that same small cavity keep growing quietly until it eventually reaches a point where a simple filling can no longer fix it on its own, and something more involved becomes necessary instead. Brief, mild sensitivity that fades within seconds is a reasonable prompt to schedule a checkup, not to panic.

A visible hole, dark spot, or rough patch

Decay that's advanced enough to actually see with the naked eye in a mirror, or that your tongue can feel as a distinct rough spot or a small hole along a tooth's surface, usually still fits comfortably within filling territory as long as it hasn't reached too deep into the tooth's structure yet. An X-ray taken at your exam shows exactly how far the decay has actually traveled beneath the surface, which is the real deciding factor here, rather than how large or small the visible spot happens to look to the eye. A dark spot can look minor on the surface and still run considerably deeper underneath.

A large old filling that's cracking or leaking

Old, large fillings can crack, wear thin over the years of everyday use, or let bacteria sneak in slowly around their edges without anyone noticing right away. When a filling eventually needs to be redone but there isn't enough natural tooth structure left around it to support a fresh one securely, a crown is usually the more durable long-term answer than trying to patch the same weakened spot again with yet another filling that likely won't hold up as well. This is a genuinely common finding in patients who had large fillings placed decades ago.

A cracked tooth or one that's had a root canal

A tooth with a significant crack, or one that's already undergone a root canal procedure, has lost enough internal structure that a filling alone often isn't strong enough anymore to protect it under everyday biting forces. A crown covers the entire tooth from above and spreads that biting force far more evenly across it, which is exactly why it's the standard, expected follow-up after a root canal on a back molar that does most of the actual chewing work every day. Skipping the crown afterward leaves the weakened tooth genuinely more likely to fracture later on.

Pain that lingers well after the trigger is gone

If hot or cold sensitivity lingers for a noticeably long stretch after you've already stopped eating or drinking, or you start noticing a dull ache with no obvious trigger at all, that combination can point to decay or damage reaching closer to the nerve itself — territory that sometimes calls for a root canal and crown together rather than a filling by itself. This is genuinely not something worth trying to diagnose on your own at home; it needs a real, in-person exam and a proper X-ray to sort out, since the same lingering ache can also come from a much more minor issue.

Why guessing isn't worth it

Two teeth can look almost completely identical on the surface and still need entirely different treatment underneath once you actually look closer, which is exactly why self-diagnosing from a bathroom mirror rarely goes the way people expect.
