what happens if you skip your dental cleaning for a year?

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: After about a year without a cleaning, plaque you missed with brushing has usually hardened into tartar, which brushing alone can no longer remove. That buildup irritates gums, raises your risk of cavities between teeth, and can quietly progress toward gum disease before it ever hurts.

Life gets busy and a six-month cleaning turns into a year, then longer than that. Nothing about that feels dramatic in the moment, which is exactly the problem — the changes happen slowly enough that you don't notice them day to day, and there's rarely a single obvious event that tells you it's time to book. Here's what's actually happening in your mouth during that gap, why it doesn't announce itself the way a cavity eventually does, and why it's worth closing sooner rather than later.

Plaque doesn't wait for your calendar

Plaque forms constantly on your teeth, and brushing and flossing remove most of it if you're genuinely consistent about it every single day, morning and night. But even a good home routine tends to miss the same spots over and over — between teeth, right along the gumline, and behind the lower front teeth where saliva glands concentrate minerals that speed up hardening. Within a matter of days, that missed plaque begins hardening into tartar, sometimes called calculus by dental staff. Once it's tartar, no amount of brushing pressure, a fancier toothbrush, or a stronger mouthwash removes it; only a professional cleaning with the right hand instruments or an ultrasonic scaler can actually take it off the tooth surface for good. This is exactly why the six-month interval most dentists recommend exists in the first place — it's roughly how long a diligent routine can hold the line before professional help is genuinely needed.

A year gives tartar time to spread and thicken

The longer tartar sits on a tooth, the more rough, porous surface area it gives new plaque to grip onto, so buildup tends to accelerate rather than stay flat across a missed year. It also collects below the gumline where you genuinely can't see it in a mirror or reach it with a brush, which is exactly where early gum disease typically gets its start without any obvious warning sign. By the time a full year has passed, tartar has usually built up in visible layers rather than as a single thin coating near the gumline, which is part of why a long-overdue cleaning can reasonably take a bit more time and effort than a routine six-month one would. Some patients even notice their teeth feeling and looking slightly longer than before, which is often the gum tissue having receded away from the buildup rather than the tooth itself changing shape.

Your gums are usually the first to react

Tartar sitting along the gumline constantly irritates the soft tissue right next to it, and irritated gums bleed easily when you brush or floss, look noticeably puffier than usual, and can gradually start to pull away from the tooth surface over time. This early stage, called gingivitis, is fully reversible with a professional cleaning and a better home routine put in place afterward. Left alone for another extended stretch of time, though, that same ongoing inflammation can progress into a stage that begins affecting the actual bone that holds your teeth in place — a much harder problem to reverse once it sets in, and one that a simple cleaning alone can no longer fully undo. That's the real dividing line between an easy fix and a longer treatment plan, and it's worth catching the difference while it's still on the easy side.

Cavities get a head start you can't see

Regular cleaning visits usually come paired with a visual check and often X-rays that catch decay between teeth long before it ever causes any noticeable pain at all. Skip that checkpoint for a full year and a small, early cavity has that much more time to grow quietly toward the nerve, completely unnoticed by you, turning what might have been a simple filling into something bigger, considerably more expensive, and genuinely more uncomfortable to treat by the time it's finally found and addressed. A year is plenty of time for a cavity to cross that line, especially between teeth where you'd never spot it yourself.

Bad breath and stained teeth are the visible clues

Tartar buildup and the bacteria living inside it are a common source of persistent bad breath that ordinary brushing and mouthwash don't fully resolve, no matter how minty or strong the rinse claims to be. Surface staining also tends to build up faster once tartar gives it somewhere rough to cling onto, which is one reason teeth can look noticeably duller, yellower, or grayer the longer a cleaning gets put off, largely independent of your coffee, tea, or wine habits. If people around you have started reacting to your breath differently, it's a reasonable prompt to stop putting off the appointment.

Getting back on track is simpler than people expect

None of this means you need to feel any guilt about a missed appointment — it just means it's worth booking one before another year quietly slips by unnoticed.
