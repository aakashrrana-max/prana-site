Sports mouthguards for kids:
which sports, which type, and how to make it last.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Sports mouthguards for kids are one of the simplest ways to prevent a broken or knocked-out tooth, and they are worth it for any sport with contact, collisions, flying balls or falls. A guard only protects if your child actually keeps it in, so fit and comfort matter as much as thickness. Here is how to choose one, how braces and aligners change the picture, and what to do if a tooth gets hurt anyway.

Short answer: yes, if your child plays a contact sport or one with balls, sticks or falls. Stock guards are cheapest but fit loosely, boil-and-bite guards fit better, and custom guards made from a scan fit most comfortably. Kids with braces need a guard designed for them. Replace guards as kids grow or when they are chewed or loose.

Which sports call for a mouthguard

Dental groups recommend a mouthguard for sports where the face can meet a ball, a stick, the ground or another player. In Miami that covers a lot of what kids actually play:

  • Contact and collision sports: football and flag football, soccer, basketball, lacrosse, rugby, ice and roller hockey
  • Combat sports: boxing, martial arts, wrestling and jiu-jitsu
  • Ball and bat sports: baseball and softball, especially for infielders and batters
  • Falls and boards: skateboarding, BMX, inline skating, gymnastics and cheer stunting; surfing, where a board can hit the face
  • Lower risk, still worth considering: volleyball, tennis and pickleball for kids who dive or play close to the net

Age is less important than activity. If a younger child plays contact sports, a guard still protects baby teeth and the adult teeth forming underneath. Teams and leagues sometimes require one; even when they do not, practices cause plenty of injuries.

Whichever type you buy, check the fit at home. A good guard covers the upper teeth back to the molars, stays in place when your child opens their mouth without biting down, does not make them gag, and lets them speak and drink water in short sentences. If they keep pulling it out on the field, it does not fit well enough to protect them.

Braces, aligners and mouthguards

Kids in braces need protection just as much, maybe more, because a hit can push the lips into brackets and cut them. A regular boil-and-bite guard molded tightly over brackets can grab onto them and be hard to remove, and it stops fitting as the teeth move. Orthodontic mouthguards are shaped with extra room for brackets and wires and are usually worn on the upper teeth. Ask your child's orthodontist which they prefer.

Clear aligners are not mouthguards. They are thin and are not designed to absorb a blow. For games and practices, the aligners usually come out and go into their case, a sports guard goes in, and the aligners go back in after a rinse. If your teen is in Invisalign, count the time out of the mouth toward the daily wear goal.

Care, storage and when to replace it

  • Rinse it with cool water after every use and brush it gently with a soft toothbrush; hot water can warp it.
  • Let it dry, then store it in a vented case. Never leave it in a hot car or gym bag in the sun: Miami heat can bend it out of shape in an afternoon.
  • Wash the case regularly too.
  • Don't share guards between siblings or teammates.
  • Replace it when it is torn, chewed through, thinner where the teeth bite, or loose, and check the fit each new season.
  • Growing kids often need a new guard every season or so, and sooner when teeth are falling out and coming in.

Bring the guard to checkups. It takes only a moment to see whether it still fits and whether it shows signs of heavy clenching. Sports guards are different from the guards made for nighttime grinding; our night guards page explains the difference.

If a tooth is injured anyway

Mouthguards reduce injuries but cannot prevent every one. If a permanent (adult) tooth is knocked out, time matters: pick it up by the crown, keep it moist and get to a dentist right away. Our step-by-step guide on a knocked-out tooth in the first 30 minutes walks you through it. A knocked-out baby tooth is a different situation and should not be put back in; call the dentist for advice.

For a chipped or cracked tooth, save any pieces in milk or saliva and call; see cracked or chipped tooth. Any blow to the head with confusion, vomiting, drowsiness or a headache that gets worse needs a doctor or the ER first, before the teeth.

Kids' sports mouthguards: common questions

At what age should a child wear a mouthguard for sports?

As soon as they play a sport with contact, falls or flying balls, regardless of age. Guards come in youth sizes for younger kids. The main thing is choosing one that fits well enough that they keep it in.

Is a boil-and-bite mouthguard good enough for kids?

For many kids it is a reasonable choice, especially when fitted carefully at home. It protects better than a loose stock guard. Custom guards usually fit more comfortably and evenly, which can make a difference for kids who play a lot or wear braces.

Can my child wear a mouthguard with braces?

Yes, and they should for contact sports. Choose an orthodontic guard designed with room for brackets rather than molding a boil-and-bite guard tightly over them. Ask the orthodontist which type they recommend.

How often should kids replace their mouthguard?

Check it at the start of each season and replace it when it is torn, chewed, thin or loose. Growing kids, especially those losing baby teeth, often need a new one every season or so.

Do mouthguards prevent concussions?

Mouthguards are designed to protect the teeth, lips and tongue. They are not considered a reliable way to prevent concussions. Follow your league's helmet and concussion rules, and see a doctor after any significant head injury.
