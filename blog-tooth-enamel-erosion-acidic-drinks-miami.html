Tooth enamel erosion from acidic drinks,
and the small habits that slow it down.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Tooth enamel erosion is the slow loss of enamel to acid that doesn't come from bacteria: soda, sports drinks, citrus, lemon water, wine, and stomach acid from reflux or vomiting. It is different from a cavity, and it often goes unnoticed until teeth look thin at the edges or start to feel sensitive. Enamel doesn't grow back, but you can slow erosion a lot with how and when you drink, not only what.

Short answer: acidic drinks soften the surface of enamel, and brushing or grinding then wears it away. Sipping all day is harder on teeth than drinking the same amount with a meal. Use a straw, rinse with water, wait before brushing, and use fluoride toothpaste. Early erosion is managed with habits and fluoride; deeper wear may need bonding, crowns or veneers.

Erosion vs decay: two different problems

A cavity is caused by bacteria. They feed on sugar and make acid right where they sit, usually in grooves, between teeth and along the gumline, and the damage is a local hole. Erosion is caused by acid that arrives from outside the tooth, or from the stomach, and washes over whole surfaces. No bacteria are involved, so even a perfectly clean mouth can erode.

That difference matters, because the fixes are different. Cutting sugar helps prevent cavities, but a sugar-free diet soda is still acidic. A diet full of lemon water and sparkling drinks can be low in sugar and still wear enamel. Many people have a bit of both problems, and the exam is where they get sorted out.

The usual sources of acid

  • Soda, regular and diet. The acid is the issue here, not only the sugar.
  • Sports and energy drinks. Often both acidic and sugary, and often sipped slowly during a workout or a long shift.
  • Citrus and lemon water. A glass of orange juice with breakfast is one thing. Lemon water sipped from a bottle from morning to evening is another.
  • Flavored sparkling water. Plain sparkling water is only mildly acidic and is generally considered a far better choice than soda. Many flavored versions add citric acid, which makes them harsher.
  • Wine, kombucha, vinegar shots and sour candy. Common, easy to overlook, and all acidic.
  • Reflux and vomiting. Stomach acid is strong. Frequent heartburn, morning sickness in pregnancy and repeated vomiting for any reason can erode the inner surfaces of the teeth. If this applies to you, it is worth telling both your dentist and your physician, without embarrassment.

Why sipping all day in Miami heat is the real risk

Each acidic sip softens the surface of the enamel for a while. Saliva then buffers the acid and helps the surface recover, but that takes time. Drink a soda with lunch and your teeth get one acid hit and a long recovery. Sip the same can over two hours and they get a steady stream of acid with no break.

Miami makes that pattern easy. In the heat, people carry a drink everywhere: a sports drink on the tennis court, lemon water at the desk, a flavored seltzer on the beach, a sweet iced coffee in the car. Being a little dehydrated also means less saliva to neutralize the acid. Swapping the all-day bottle for plain water, and saving the acidic drink for a meal, protects teeth more than any toothpaste can.

Habits that protect your enamel

  • Drink acidic drinks with a meal, and finish them in one go instead of nursing them.
  • Use a straw positioned toward the back of the mouth, so less liquid washes over the front teeth.
  • Rinse with plain water afterward, or follow with a little milk or a piece of cheese.
  • Wait before brushing. Brushing softened enamel can scrub it away, so wait at least 30 minutes, and many dentists suggest closer to an hour. The same goes after reflux or vomiting: rinse with water, then wait.
  • Use a fluoride toothpaste and a soft brush with light pressure. Your dentist may suggest a higher-fluoride paste if erosion is active.
  • Chew sugar-free gum after meals to bring up saliva.
  • Don't swish acidic drinks or hold them in your mouth.
  • If you clench or grind, mention it. Softened enamel wears down faster under grinding, and a night guard can help protect it.

Signs of erosion you can spot yourself

  • Translucent edges on the front teeth, especially visible when you hold them against a dark background.
  • Cupping: small scooped-out dips on the chewing surfaces of the back teeth.
  • Yellowing, as thinner enamel lets the darker dentin underneath show through.
  • Sensitivity to cold, sweet or acidic foods.
  • Fillings that seem to stand up above the tooth around them, because the enamel next to them has worn away.
  • A smooth, glassy look with fewer of the natural ridges teeth usually have.

Repair options, from simple to bigger

Lost enamel doesn't regrow, but erosion can often be stopped or slowed, and worn areas can be rebuilt. At Prana Dental, Dr. Rana starts by finding the source: drink habits, reflux, grinding or a dry mouth from medication. Comparing 3D scans over time can help show whether wear is still progressing. From there, treatment depends on how far it has gone:

  • Early erosion: habit changes, fluoride varnish and a fluoride toothpaste, with monitoring at checkups.
  • Sensitive or thinned spots: tooth-colored bonding can cover exposed dentin and rebuild worn edges.
  • Larger or deeper wear: onlays or crowns rebuild back teeth; veneers can restore worn front teeth once the cause is under control.
  • Reflux: treated by your physician; dental repairs last longer once the acid is controlled.

Enamel erosion questions

Is sparkling water bad for your teeth?

Plain sparkling water is only mildly acidic and is generally considered a much better choice than soda or juice. Flavored versions with added citric acid are harsher. Drinking it with meals rather than sipping all day lowers the risk further.

Does lemon water damage your teeth?

It can, mainly when it is sipped throughout the day. Drink it in one sitting, use a straw, rinse with plain water afterward and wait before brushing. Your dentist can tell you whether you show signs of erosion.

Can enamel grow back?

No. Once enamel is lost, the body can't rebuild it. Fluoride and saliva can harden softened enamel surfaces, and a dentist can cover worn areas with bonding, onlays, crowns or veneers.

Should I brush my teeth right after drinking something acidic?

It is better to wait. Acid softens the enamel surface for a while, and brushing straight away can wear it down. Rinse with water and wait at least 30 minutes, and ideally closer to an hour, before you brush.

Is tooth erosion the same as a cavity?

No. Cavities are caused by bacteria feeding on sugar in specific spots, while erosion comes from acid in drinks, food or the stomach and wears down broad surfaces. Many people have some of both, which a dental exam can sort out.
