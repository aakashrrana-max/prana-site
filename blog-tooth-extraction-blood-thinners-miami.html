Tooth extraction on blood thinners?
Keep taking it, and let us plan around it.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

A tooth extraction on blood thinners is usually done safely without stopping the medicine, because bleeding from a tooth socket can be controlled right where it happens, while stopping a blood thinner can raise your risk of a stroke or clot. The key is planning: telling your dentist about every medicine and supplement, coordinating with the doctor who prescribes it, and using local measures to control bleeding during and after the extraction. Here is how that usually works.

Short answer: never stop or skip a prescribed blood thinner or antiplatelet on your own before dental work. For many routine extractions, people keep taking their medicine and bleeding is controlled with pressure, stitches and dressings in the socket. Any change to your dose or timing is decided by your prescribing physician. Complex cases may be referred to an oral surgeon.

Why you shouldn't stop it yourself

Blood thinners and antiplatelet medicines are prescribed for serious reasons: atrial fibrillation, a past clot in the legs or lungs, an artificial heart valve, a heart stent, a previous stroke or heart attack. A few days without them can let a dangerous clot form. A tooth socket, on the other hand, is small, easy to reach and easy to press on.

That is why, for many simple extractions, the common approach today is to continue the medicine and manage bleeding locally. Whether that applies to you depends on the medicine, the dose, your other conditions and how many teeth are coming out. If any adjustment is needed, such as the timing of a dose on the day of the extraction, that call belongs to the physician who prescribed it, not to you or the dentist alone.

Tell us about every medicine and supplement

Bring a written list with doses, or the bottles themselves. These are the groups that matter most for bleeding:

  • Anticoagulants: warfarin, the newer oral anticoagulants such as apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran and edoxaban, and injectable heparins.
  • Antiplatelets: daily low-dose aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel and ticagrelor. Two antiplatelets together, common after a stent, need extra care, and stopping them early can be especially risky.
  • Over-the-counter pain relievers: anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofen and naproxen can add to bleeding, particularly on top of a blood thinner.
  • Supplements: fish oil, vitamin E, ginkgo, garlic pills and turmeric may affect bleeding for some people. Mention them even if they seem harmless.
  • Health conditions: liver or kidney disease, a low platelet count or a known bleeding disorder change the plan, too.

How we plan it with your physician

At the first visit, Dr. Rana takes X-rays, looks at how the tooth is likely to come out and goes over your medicine list with you. Then the planning usually looks like this:

  • We contact your prescribing physician or cardiologist, with your permission, to share what is planned and ask whether anything about your medicine should change.
  • If you take warfarin, we need a recent INR (the blood test that measures how thin your blood is), usually taken close to the day of the extraction. If the number is higher than expected, the extraction may be postponed.
  • We often limit how many teeth come out in one visit, so the bleeding area stays small.
  • We usually suggest a morning appointment, earlier in the week. That leaves the rest of the day to watch the area and gives you time to reach us, since we are closed on Sunday.
  • You get written aftercare instructions and a clear plan for what to do if bleeding starts again at home.

How bleeding is controlled in the chair

Most of the work happens before you leave the office. Several local measures are commonly combined:

  • A gentle technique that disturbs as little gum and bone as possible
  • A dissolvable dressing placed in the socket to help a clot form and hold
  • Stitches to pull the gum edges together over the socket
  • Firm pressure on gauze while you rest in the chair
  • In some cases, a prescription mouthwash that helps stabilize the clot, used exactly as directed

We check that bleeding is under control before you go home. Local anesthetic keeps you comfortable during the extraction, and oral sedation is available for nervous patients, though it adds to the list of things we coordinate. Our tooth extraction page walks through the visit step by step.

At home: the first day

Some oozing and pink-tinged saliva for the first day is normal, and a bruise on the cheek can appear in people on blood thinners. To keep the clot in place:

  • Bite firmly on fresh gauze for 30 to 45 minutes after you get home. Don't chew on it or keep changing it.
  • If oozing continues, press a damp black tea bag on the socket for 20 to 30 minutes. The tannins in tea can help a clot form.
  • Keep your head raised, rest, and skip exercise and alcohol for the day.
  • For the first 24 hours, don't rinse, spit or use a straw, and avoid hot drinks. Our guide to preventing dry socket explains why the clot matters.
  • Ask your physician or pharmacist which pain reliever is safe with your medicine before taking one. Anti-inflammatories may add to bleeding.
  • Keep taking your blood thinner as your physician directed.

Call us if bleeding keeps going after two rounds of firm pressure. Go to the ER if bleeding is heavy and won't stop, if you feel faint or dizzy, or if you have trouble breathing or swallowing.

When an oral surgeon is the better choice

Dr. Rana is a general dentist, and many extractions for people on blood thinners can be done in the office. We refer you to an oral surgeon when the extraction is surgical or the tooth is impacted, when several teeth need to come out at once, when you have a bleeding disorder or significant liver disease, when your INR is unusually high or hard to control, or when you take more than one medicine that affects bleeding. Impacted wisdom teeth often fall into that group. If we refer you, we send your X-rays and medicine list along, so the oral surgeon starts with the full picture.

Extractions and blood thinners: common questions

Do I need to stop blood thinners before a tooth extraction?

Usually not, and you should never stop on your own. For many simple extractions, the medicine is continued and bleeding is controlled locally with pressure, stitches and dressings. If any change is needed, your prescribing physician decides it.

Can you get a tooth pulled while taking aspirin every day?

In most cases, yes. Daily low-dose aspirin is usually continued for a routine extraction, because stopping it can raise the risk of a heart attack or stroke. Tell your dentist you take it so bleeding control can be planned.

How long will I bleed after an extraction on blood thinners?

Oozing and pink saliva often last most of the first day and can be a little longer than usual. Steady, heavy bleeding that doesn't respond to firm pressure on gauze is not normal and needs a call to the dentist, or the ER if it is severe.

What INR is safe for a tooth extraction?

There isn't a single number for everyone. Many simple extractions can be done when the INR is within the usual therapeutic range, but your dentist and physician decide together based on the procedure and your health. A recent test, close to the day of the extraction, is usually needed.

What pain reliever can I take after an extraction if I'm on blood thinners?

Ask your physician or pharmacist before taking anything. Anti-inflammatory pain relievers can increase bleeding when combined with blood thinners, so a different option is often suggested. Follow the label and the advice you are given.
