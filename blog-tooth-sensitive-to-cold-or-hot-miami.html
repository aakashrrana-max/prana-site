Tooth sensitive to cold or hot?
What the kind of pain is telling you.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

A tooth sensitive to cold or hot is reacting because something has let temperature reach the nerve more easily than it should, and how long the pain lasts matters more than how sharp it is. A quick twinge from ice water that fades in seconds usually points to exposed root surface or worn enamel. Pain from hot coffee, or any pain that lingers after the drink is gone, is more likely to mean the nerve itself is inflamed. Here is how to read the signs, how to use sensitivity toothpaste properly and what a dentist can do.

Short answer: brief cold sensitivity is common and often improves with a sensitivity toothpaste and gentler brushing, though a cavity or crack should be ruled out. Sensitivity to heat, pain that lingers for more than about 30 seconds, or pain that starts on its own suggests a nerve problem and should be checked within a few days, sooner if it is getting worse.

Why teeth feel temperature at all

Under the enamel and the gum line lies dentin, a layer full of microscopic tubes that lead toward the nerve in the center of the tooth. Healthy enamel and gum cover the dentin, so temperature barely registers. When dentin is exposed, cold makes the fluid inside those tubes shift, and the nerve reads that movement as a sharp jolt.

That is why ordinary sensitivity is fast and short: the jolt stops once the cold is gone. A nerve that is itself inflamed behaves differently. It overreacts, it keeps aching after the trigger is removed, and it may start to react to heat as well. Before you call, notice these details:

  • Is it one tooth or a whole area? Several teeth along the gum line suggest exposed roots or worn enamel; one tooth suggests a cavity, a crack or a nerve problem.
  • What sets it off: cold, heat, sweets, air when you breathe in, or brushing?
  • How long does it last after the trigger is gone: a second or two, or half a minute and more?
  • Does it ever hurt with no trigger at all, or wake you up?
  • Did it start after a new filling, a crown, a deep cleaning or whitening?

Common reasons a tooth reacts to cold

Exposed roots

Roots have no enamel, only a thin outer layer that wears away easily. When gums pull back from hard brushing, gum disease or simply age, the root is left open to cold drinks and cold air. Our post on receding gums explains why it happens.

Worn enamel

Acid softens enamel, and brushing soon after can wear it faster. Frequent citrus, lime in drinks, sodas, sports drinks during long beach days and acid reflux all add up. Grinding at night wears the biting edges too.

A cavity, a leaking filling or a crack

Decay or a gap under an old filling lets cold and sugar reach the dentin directly. A cracked tooth often gives a sharp twinge with cold on one tooth only, sometimes with pain on biting.

Recent dental work

Mild cold sensitivity for days to a few weeks after a filling, crown, deep cleaning or whitening is common and usually fades steadily. If it is getting worse rather than better, call.

When heat points to a nerve problem

Heat is the more worrying trigger. When the pulp, the soft tissue inside the tooth, is badly inflamed or starting to die, warmth can set off a deep, throbbing ache that builds for a while instead of fading. Some people notice that their morning cafecito sets off pain that lasts long after the cup is empty.

A classic sign is a tooth that hurts with heat and feels better with cold water, to the point that people walk around sipping ice water to keep the pain down. That pattern usually means the nerve won't recover on its own, and the tooth will typically need a root canal or, if it can't be saved, an extraction.

Be wary, too, of severe sensitivity that suddenly stops. The nerve may have died, which is not the same as healing, and an infection can follow. If you also have swelling, a pimple on the gum, fever, or trouble swallowing or breathing, see the warning signs on our toothache treatment page; spreading swelling, fever or trouble breathing or swallowing means the ER.

Using sensitivity toothpaste the right way

Desensitizing toothpastes work by calming the nerve's response or by plugging the open dentin tubes. Common active ingredients include potassium nitrate and stannous fluoride. They work well for many people with mild, brief cold sensitivity, but only with steady use:

  • Brush with it twice a day, every day, with a soft brush and light pressure.
  • Spit out the extra, but don't rinse with water afterward, so the ingredients stay on the teeth.
  • At night, you can dab a little on the sensitive spot with a clean finger and leave it there.
  • Give it two to four weeks before deciding whether it helps.
  • After acidic food or drinks, rinse with water and wait a while before brushing, roughly half an hour or more.
  • If one tooth is still sensitive after a month, see a dentist. Toothpaste can mask a cavity or a crack without fixing it.

What a dentist can do

Treatment depends on what the exam finds. Dr. Rana tests the tooth with cold, checks the gums, looks for cracks and takes X-rays when needed, then explains the options with prices in writing before anything starts.

Tooth sensitivity: common questions

Why is my tooth suddenly sensitive to cold?

Sudden cold sensitivity often comes from a newly exposed root, a small cavity, a filling that has started to leak, or a crack. Sometimes it follows whitening or a recent cleaning and fades on its own. If it is limited to one tooth or lasts more than a few weeks, have it checked.

Is sensitivity to hot worse than sensitivity to cold?

Often, yes. Brief cold sensitivity is usually about exposed dentin, while pain from heat, especially pain that lingers or is relieved by cold water, more often means the nerve inside the tooth is inflamed. That deserves a dental visit within a few days.

How long does sensitivity toothpaste take to work?

Many people notice a difference within a couple of weeks of brushing with it twice a day, but give it two to four weeks. Don't rinse with water after brushing. If one tooth still hurts after that, see a dentist.

Can a sensitive tooth heal on its own?

Mild sensitivity after a filling, whitening or a cleaning often settles by itself. Sensitivity from a cavity, a crack or an inflamed nerve does not go away permanently without treatment, even if it quiets down for a while.

Does a tooth sensitive to hot always need a root canal?

Not always, but heat sensitivity that lingers is one of the more common signs that a root canal may be needed. Testing and X-rays show whether the nerve can recover, and the dentist explains the options before deciding.
