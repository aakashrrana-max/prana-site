Water flosser vs string floss,
and how to pick the one you will use.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Water flosser vs string floss is less of a contest than the ads suggest: both clean between teeth, and the right one is the one you will actually use every day. String floss scrapes plaque off the side of each tooth, while a water flosser flushes food and breaks up plaque with a pulsing stream. For people with braces, aligner attachments, bridges or implants, the water flosser often wins on ease. Here is how to choose, and how to use each one properly.

Short answer: string floss is cheap, portable and very good at wiping plaque out of tight contacts. A water flosser is easier for people with braces, bridges, implants, gum pockets or stiff hands. Interdental brushes are a strong third option where the spaces are wider. Doing it daily matters more than which tool you pick.

What each tool actually does between your teeth

Plaque is a sticky film of bacteria, not loose crumbs, so whatever you use between teeth needs some mechanical action to lift it. That is where the two tools part ways.

String floss works by contact. Curved against one tooth and then its neighbor, it wipes the film off each side wall and slips just under the gumline. It is very good in tight contacts, which is where many cavities between teeth start. Its weak points are technique (lots of people snap it straight down and saw back and forth) and access around wires, bridges and implants.

A water flosser works by flushing. Sometimes called an oral irrigator, it pulses a thin jet of water between teeth and along the gumline. The pulsing knocks loose soft plaque and trapped food, and it can reach a little way into gum pockets and under a fixed bridge where floss struggles. It is less effective at cleaning a very tight contact, because water flows around that spot rather than scraping it.

Used every day, either one can cut down on plaque and on gums that bleed. Neither replaces brushing, and neither replaces a professional teeth cleaning: once plaque hardens into tartar, only scaling instruments in the dental chair take it off.

Who benefits most from a water flosser

For a healthy mouth with no dental work in the way, string floss is usually enough. A water flosser earns its counter space in a few specific situations:

  • Braces or aligner attachments. Brackets, wires and the small tooth-colored bumps used with Invisalign catch food all day. Threading floss under a wire every night is slow, and many teens quietly give up. A water jet run along the brackets clears much of that in a couple of minutes.
  • Bridges. The false tooth in a bridge is joined to the teeth beside it, so floss can't drop down between them. You either thread floss underneath with a threader or flush the space with water. Our dental bridges page explains why that hidden spot needs daily attention.
  • Implants and fixed full-arch teeth. Gum around an implant can get inflamed much like gum around a natural tooth. A water flosser on a moderate setting is a gentle way to clean around implant crowns and under a fixed arch, alongside any floss or brushes your dentist shows you.
  • Arthritis, tremor or limited reach. Steering floss to the back molars is hard with sore joints. A handle and a button are far easier, and floss picks are a workable middle ground.
  • Gum pockets after treatment. After care for gum disease, some pockets stay a bit deeper than ideal. Irrigation can help keep them cleaner between maintenance visits, though it never replaces those visits.

How to use string floss so it does its job

Gums that bleed when you floss are often gums that rarely get flossed, and daily gentle cleaning usually calms them within a week or two. The technique, step by step:

  • Pull off roughly 18 inches. Wind most of it around one middle finger and hold a short working length between your thumbs and index fingers.
  • Ease it through the contact with a gentle side-to-side rocking motion. Don't let it snap down onto the gum.
  • Curve it into a C shape hugging one tooth, then slide it up and down a few times, just under the gumline.
  • Curve it the opposite way against the neighboring tooth and repeat. Every gap has two walls to clean.
  • Unwind a fresh section as you move along, and remember the back surface of the last molar on each side.

Waxed floss or flat tape glides more easily if regular floss shreds. Shredding in the same gap every time can point to a rough filling edge or a cavity, so mention which spot it is at your next visit.

How to use a water flosser without soaking the bathroom

  • Fill the reservoir with lukewarm water. Cold water can sting teeth that are already sensitive.
  • Start on the lowest pressure setting and step it up over a week or two as your gums adjust.
  • Lean over the sink and put the tip in your mouth before switching it on. Keep your lips loosely closed so the water drains down into the sink instead of across the mirror.
  • Aim the tip at the gumline, roughly at a right angle to the teeth, and pause for a second or two at each gap, outside and inside.
  • Follow the same route every time (upper outside, upper inside, lower outside, lower inside) so no area gets skipped.
  • Empty the reservoir afterward, let the parts air dry, and change the tip as often as the instructions say.

Plain water does the job. If you want to add mouthwash to the tank, check the device instructions first, since some ingredients can damage the parts.

Interdental brushes, and all three side by side

Interdental brushes look like tiny bottle brushes on a handle. They push into the space between two teeth and scrub both walls at once. Where a gap is open enough to fit one, they often clean more thoroughly than floss, because the bristles fill the whole space.

They suit people with gum recession, the open spaces next to a bridge or implant, and anyone who finds floss fiddly. The right size fits snugly without forcing; too small barely touches the teeth, too big can scrape the gum. Many people use two or three sizes in different parts of the mouth.

What we look for at your cleaning

Your gums are a progress report on home care. Bleeding points and tartar between particular teeth usually show exactly which gaps are being missed. At a cleaning, Dr. Rana and the hygiene team can show you on your own teeth where to angle the floss or the water tip and which brush sizes fit your spaces.

Flossing questions people ask

Is a water flosser as good as flossing?

For many people it is a reasonable alternative, especially around braces, bridges and implants. String floss is better at wiping plaque from tight contacts, while a water flosser is better at flushing areas that floss can't reach easily. If you use only a water flosser, ask at your next cleaning whether any tight spots still need floss.

Can a water flosser damage your gums?

On a sensible setting it is gentle for most people. Jumping straight to full pressure can leave gums sore, so start low and increase slowly. If your gums hurt or bleed more after a couple of weeks of use, turn it down and have them checked.

Do I need a water flosser if I wear Invisalign?

You don't need one, but it helps. Attachments and the edges of the trays catch food, and anything left on your teeth sits under the plastic for hours. Brushing and flossing before the trays go back in matters most, and a water flosser is a handy extra.

Why do my gums bleed when I floss?

Usually because the gums are inflamed from plaque that has been left in place. With gentle daily flossing, the bleeding often settles within a week or two. Bleeding that keeps going, or comes with swelling, bad breath or loose teeth, is a reason to see a dentist.
