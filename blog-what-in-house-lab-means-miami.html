what "in-house lab" actually means and why it changes pricing

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated September 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: "In-house lab" means the dental practice itself designs and manufactures restorations, rather than paying an outside company to do it. Cutting out that middleman fee is a direct reason Prana can offer crowns and veneers from $999.

"In-house lab" is a phrase you'll see on a lot of dental websites, but it's worth knowing exactly what it means and why it matters for what you pay, rather than taking it as a vague marketing phrase. It's not just a nice-sounding feature — it's a real, structural difference in the cost behind your crown or veneer, and understanding it can help you evaluate any dental quote more clearly, whether it comes from Prana or anywhere else you're considering for treatment.

The plain definition

An in-house lab means the dental practice itself owns the equipment and employs the people who design and manufacture restorations like crowns, veneers, and full-arch prosthetics — as opposed to sending that work to a separate, independently owned dental laboratory business. It's the difference between a practice doing the manufacturing itself versus outsourcing it to a vendor down the supply chain that the patient never interacts with or even knows about. Both models exist widely; the in-house model is simply less common because it requires the practice to invest in its own scanning, design, and milling equipment, plus the staff training needed to operate it well and consistently, and the ongoing cost of keeping that equipment current as the technology improves. That upfront investment is exactly why fewer practices choose to build it out themselves.

How the traditional cost structure works

When a practice outsources to an external lab, that lab charges a fee for designing and fabricating each restoration — a cost that's separate from the practice's own overhead and gets built into what you're charged as the patient. It's an entire second business, with its own costs and profit margin, sitting between your exam and your finished crown. That fee exists regardless of how efficient or affordable the dental practice itself tries to be, because it's a cost passed through from a company the practice doesn't control and generally can't negotiate away no matter how many cases it sends there over the course of a year. That fee is simply a fixed part of the outsourced model, built in before your appointment ever begins.

How removing that step changes the math

When the practice designs and mills the restoration itself, that separate lab fee simply isn't part of the cost anymore. There's no outside vendor's markup layered on top of the practice's own costs, and no separate outside business taking its own cut of every single case. That's a direct, structural reason porcelain crowns and porcelain veneers can be priced at $999 each at Prana — not a temporary discount, but a reflection of a genuinely different cost structure that holds up over time rather than fluctuating with an outside vendor's own pricing decisions from one year to the next. It's a structural saving, not a promotional rate that can quietly change later.

The side benefits that come with it

Beyond cost, having the lab in-house also means faster turnaround (often same-day for many crown cases), tighter quality control since the same team designs and checks the fit, and a straightforward line of accountability if an adjustment is needed. It's also part of why implant and full-arch work at Prana carries a 5-year workmanship guarantee — there's no separate outside company to point to if something needs revisiting later on, since the same team handled every part of it, from the original design to the finished restoration.

Why this term gets used loosely on some websites

Because "in-house lab" sounds appealing, it's occasionally used loosely to describe a practice that simply handles impressions or minor adjustments on-site while still outsourcing the actual design and milling to a separate company. That's worth distinguishing from a practice that genuinely designs and manufactures the restoration itself from start to finish. If a practice advertises an in-house lab, it's fair to ask specifically what happens on-site versus what still gets sent out to a vendor, since the two claims aren't the same thing at all, even though on the surface they sound almost identical to a patient who isn't asking closely.

What this means when you're comparing providers

If you're comparing quotes between practices, it's worth asking directly whether crowns, veneers, or full-arch restorations are made in-house or sent to an outside lab. That single answer explains a meaningful part of why prices differ from one office to another, and it's a fair, specific question to ask before committing to treatment at your free consult, whichever practice you end up choosing for your treatment.
