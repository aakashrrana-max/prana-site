White spots on teeth,
what they are and what fixes them.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

White spots on teeth usually come from one of three things: early decay that has pulled minerals out of the enamel, fluorosis from extra fluoride while teeth were forming, or enamel that developed thinner or softer than normal. They can look alike in a mirror, but they behave very differently. Some can harden and fade with fluoride, while others need a cosmetic fix. Here is how dentists tell them apart and what each option does.

Short answer: chalky spots near the gumline, often after braces, are usually early decay and can often be stopped and partly reversed with fluoride. Fluorosis and developmental spots don't remineralize, but they can be treated with resin infiltration, microabrasion, bonding or veneers. Whitening can make spots look more obvious at first, so get them diagnosed before you whiten.

A quick tip: if spots show up in the morning and are gone by lunch, dryness is the likely explanation. Spots that are there every day, in the same place, need a proper look.

White spots after braces

This is the most common reason people notice white spots as teens or young adults. Brackets make cleaning harder, plaque sits around their edges for months, and the acid it produces pulls minerals out of the enamel in a ring or square around where each bracket was. When the braces come off, the outline is suddenly visible.

These spots are early decay, not just a stain. The good news is that the surface is often still intact, so with careful cleaning and fluoride the area can harden again, and some spots fade noticeably over the following months. The surface layer sometimes remineralizes faster than the deeper part, which can leave a faint mark even when the tooth is no longer at risk.

Clear aligners can also lead to spots if sugary drinks are sipped with the trays in, since the plastic holds the liquid against the teeth. Brushing before putting trays back in matters.

Which spots can heal on their own, with help

Only the decay type can remineralize, and only while it is still an early surface lesion. Saliva and fluoride bring minerals back into the enamel, especially if the plaque that caused the problem is cleaned off every day. What helps:

  • Brushing twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste, paying attention to the gumline
  • Fluoride varnish at dental visits, and sometimes a prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste for a while
  • Cutting down how often you have sugary or acidic snacks and drinks, not only how much
  • Regular checkups, so the dentist can confirm the spots are getting harder or at least not growing

Fluorosis and developmental spots don't respond to this, because nothing is being lost. They are cosmetic, and they are only treated if they bother you. Our post on fluoride for kids explains how to prevent fluorosis in young children.

Cosmetic options, from gentlest to most involved

Resin infiltration

A thin, runny resin is drawn into the porous enamel of the spot and hardened with a light. Because the resin fills the tiny gaps that make the spot look white, the area often blends much better with the tooth. There is usually no drilling or numbing. It suits many early decay spots after braces and some mild developmental spots.

Microabrasion

A mild acid paste is gently polished over the spot to remove a very thin outer layer of enamel. It suits cases where the white mark sits right at the surface, as in some mild fluorosis. It isn't suitable for deep spots, because only a small amount of enamel can be removed safely.

Bonding

For deeper or rough spots, a small amount of the affected enamel is removed and replaced with tooth-colored resin matched to the tooth. Our dental bonding page walks through that visit.

Porcelain veneers

When spots cover several front teeth, or are combined with other things you want to change, porcelain veneers can cover the fronts of the teeth. They start from $999 per tooth at Prana and are a bigger, usually irreversible step, so they are rarely the first answer for spots alone.

Whitening and white spots

Whitening is often people's first idea, and it can backfire at first. Whitening gel dries the teeth out for a day or two, which makes white spots stand out more. As the teeth rehydrate and the rest of the tooth lightens, spots often blend in better, but they rarely disappear completely.

That is why the order matters. Treat any active decay first, then decide whether to combine professional whitening with resin infiltration or bonding. When bonding is part of the plan, whitening usually comes first, and the bonding is matched to the new shade after it settles.

Getting the spots diagnosed

A dentist tells these types apart by looking at the pattern, drying the tooth gently, feeling the surface and asking about your history, such as braces, childhood fluoride and injuries to baby teeth. White spot questions

Why do I have white spots on my teeth?

The most common reasons are early decay from plaque, fluorosis from extra fluoride in early childhood, and enamel that formed thinner or softer than normal. Temporary white patches can also appear when teeth dry out overnight. A dental exam tells these apart.

Do white spots on teeth go away?

Spots from temporary dryness fade within hours. Early decay spots can harden and fade partly with fluoride and good cleaning. Fluorosis and developmental spots don't go away on their own, but they can be treated cosmetically.

Can whitening remove white spots?

Usually not on its own. Whitening can make spots look more obvious for a day or two, then they often blend better as the rest of the tooth lightens. Many people combine whitening with resin infiltration or bonding.

Are white spots on teeth a cavity?

Some are. Chalky spots near the gumline or around where braces brackets sat are often early decay, which can usually be stopped before it becomes a hole. Other spots, like fluorosis, are not decay at all.

How do you fix white spots after braces?

First by stopping the decay process with careful cleaning and fluoride, and waiting a few months to see how much fades. Spots that remain can often be treated with resin infiltration, microabrasion or bonding.
