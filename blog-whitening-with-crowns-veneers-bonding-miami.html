Can you whiten crowns, veneers or bonding?
No, so plan the order instead.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

You can't whiten crowns, veneers, bonding or tooth-colored fillings: whitening gel lightens natural enamel and dentin, and those materials keep the shade they were made in. That doesn't mean whitening is off the table if you have dental work. It means the order of steps, and which teeth are visible when you smile, decide whether the result looks even. Here is how to plan it, case by case.

Short answer: whitening changes your natural teeth only. If you have visible crowns, veneers or bonding, whiten first, let the shade settle, then match or replace the restorations to the new color. If old work already doesn't match, it can often be polished, rebonded or replaced. Restorations on back teeth that don't show usually don't matter.

Why the gel doesn't change dental work

Peroxide whitening works by passing into the tooth and breaking down stain molecules inside the enamel and dentin. Porcelain and zirconia are glazed ceramics, and composite resin is a plastic-based material. Neither has that internal structure, so the gel has nothing to lighten.

Whitening, along with a professional polish, can remove some surface stain from the outside of a restoration, which is why old bonding sometimes looks slightly fresher afterward. The underlying shade stays the same. So the more your natural teeth lighten, the more any front restoration that used to match may start to look darker by comparison.

Why timing matters: whiten, wait, then match

Right after whitening, teeth are slightly dehydrated and look lighter than they will a week later. Matching a new crown or bonding to that temporary shade means it can end up looking too bright once the color settles. Most dentists wait about two weeks before taking a final shade. Waiting also helps bonding, since freshly whitened enamel doesn't hold bonding material as well for a short while.

The step people forget is the one after matching. Natural teeth slowly pick up stain again from coffee, tea and red wine, and a crown or veneer does not. If you don't keep up the whitened shade, the natural teeth drift darker and the new restoration starts to stand out in the other direction. Keeping your custom trays and doing an occasional top-up is the simple fix. Our post on how long whitening lasts covers upkeep.

When old restorations don't match

Maybe you whitened at home and now an old filling stands out, or your front bonding has yellowed over the years. Options, from least to most involved:

  • Polishing. Surface stain on composite can often be polished away in a short visit. It won't change the base shade, but it can close a small gap in color.
  • Resurfacing or replacing bonding. A thin new layer of composite, or full replacement, matched to your current shade. See dental bonding for what that visit involves.
  • Replacing a crown. A front crown that is now darker than its neighbors can be remade in a lighter shade. If it is older and its edges are showing, that may be due anyway.
  • Veneers. If several front teeth have old bonding or fillings, porcelain veneers, from $999 per tooth, can give a uniform color across the smile.
  • Leaving it. If the mismatch is only visible up close or on a back tooth, many people decide it isn't worth changing.

Store-bought kits and whitening toothpaste with dental work

Strips and over-the-counter gels follow the same rule: they lighten only natural tooth. The catch with dental work is that strips lie flat across every tooth, so a bonded edge or a crown in the middle of the strip stays the same while the enamel beside it lightens. That is how people end up with a patchy look they didn't expect.

Whitening toothpastes deserve a mention too. Most of them remove surface stain by being more abrasive, not by bleaching. Used hard and often, very abrasive pastes can dull the polish on composite bonding and make it pick up stain faster. If you have bonding or veneers on front teeth, a regular fluoride toothpaste and a soft brush are the safer daily choice, with whitening done under a dentist's eye.

Check the old work before you whiten

Whitening gel can seep into tiny gaps at the edges of old fillings and crowns, which can cause sharp sensitivity and may hide decay starting underneath. Exposed root surfaces near old crowns are often sensitive too. Whitening and dental work: common questions

Can you whiten a crown?

No. Crowns are made of porcelain, zirconia or metal-based materials that whitening gel doesn't lighten. If your natural teeth are whitened and a front crown no longer matches, the crown can be replaced in a lighter shade.

Can veneers be whitened?

Porcelain veneers keep their color and don't respond to whitening. A professional polish can remove surface stain around the edges. If you want lighter veneers, they would need to be replaced.

Will my bonding match after I whiten my teeth?

Probably not exactly, because bonding stays the same shade while your teeth lighten. Many people whiten first, wait about two weeks, then have the bonding polished or redone to match the new shade.

How long after whitening can I get a crown or veneers?

Most dentists wait about two weeks so the tooth color settles before a shade is chosen. Your dentist will confirm the timing for your case.

Is it safe to whiten if I have a lot of fillings?

Usually, yes, as long as the fillings are sealed and there is no decay. Leaking fillings can let gel reach sensitive areas, so a dental exam first is wise. Plan for any visible fillings that may need replacing afterward to match.
