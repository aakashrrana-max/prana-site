Wisdom tooth pain: is it an emergency?
How to judge a flare-up, and what to do next.

By Dr. Aakash Rana, DDS · Updated October 2026

Wisdom tooth pain is usually not an emergency by itself, but it becomes one when it comes with spreading swelling, fever, trouble swallowing or trouble opening your mouth. Most flare-ups are caused by an irritated gum flap over a tooth that is only partly through, food trapped under that flap, or decay on the tooth or its neighbor. This guide starts with the warning signs, then explains the common causes, what helps at home, and how removal is decided once the flare-up calms down.

Short answer: go to the ER now if a painful wisdom tooth comes with swelling under the jaw or toward the eye, fever, trouble swallowing or breathing, or you can barely open your mouth. A sore, swollen gum behind your last molar without those signs usually needs a dental visit within a day or so. Rinse with warm salt water, keep the area clean and use a cold compress until you are seen.

Red flags: when it means the ER

Wisdom teeth sit at the very back of the jaw, close to the throat and to spaces in the neck where an infection can spread. That is why the warning signs here matter more than for a front tooth. Go to the ER, or call 911, if you have:

  • Swelling that spreads under the jaw, down the neck or up toward the eye
  • A fever along with facial swelling
  • Trouble swallowing, drooling because swallowing hurts, or a muffled voice
  • Any trouble breathing
  • A jaw that has stiffened so much you can only open a finger's width or less, along with swelling

These signs can mean the infection has moved beyond the gum. The ER can treat a spreading infection; the tooth itself is dealt with afterward. Before we open on Monday, November 16, 2026, anyone with these signs should go to the ER rather than wait.

What is usually behind a flare-up

Pericoronitis: the gum flap gets infected

When a wisdom tooth only partly breaks through, a flap of gum often stays over part of it. Bacteria and food collect underneath, and the flap becomes swollen and sore. It can get worse quickly if the upper wisdom tooth bites down on the swollen flap each time you chew. This is the most common reason for a painful wisdom tooth in teens and young adults.

Food trapped under the flap

A small piece of food, a seed or a popcorn hull wedged under the gum can set off pain within hours. Sometimes gentle cleaning is enough to settle it, but the space tends to trap food again, so flare-ups often repeat.

Decay on the wisdom tooth or the molar in front

Wisdom teeth are hard to reach with a toothbrush, and a tilted one can leave a gap where plaque collects against the back of the second molar. A cavity there can cause pain that feels like it comes from the wisdom tooth. Catching it early matters, because the second molar is the tooth you want to keep.

A cyst, rarely

Occasionally a fluid-filled sac forms around a wisdom tooth that never came in. Cysts often cause no symptoms at all and are found on an X-ray rather than felt, and they need a careful look, often by an oral surgeon.

What helps at home for a day or two

These steps can ease a mild flare-up while you arrange a visit. They don't treat the cause, so the pain often returns if nothing else is done.

  • Rinse with warm salt water after every meal, swishing gently toward the back of the mouth.
  • Brush the area carefully with a soft brush, including the gum over the tooth. A water flosser on its lowest setting can help flush food out from under the flap.
  • Use a cold compress on the outside of the jaw, 15 to 20 minutes at a time. Avoid heat on a swollen face.
  • Take an over-the-counter pain reliever as the label directs, if it is safe for you, and never hold a tablet against the gum.
  • Stick to soft food and chew on the other side so the upper tooth doesn't keep biting the sore flap.
  • Drink plenty of water. In Miami heat, a sore mouth makes it easy to drink too little.
  • Don't take leftover antibiotics or someone else's prescription. If antibiotics are needed, a dentist or physician will prescribe the right one.

At the visit: calm it down, then plan

The first goal is to get the infection and pain under control. Dr. Rana examines the area, takes an X-ray or 3D image to see the tooth's position and roots, and usually cleans and flushes out the space under the flap. Antibiotics are prescribed only when the infection is spreading or you have general symptoms such as fever, not for every sore gum.

Once things settle, the next question is whether the tooth should come out. A wisdom tooth that has flared up once may stay quiet for years, but repeated flare-ups, decay on the neighboring molar or a tooth that can't come in properly are common reasons to remove it. Sometimes the upper wisdom tooth that keeps biting the flap is the one removed first. Removal is often planned for after the swelling has gone down, because healing tends to go more smoothly that way, and you can read how to lower the risk of a dry socket after an extraction.

Wisdom tooth pain: common questions

When should I go to the ER for wisdom tooth pain?

Go if the pain comes with swelling spreading under the jaw, down the neck or toward the eye, a fever, trouble swallowing or breathing, or a jaw that barely opens. Those signs can mean an infection is spreading. For a sore gum without them, a dentist is the right place.

Can wisdom tooth pain go away on its own?

The pain from a flare-up often settles in a few days, especially with salt water rinses and careful cleaning. The cause usually stays, though, so many people get repeated flare-ups until the tooth is treated or removed.

Why does my wisdom tooth pain spread to my ear?

The back of the jaw and the ear share nerve pathways, so pain from an inflamed wisdom tooth area often feels like it is in the ear or throat. If you also have swelling, fever or trouble swallowing, treat it as urgent.

Can an infected wisdom tooth be pulled right away?

Sometimes, but often the infection is calmed first and the tooth is removed a little later, when the swelling is down. The dentist decides based on how the area looks, the tooth's position and your health.

Is pericoronitis serious?

A mild case is common and usually responds to cleaning and home care. It becomes serious when the infection spreads, causing facial swelling, fever or trouble swallowing, which needs the ER. Repeated episodes are a common reason to remove the tooth.
