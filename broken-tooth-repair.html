Not all breaks are the same

A tooth has three main layers: hard enamel on the outside, softer dentin under it, and the pulp in the center, where the nerve and blood vessels live. The deeper a break reaches, the more likely it is to hurt and the more involved the repair. Dentists usually sort breaks into a few types:

Common types of broken teeth
TypeWhat it usually looks or feels likeRepairs often considered
Craze linesFine surface lines in the enamel, common in adults, and they usually don't hurtOften nothing, or polishing if they bother you
Chipped enamelA small piece missing from an edge, sometimes a rough spot your tongue keeps findingSmoothing or bonding
Chip into the dentinA larger piece missing, a yellowish area showing, sensitivity to cold or sweetsBonding, a veneer, an onlay or a crown
Fractured cuspA corner of a back tooth breaks off, often around a large old fillingAn onlay or a crown
Cracked toothSharp pain when biting or releasing, pain with cold, the crack may be hard to seeA crown, sometimes with root canal treatment first
Break that exposes the nerveA pink or red spot in the center, often sharp pain or bleedingUsually root canal treatment and a crown
Split tooth or a crack below the gumThe tooth moves in two parts or the crack runs under the gum lineSometimes partial saving; often extraction and replacement
Vertical root fractureA crack in the root, often found after a root canal, with gum swelling or a tender toothUsually extraction and replacement

You can't always tell which type you have by looking in a mirror. A crack can run under the gum or through the tooth in a direction you can't see, which is why pain without a visible chip still deserves an exam. First steps right after a tooth breaks

Stay calm and look after the person first, then the tooth. Our emergency dentist page has a full first-hour checklist. In short:

  • Rinse the mouth gently with lukewarm water and check for cuts to the lips, tongue or cheek.
  • Press clean gauze or a folded cloth on any bleeding for about 10 minutes.
  • Hold a cold pack on the outside of the face, on and off, to limit swelling.
  • Take an over-the-counter pain reliever as directed on the label, if it is safe for you.
  • Cover a sharp edge with dental wax or sugar-free gum, and chew on the other side.
  • Call or text (786) 579-0521 to tell us what happened.

Save the broken pieces

If you can find the piece of tooth, keep it. For a front tooth in particular, a clean break can sometimes be bonded back on, which can look very natural because it is your own enamel. Whether that works depends on the size of the piece, how cleanly it broke and whether the nerve is involved, so there is no promise, but bringing it gives you one more option.

Pick the piece up without scrubbing it, rinse off any dirt, and keep it in a small container of milk or water so it doesn't dry out. A tooth fragment that dries out can lose some of its color and become harder to bond. Bring it to your visit along with the container.

If a whole adult tooth was knocked out, root and all, that is a different and more time-sensitive emergency. Follow our guide to the first 30 minutes after a knocked-out tooth and get to a dentist right away.

When a broken tooth means the ER

A broken tooth on its own is a dental problem. Go to the ER now, or call 911, if the break came with any of the following:

  • A hard blow to the face from a fall, a crash or a sports hit, especially if the jaw may be broken or the teeth no longer meet the way they did
  • A hit to the head, confusion, vomiting or passing out
  • Bleeding from the mouth that firm pressure doesn't stop
  • Swelling spreading toward the eye or down the neck, fever, or trouble breathing or swallowing

Repair options, from smallest to largest

Dentists try to keep as much of your natural tooth as they can. Here are the main repairs, roughly in order of how much tooth they involve:

Smoothing and polishing

A tiny chip at the edge can often be rounded off and polished in a few minutes. No numbing is usually needed, and the tooth looks and feels smooth again.

Dental bonding

For a chip into the enamel or a small part of the dentin, tooth-colored composite resin is shaped onto the tooth, hardened with a light and polished. Dental bonding is usually done in one visit, and it is often a good choice for front teeth. It can stain and chip over time, so it may need touch-ups. If you are weighing it against a veneer, see bonding vs veneers.

Veneers

When a front tooth has a larger chip, or you want it to match neighbors that you are also improving, a porcelain veneer covers the front surface. It takes more than one visit and removes a thin layer of enamel, so it isn't the first answer for every chip.

Onlays and crowns

A back tooth that has lost a cusp or has a crack usually needs more strength than bonding can give. An onlay covers part of the chewing surface; a crown covers the whole tooth and helps hold a cracked tooth together. Our in-house lab designs and makes them from your 3D scan, which can mean fewer outside steps and a closer match to your other teeth.

Root canal treatment, then a crown

If the break or crack has reached the pulp, or the nerve has become inflamed or infected, a root canal cleans and seals the inside of the tooth. A crown then protects what is left. Many teeth treated this way can last for years, though it depends on how much tooth remains and on your bite.

Extraction and replacement

A tooth that is split down the middle, or cracked well below the gum or into the root, often can't be saved. In that case a tooth extraction is the honest answer. The space can later be filled with a bridge, a partial denture or a dental implant, and we talk through which suits your bone, health and budget.

Sports injuries

Basketball, soccer, baseball, skateboarding, martial arts and cycling are common sources of broken front teeth, for adults and kids alike. After a sports hit, check for a head injury first. Any sign of a concussion, a possible broken jaw or heavy bleeding means the ER before the dentist.

Once the head and jaw are cleared, bring the person and any broken pieces to us. Front teeth that have been hit hard can look fine and still be bruised inside, so we often recheck the nerve over the following weeks and months, even after the chip is repaired. A properly fitted mouthguard lowers the risk of the next injury. We make custom sports guards from a 3D scan in our in-house lab; see our page on night guards and sports guards.

Broken teeth in kids

Kids fall, collide and chew on things they shouldn't, so chipped teeth are common in childhood. How we treat them depends on whether the tooth is a baby tooth or an adult tooth.

Baby teeth: a small chip is often just smoothed and watched. A badly broken or infected baby tooth may need treatment or removal, because an infection can affect the adult tooth forming underneath. Never try to put a knocked-out baby tooth back in.

Young adult teeth: new front teeth in children have roots that are still growing, so we try hard to keep the nerve healthy. A chip is often bonded the same day, and the tooth is checked again over time.

We are a family practice and see kids and teens, though Dr. Rana isn't a pediatric dentist. For very young children or complex injuries, we may refer to a pediatric dentist. More on our kids dentistry page.

Aftercare and protecting the repair

After numbing, wait until the feeling comes back before you eat. A repaired tooth can feel a little sensitive to cold for a few days; that usually settles. If your bite feels high or uneven, call us, because a small adjustment can prevent soreness and further damage.

  • Avoid biting directly on ice, hard candy, pens, fingernails and bottle caps, especially with bonded front teeth.
  • Brush and floss around the repair the same way you do your other teeth.
  • If a temporary crown was placed, chew on the other side and avoid sticky food until the final one is in.
  • Wear a night guard if you grind, since grinding is a common reason repairs chip again.
  • Wear a mouthguard for contact sports.
  • Keep your regular checkups so we can look after the repair and the nerve over time.

Questions, answered

Can a broken tooth be fixed?

Most broken teeth can be repaired. Small chips are usually smoothed or bonded, larger breaks often need a crown, and breaks that reach the nerve may need a root canal first. A tooth split down the middle or cracked into the root often can't be saved and is replaced instead.

How long can I wait to fix a chipped tooth?

A small chip with no pain and no sharp edge can usually wait a few days to a couple of weeks. A chip that hurts, is sensitive to cold, bleeds or shows a pink spot should be seen the same day if possible. Waiting too long can let a crack spread or bacteria reach the nerve.

Can a cracked tooth heal on its own?

No. Unlike a broken bone, a crack in a tooth doesn't knit back together, and chewing can make it grow. Treating it early, often with a crown, gives the tooth a better chance of being saved.

Is bonding or a crown better for a chipped tooth?

It depends on the size and location of the chip. Bonding suits small and medium chips, especially on front teeth, and is usually done in one visit. A crown is usually stronger for back teeth, large breaks or cracked teeth. Dr. Rana explains the trade-offs for your tooth.

Does fixing a broken tooth hurt?

Small repairs like smoothing or bonding often need little or no numbing. For larger repairs, local anesthetic numbs the area, so most people feel pressure rather than pain during treatment. Mild tenderness for a few days afterward is common, and oral sedation is available for suitable patients who are anxious.

Can a front tooth that broke in half be saved?

Often, yes, if the break is above the gum and the root is healthy. Depending on whether the nerve is exposed, the repair may be a bonded fragment, bonding, a crown, or a root canal and a crown. Bring the broken piece in milk or water, and come in as soon as you can.

Does dental insurance cover a broken tooth?

Many plans pay toward repairs such as bonding, crowns and root canals, usually after deductibles and up to your annual maximum, and some have waiting periods. Purely cosmetic repairs may not be covered. Most major dental insurance is accepted here, and we check your benefits for you.
