Go to the ER now if you have any of these

Most abscesses are treated in a dental office. A few become dangerous because the infection spreads into the face, neck or bloodstream. Don't wait for a dental appointment, and go to the ER or call 911, if you have:

  • Swelling that is spreading toward your eye, along the jaw or down your neck
  • A fever, chills or feeling generally very unwell
  • Trouble breathing, trouble swallowing, or drooling because swallowing hurts
  • A jaw so swollen or stiff that you can barely open your mouth
  • Swelling under the tongue or the floor of the mouth that pushes the tongue up
  • Bleeding that firm pressure doesn't stop, or swelling after a blow to the face

The ER can treat a spreading infection, and you will still need a dentist afterward to deal with the tooth. What a dental abscess is

An abscess is a pocket of pus that forms when bacteria get somewhere they shouldn't and the body walls the infection off. In the mouth, there are three common kinds:

  • At the root tip (periapical). Bacteria reach the pulp through deep decay, a crack or an old injury, the nerve dies, and infection collects at the end of the root inside the bone. This is the most common kind.
  • In the gum (periodontal). Infection builds in a deep gum pocket beside the tooth, often linked to gum disease or something trapped under the gum.
  • Around a partly erupted tooth (pericoronal). Usually a wisdom tooth, where food and bacteria collect under a flap of gum.

Knowing which kind you have matters, because each one is treated a little differently. That is something the exam and X-rays sort out.

Signs of a tooth or gum abscess

Abscesses don't always look dramatic. Some cause severe pain, others very little. Common signs include:

  • A deep, throbbing ache that may spread to the ear, jaw or neck on the same side
  • A tooth that is very tender to touch or bite on, or feels slightly raised
  • A pimple-like bump on the gum, sometimes leaking a salty or bad-tasting fluid
  • Swelling of the gum, cheek or jaw
  • Bad breath or a bad taste that doesn't go away with brushing
  • Tender, swollen glands under the jaw
  • Pain that eases suddenly after a bump drains, even though the problem is still there

Our blog post on abscess signs covers how to tell a minor issue from an emergency. If you aren't sure, call or text and describe what you see.

Why an abscess is urgent even when it stops hurting

An abscess doesn't clear up for good on its own. The pain may come and go, and a gum bump may drain and shrink, but the bacteria are still living inside the tooth or the gum pocket. Drainage only lets some pressure out. Without treatment of the source, the infection usually returns, and in the meantime it can slowly destroy bone around the tooth.

Less often, an infection can spread into the spaces of the face and neck, which can become serious within hours or days. That is why we treat any abscess as something to see soon, and why the ER signs above matter so much.

What we do at your visit

The first priority is your safety, then relief, then getting rid of the source. A typical visit goes like this:

  • Check for spread. Dr. Rana looks at the swelling, your temperature, your ability to open, swallow and breathe comfortably, and the glands in your neck. If the infection looks like it is spreading, he sends you to the ER rather than delaying.
  • Find the source. He examines the teeth and gums, tests the nerve, and takes X-rays to see the root tip and the bone.
  • Explain the options. You hear which tooth is involved, what kind of abscess it is, and the realistic choices, along with their cost in writing before treatment.
  • Relieve the pressure. With the area numbed, he can often drain the abscess, either through the tooth or through a small opening in the gum. Many people feel better quickly once the pressure is released.
  • Treat the source. Depending on the tooth, that may start the same day or at a follow-up visit soon after.

Local anesthetic can work less well in an inflamed area, so we take our time, and oral sedation is available for suitable patients who are anxious.

Treatment options

Draining an abscess helps, but the tooth or gum that caused it has to be treated too. These are the main ways to do that.

Root canal treatment

If the tooth can be saved, a root canal removes the infected pulp, cleans and disinfects the canals, and seals them. This lets the infection drain and the bone heal over time while you keep your own tooth. A back tooth usually needs a crown afterward, made in our in-house lab. Many people find it is much like a filling; our post on what a root canal feels like explains more.

Extraction

If the tooth is cracked into the root or too damaged to restore, removing it takes away the source of infection. A tooth extraction also lets the abscess drain through the socket. We then talk through replacing the tooth, for example with a bridge or a dental implant once the area has healed. If you are torn between the two, see root canal vs extraction.

Gum abscess treatment

For an abscess in a gum pocket, the area is drained and the pocket cleaned thoroughly to remove plaque, tartar and anything trapped under the gum. Ongoing gum disease treatment, such as scaling and root planing, helps keep it from coming back.

Antibiotics, only when appropriate

Antibiotics don't replace treatment of the tooth. They can't reach bacteria inside a tooth whose nerve has died, so on their own they usually only quiet an abscess for a while. Dr. Rana may prescribe them when he decides they are appropriate, for example if the infection is spreading, you have a fever, or you have a health condition that raises your risk. If you are given a prescription, take it exactly as directed and finish it unless you are told otherwise.

Please don't take leftover antibiotics, or antibiotics prescribed for someone else. They may be the wrong drug or dose, they can hide warning signs, and unnecessary antibiotics add to resistance.

When we refer to a specialist

Dr. Rana is a general dentist and treats most abscesses himself, including drainage, many root canals and routine extractions. When something needs more focused care, we tell you plainly. A tooth with complicated or previously treated canals may be referred to an endodontist. A difficult surgical extraction may be referred to an oral surgeon. Advanced gum disease may be referred to a periodontist. And any infection that is spreading goes to the ER or a hospital the same day.

While you wait to be seen

Home care can make you more comfortable, but it doesn't treat an abscess. Keep watching for the ER signs listed at the top of this page.

  • Take an over-the-counter pain reliever as directed on the label, if it is safe for you.
  • Hold a cold pack wrapped in a thin cloth on the outside of the cheek for 15 to 20 minutes at a time. Don't use heat.
  • Rinse gently with lukewarm salt water to keep the area clean.
  • Don't squeeze, pop or cut a gum bump.
  • Drink fluids and rest, and sleep with your head raised on an extra pillow.
  • Chew on the other side and skip very hot, cold or sugary food.

Aftercare and healing

After drainage, a root canal or an extraction, soreness and some swelling for a few days is common. For most people, the pain that brought them in eases noticeably within a day or two, and swelling keeps going down over the following days. Bone around a root tip can take months to fill back in, which we check on future X-rays.

Follow the instructions we give you for your treatment. Keep the area clean with gentle brushing and rinsing, eat soft food on the other side, and come back for the crown or follow-up visit when advised. Call or text us right away if swelling gets bigger instead of smaller, if pain gets worse after two or three days, or if you develop a fever. If you were prescribed antibiotics and develop a rash, hives or trouble breathing, stop and get emergency care.

Preventing another abscess

  • Treat cavities while they are small, before they reach the nerve.
  • Have a cracked or chipped tooth checked early.
  • Keep up with regular cleanings and checkups, and treat gum disease when it is found.
  • Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and clean between teeth daily.
  • Wear a mouthguard for sports, since an old injury can lead to an abscess years later.
  • Finish treatment that has been started, such as getting the crown after a root canal.

Questions, answered

Can a tooth abscess go away on its own?

No. The pain may ease and a gum bump may drain, but the infection stays inside the tooth or gum pocket and usually comes back. It needs treatment of the source, such as a root canal, a gum cleaning or an extraction.

Can a dentist drain an abscess on the same day?

Often, yes. Once the area is numbed, the dentist can usually release the pressure through the tooth or a small opening in the gum, which tends to bring relief. The tooth still needs treatment to stop the infection from returning. Call or text first so a same-day time can be held.

Do I need antibiotics for a tooth abscess?

Not always. Many abscesses are treated by draining them and treating the tooth, without antibiotics. A dentist may prescribe them when they decide they are appropriate, such as when an infection is spreading or you have a fever. Don't take leftover or borrowed antibiotics.

Is a tooth abscess dangerous?

It can be. Most abscesses stay local and are treated in a dental office, but some spread into the face, neck or bloodstream. Swelling near the eye or down the neck, fever, or trouble breathing or swallowing means go to the ER now.

Should I go to the ER or a dentist for a tooth abscess?

If you have spreading swelling, fever, or trouble breathing or swallowing, go to the ER. Otherwise a dentist is usually the right place, because the ER can treat the infection but generally can't fix the tooth that caused it.

Is a root canal or extraction better for an abscessed tooth?

If the tooth can be restored, a root canal keeps your natural tooth and is often the first choice. If it is cracked into the root or too damaged, extraction may be more sensible. The decision depends on the tooth, your health and your preference, and Dr. Rana explains both options.

How long does an abscess take to heal after treatment?

Pain often eases within a day or two of drainage and treatment, and swelling usually keeps going down over several days. The bone around the root tip can take months to fill in. If swelling grows or pain worsens after a few days, contact your dentist.
