What bonding can fix

Bonding means adding tooth-colored composite to a tooth, shaping it, and hardening it with a curing light. Because the material is added rather than cut away, it suits small, specific changes. These are the jobs it does well:

  • Chipped edges. A corner knocked off a front tooth is the classic bonding repair.
  • Small gaps. A narrow space between the two front teeth, or between other teeth, can often be closed by widening the teeth slightly on each side.
  • Small shape changes. A tooth that is a little short, pointed, worn flat or narrower than its partner can be built out to match.
  • Spots that whitening won't lift. Some small dark or white marks can be covered with a thin layer of composite.
  • Some exposed roots. When gums pull back, the root surface can become sensitive or wear a notch near the gum line. Covering it with composite can protect it and may ease sensitivity. Our post on why gums recede explains the causes.
  • Small cavities. A tooth-colored filling is a form of bonding too; see tooth-colored fillings.

When bonding isn't the right tool

Bonding is versatile, but it is not the answer to everything, and using it for the wrong job usually means repairs every few months. We will steer you elsewhere when:

  • A large part of the tooth has broken off, or the break reaches the nerve. A crown or other repair is usually stronger; see broken tooth repair.
  • You grind or clench hard. Composite can chip under that force, so a night guard is often part of the plan, or another material is a better choice.
  • Teeth are crowded or rotated. Moving them with orthodontic treatment such as Invisalign may give a better result than adding bulk.
  • All your teeth are darker than you would like. Teeth whitening or veneers usually suit an overall color change better.
  • The gap is wide or many teeth are involved. Large amounts of composite can look bulky and need more upkeep.

Bonding vs veneers

Both bonding and porcelain veneers change how front teeth look, and they are often confused. The simplest way to decide is to think about scope. For one chip, one small gap or one tooth that is a little short, bonding is often the sensible first step. For a full change in the color, shape and length of six to ten front teeth, veneers usually look more uniform and hold their color longer.

Bonding can also work as a trial. If you are unsure about a new tooth shape, bonding a tooth or two lets you live with the change before you commit to porcelain. Our digital smile preview helps you see the likely result either way. For a fuller side-by-side, read composite bonding vs veneers.

Shade matching: how bonding blends in

Natural teeth are not one flat color. They are slightly more see-through at the biting edge and warmer near the gum. Composite comes in many shades and levels of translucency, and a good bond often uses two or three in thin layers so the repair blends in from across the room and up close.

We choose the shade at the very start of the visit. Teeth get lighter when they dry out during treatment, so a shade picked halfway through can end up too bright once your teeth are moist again.

One important detail: composite doesn't whiten. If you want brighter teeth, whiten first, then bond to the new shade. We usually wait a couple of weeks after whitening so the color settles and the bond grips well. Bonding a tooth and whitening the rest later can leave the bonded tooth looking darker than its neighbors.

What happens at the visit

Many bonding cases are finished in one appointment. Here is how it usually goes:

  • Agreeing on the goal. We look at the tooth together, often with the 3D scan and smile preview from your first visit, and agree on the shape you want.
  • Picking the shade. We match the composite to your tooth before it dries out.
  • Preparing the surface. The enamel is lightly roughened, sometimes with a very small bevel at the edge so the repair blends. A mild gel and a bonding liquid help the resin hold on.
  • Building and sculpting. The composite goes on in thin layers and is shaped by hand, then each layer is hardened with a curing light.
  • Shaping and checking your bite. We trim and contour the tooth, then check how it meets the opposite teeth when you bite and slide side to side.
  • Polishing. A final polish gives the composite a natural shine and a smooth surface that resists stains.

Time depends on the number of teeth and how much shaping each needs. A small chip can be a short visit; closing a gap or reshaping several teeth takes longer, and larger cases can be split over two visits.

Do you need to be numbed?

Often not. Many cosmetic bonding cases only touch the outer enamel, which has no nerves, so lots of people need no anesthetic at all. We do numb the area when the work goes deeper, when there is decay to remove, when a chip is close to the nerve, or when we are covering a sensitive root surface. You can always ask for numbing if you would feel more comfortable with it.

How long bonding lasts, and staining

Bonding often lasts several years before it needs a touch-up, and some people get longer. The biggest factors are where the tooth is, how hard you bite on it, and your habits. A small repair on a side tooth tends to last longer than a large edge repair on a front tooth that takes the force of biting into food.

Composite is more porous than porcelain, so it can slowly pick up stains from coffee, tea, red wine, colored sauces and tobacco. It can also lose some of its shine. Polishing at your regular cleanings often brings the gloss back. Many people avoid strongly colored food and drinks for the first day or two after bonding while the surface settles.

The good news is that bonding is usually easy to repair. A chipped corner can often be patched without redoing the whole tooth, and stained bonding can be resurfaced or replaced in a short visit.

Caring for bonded teeth

  • Brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste and a soft brush, and skip very gritty whitening pastes that can dull the surface
  • Clean between your teeth daily; the edge where composite meets tooth is a spot plaque likes
  • Don't bite your nails, pens, ice or hard candy, and don't use your teeth to open packages
  • Rinse with water after coffee, tea or red wine
  • Wear a night guard if you grind or clench
  • Keep up regular cleanings and tell the hygienist which teeth are bonded

Chipped tooth? Start here

A small chip with no pain is rarely an emergency. Smooth sharp edges with a little dental wax or sugarless gum to protect your tongue, avoid biting on that tooth, and call or text to book. Many small chips can be bonded at a regular visit. If you find the broken piece, keep it in a little milk or water and bring it, since sometimes it can be bonded back on.

A larger break, pain, sensitivity that lingers, a tooth that feels loose or a bit of pink or red inside the break needs to be seen sooner. What dental bonding costs

Bonding is usually one of the more affordable ways to improve a front tooth, and it generally costs less per tooth than a porcelain veneer. The exact price depends on your case and is confirmed in writing before treatment. The main cost drivers:

  • How many teeth are bonded
  • How large and complex each repair is, from a small corner to rebuilding an edge or closing a gap
  • How much layering is needed for a natural look on a highly visible tooth
  • Whether old bonding or an old filling has to come off first
  • Whether you whiten before bonding, which is a separate treatment

Remember the long view too: bonding may need touch-ups over the years, while veneers cost more upfront and are replaced less often. Insurance: cosmetic or repair?

Insurance usually looks at why the tooth is being bonded. When bonding repairs damage, such as a tooth chipped in a fall, a small cavity, a broken filling or a worn root surface causing sensitivity, many plans treat it like a filling, a basic service paid after your deductible and up to your annual maximum. Those maximums are often in the $1,000 to $2,000 range.

Bonding done purely to change how teeth look, such as closing a gap or reshaping an even, healthy tooth, is usually considered cosmetic and often isn't covered. Every plan is different, and the notes and photos from your visit matter. We accept most major dental insurance, and we check your benefits for you.

Questions, answered

How long does dental bonding last?

Bonding often lasts several years before it needs a touch-up, and some people get longer. It depends on where the tooth is, how hard you bite, grinding and habits like biting nails or ice. Small chips and worn spots can usually be repaired without redoing the whole tooth.

Does tooth bonding hurt?

Many bonding cases only involve the outer enamel and need no numbing at all. When the work goes deeper, involves decay or covers a sensitive root, we numb the area first. Most people feel only mild pressure, and you can ask for numbing anytime.

Is bonding or veneers better for a chipped tooth?

For a small chip on one or two teeth, bonding is often the simpler and less costly choice. Veneers make more sense when you want to change the color and shape of several front teeth or want a more stain-resistant result. We show you both options at your visit.

Can you whiten bonded teeth?

No. Whitening lightens natural enamel but doesn't change the color of composite. If you want whiter teeth, whiten first and bond afterward to the new shade, usually after waiting a couple of weeks. Stained bonding can be polished or replaced.

How much does dental bonding cost in Miami?

It depends on how many teeth are bonded and how much shaping each one needs. Bonding usually costs less per tooth than porcelain veneers, which start from $999 per tooth. We confirm the exact price in writing before treatment.

Does insurance cover dental bonding?

When bonding repairs a chip, a cavity or a sensitive exposed root, many plans cover it like a filling. Bonding done only for appearance, such as closing a gap, is usually considered cosmetic and often isn't covered. We check your benefits for you.

Can bonding close a gap between front teeth?

Often, yes, if the gap is small. Composite is added to the sides of the teeth next to the gap so they meet, while keeping their proportions natural. Wide gaps may look better with orthodontic treatment or veneers, and we will tell you which suits your smile.
