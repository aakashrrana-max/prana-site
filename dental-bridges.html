How a dental bridge works

A bridge literally bridges a gap. The replacement tooth in the middle is called a pontic. It is joined to supports on one or both sides, called abutments, which are usually crowns on your natural teeth or on dental implants. The whole piece is made as one unit and cemented or screwed in place, so it doesn't come out at home.

Bridges are described by the number of units. The most common is a three-unit bridge: two crowns on the neighboring teeth with one false tooth between them, replacing a single missing tooth. Longer bridges can replace two or more teeth in a row, as long as the supports are strong enough to carry the load.

Why fill the gap at all? When a tooth is missing, the teeth beside it tend to tip into the space, and the tooth above or below it can drift toward it. Over time that can change your bite, open spaces where food packs in, and make the area harder to clean. Our article on ignoring a missing tooth explains what usually happens next.

Types of dental bridges

Not every gap calls for the same design. These are the main options Dr. Rana considers:

Traditional bridge

The most common type. The teeth on both sides of the gap are reshaped and covered with crowns, and the false tooth is attached between them. It is strong and well proven. The tradeoff is that enamel has to be removed from the anchor teeth, which is permanent. It makes the most sense when those neighbors already have large fillings or old crowns that would benefit from coverage anyway.

Implant-supported bridge

When three or more teeth in a row are missing, two or more implants can serve as the anchors instead of natural teeth. Your healthy teeth are left alone, and the implants load the jawbone the way roots do. It needs enough bone, a minor surgery and a healing period of a few months before the final bridge is attached, and it usually costs more than a traditional bridge.

Maryland bridge (resin-bonded)

A false tooth with thin wings, made of metal or ceramic, that are bonded to the backs of the neighboring teeth. Very little enamel is removed. It is mostly used for front teeth, where biting forces are lighter, and it can be a good fit for younger people waiting on an implant or for someone who doesn't want healthy teeth trimmed. The bond can come loose, especially with a heavy bite or grinding, and it isn't suitable for every gap, so we only suggest it when your bite and teeth allow.

Cantilever bridge

A false tooth supported from one side only. It is used in limited situations, usually toward the front of the mouth, because the single anchor carries all the force. We use it sparingly and explain why if we think it fits.

Bridge or implant: how to decide

For a single missing tooth, the usual choice is between a three-unit bridge and a single dental implant. Both can work well for many years. They differ in what they ask of your other teeth, your bone and your calendar.

A single implant replaces the root as well as the tooth, stands on its own, and leaves the neighboring teeth untouched. At Prana, a single implant starts from $1,999, including the implant, abutment and crown, with the exact price confirmed in writing before treatment. It needs enough healthy bone, a minor surgery and usually a few months of healing before the final crown. Read more on our dental implants page.

A bridge needs no surgery and is usually finished in a few weeks. It can be the more sensible choice when:

  • The neighboring teeth already have large fillings or crowns that need replacing anyway
  • There isn't enough bone for an implant and you'd rather avoid grafting
  • A health condition or medication makes implant surgery less advisable
  • You want the gap closed in weeks rather than months

The long-term picture matters too. A bridge depends on its anchor teeth, so if one of them develops decay or a cracked root, the whole bridge is affected. An implant has its own risks, such as gum infection around it if it isn't kept clean. Our guide to implant vs bridge cost compares the two over time. If several teeth are missing on different sides of the mouth, a removable partial can also make sense; see our page on dentures.

Your bridge, visit by visit

1. Free first visit

An exam, a digital 3D scan of your teeth and bite, and a smile preview. We look at the gap, the teeth around it and your gums, and talk through whether a bridge, an implant or another option fits you. X-rays are a separate step, and their cost is confirmed with you before they are taken.

2. Getting the foundation ready

A bridge is only as healthy as what holds it. If an anchor tooth has decay, it is treated first. If a tooth needs a root canal, that is done before the bridge. If your gums are inflamed or measuring deep, we treat that first, because a bridge placed on unhealthy gums is harder to keep clean; see gum disease treatment.

3. Preparing the anchor teeth

The area is numbed with local anesthetic. For a traditional bridge, the anchor teeth are reshaped so the crowns can fit over them. Then we take a digital scan instead of the putty impressions many people remember, which most patients find much more comfortable. Our article on 3D scanning vs impressions explains how that works. We match the shade to your natural teeth and fit a temporary bridge so you don't leave with a gap.

4. Made in our in-house lab

Your bridge is designed and made by our in-house lab, working directly with Dr. Rana. That makes it easier to fine-tune shape and shade, and to make changes quickly if something needs adjusting. We give you the timeline at the preparation visit; it depends on the design and material, and we don't promise same-day bridges.

5. Fitting and cementing

The temporary comes off and the final bridge is tried in. We check the fit at the gumline, the color, how it feels against your tongue and how your teeth meet when you bite and slide. Once you are both happy, it is cemented in place. Small bite adjustments are normal at this stage.

6. A quick follow-up

Many people get used to a new bridge within a few days. If your bite feels high, or a tooth stays sensitive after the first week or two, come back for a quick check. A small adjustment usually solves it.

Caring for a bridge: floss threaders and more

The false tooth sits on the gum rather than in it, so regular floss can't slide down between it and its neighbors. Food and plaque collect under the pontic and around the edges of the crowns, and that is where most bridge problems start. Daily care looks like this:

  • Floss threaders or super floss. A threader works like a needle: you pull floss through the threader, guide it under the false tooth, then floss the sides of each anchor tooth. Super floss has a stiff end built in.
  • A water flosser. It flushes debris from under the bridge and is easier for some people, though most still need a threader or brush to remove sticky plaque.
  • Small interdental brushes. Useful where gaps are wider, especially around implant-supported bridges.
  • Brush twice a day with a soft brush and fluoride toothpaste, paying attention to where the crowns meet the gum.
  • Regular checkups and cleanings, so we can spot decay at the crown edges early, while it is still small.
  • Go easy on very hard things. Ice, hard candy, and using your teeth to open packages can chip porcelain. If you grind at night, a night guard helps protect the bridge.

How long a dental bridge lasts

With good care and regular checkups, many bridges last a decade or more, and some much longer. How long yours lasts depends on the health of the anchor teeth, your gums, your bite, whether you grind, and how well the area is cleaned. Nobody can honestly promise you a number.

Bridges rarely fail because the porcelain wears out. More often, decay starts at the edge of an anchor crown, gum disease weakens the support, or an anchor tooth cracks. Signs worth a visit include:

  • A bad smell or taste coming from under the bridge
  • A bridge that feels loose, rocks or clicks
  • New sensitivity or pain in an anchor tooth
  • Gums that bleed, swell or have receded to show a dark edge
  • A chip or crack in the porcelain

Replacing an old bridge

Many people come to us with a bridge that is 15 or 20 years old and showing its age: a dark line at the gumline, a chip, a gap where food traps, or a dentist who mentioned decay underneath. Replacing it usually means carefully cutting the old bridge off, cleaning up the anchor teeth and checking what is left.

The honest part is that the condition of the anchors can't be fully known until the old bridge is off. Sometimes they are healthy and a new bridge goes right back on. Sometimes an anchor needs a filling, a build-up or a root canal first. Occasionally a tooth can't be saved, and the plan changes to a longer bridge, an implant-supported bridge, or implants. We explain these possibilities before we start, and if the plan changes once the bridge is off, you get the updated price in writing before we go further.

Old bridge, new bridge, or something else? Sometimes the gap that made sense to bridge years ago is now better suited to an implant, especially if an anchor tooth is failing. We lay out the options side by side.

What a dental bridge costs in Miami

We don't publish a bridge price, because the range of bridges is wide and a number without your mouth in front of us would be a guess. Your exact price is confirmed in writing before any treatment. These are the things that move it:

  • Number of units. A three-unit bridge costs less than a four- or five-unit bridge.
  • Type. A Maryland bridge usually costs less than a traditional bridge, and an implant-supported bridge costs more because it includes the implants.
  • Material. Options include zirconia, other all-ceramic materials and porcelain fused to metal, each with different strength, looks and cost.
  • Work on the anchor teeth. Fillings, build-ups, root canals or gum treatment add to the total if they are needed.
  • Imaging and sedation. X-rays, and a CBCT for implant-supported bridges, plus optional oral sedation.
  • Replacing an existing bridge. Removing the old one and repairing what is underneath can add steps.

When you compare a bridge with an implant, think about the cost over time, not only the first bill. A bridge that lasts many years can be money well spent, while one built on weak anchors may need replacing sooner. We give you itemized prices for each option so you can compare them fairly.

Dental bridge questions, answered

How long does a dental bridge last?

Many bridges last a decade or more with good care, and some last much longer. The lifespan depends mostly on the health of the anchor teeth and gums, your bite, and how well you clean under the bridge. Decay at the edge of an anchor crown is the most common reason a bridge has to be replaced.

Is a bridge or an implant better for one missing tooth?

Neither is right for everyone. An implant leaves the neighboring teeth untouched and stands on its own, but it needs enough bone, a minor surgery and a few months of healing. A bridge needs no surgery and is faster, and it can make more sense when the neighboring teeth already need crowns. We show you both options with written prices.

Does getting a dental bridge hurt?

The teeth are numbed before they are prepared, so most people feel pressure rather than pain. Afterward the anchor teeth and gums may be sore or sensitive to cold for a few days. If you get nervous at the dentist, ask us about oral sedation.

How do you clean under a dental bridge?

Use a floss threader or super floss to pass floss under the false tooth every day, then clean the sides of each anchor tooth. A water flosser and small interdental brushes help too. Brushing twice a day and regular professional cleanings protect the edges of the crowns from decay.

How many teeth can a bridge replace?

A traditional bridge most often replaces one or two teeth in a row. Longer spans put more stress on the anchor teeth, so for three or more missing teeth an implant-supported bridge or a partial denture is often the safer choice. The right answer depends on your bite and the health of the supports.

Can a bridge be put on teeth that have gum disease?

Gum disease should be treated and under control first. A bridge on inflamed gums or weakened bone is harder to clean and more likely to run into problems. Once the gums are stable, a bridge is often still possible.

Does insurance cover dental bridges?

Many dental plans cover bridges as a major service, usually at a lower percentage after the deductible, and some have waiting periods. Annual maximums often limit how much of the cost the plan pays in one year. We check your benefits for you and give you the exact price in writing before treatment.
