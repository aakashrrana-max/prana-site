What to do before you get here

A few simple steps can help before you reach us. For a toothache, rinse with warm water and gently floss to clear anything caught between teeth. Avoid placing aspirin or any painkiller directly against the gum, since that can burn the tissue. If your face is swollen, a cold compress on the outside of your cheek helps more than heat, which can make swelling worse. For a knocked-out tooth, keep it moist in milk or saliva, avoid touching the root, and get to us as quickly as you can. Timing matters for whether it can be saved. For a chipped or broken tooth, save any pieces if you can and rinse your mouth gently with warm water. If you've lost a filling or crown, keep the piece if possible and avoid chewing on that side until we see you. For a broken denture, please don't try to repair it yourself with glue. Bring the pieces in and we'll take it from there.

When to go to the ER instead

Most dental emergencies are safely treated right here in our office, but a few situations call for an emergency room instead. Go to the ER or call 911 if you have facial swelling along with a fever, any trouble breathing or swallowing, or significant trauma to the jaw from a fall or impact. Those symptoms can point to an infection that's spread beyond the tooth or a possible fracture, and both need care beyond what a dental office can safely provide. If you're not sure which applies to you, call us first. We'd rather help you figure out the right next step than have you guess.

Dental emergency or ER? A quick guide

Dentists treat teeth, gums and the soft tissue around them. Emergency rooms treat problems that threaten your breathing, spread through the body or involve broken bones. Many ERs can ease pain or start antibiotics for a bad tooth, but they usually can't repair the tooth itself, so you will still need a dentist afterward. Use this table as a rough guide:

The first hour: what to do, step by step

What you do in the first hour can make a real difference, especially for a tooth that has been knocked out. Here is what generally helps for the four problems we hear about most.

A knocked-out tooth

  • Pick the tooth up by the crown, the white part you chew with. Try not to touch the root.
  • If it is dirty, rinse it for a few seconds with milk or water. Don't scrub it, dry it or wrap it in tissue.
  • If you can, slide it back into the socket facing the right way, then bite gently on clean gauze or a folded cloth to hold it there.
  • If it won't go back in, keep it in a small cup of milk or your own saliva. Plain water is a last resort.
  • Get to a dentist as fast as you can. A tooth put back within roughly 30 minutes to an hour is much more likely to reattach than one left out longer.
  • Baby teeth are different. Don't push a knocked-out baby tooth back in, because that can harm the adult tooth growing underneath. Bring your child, and the tooth if you have it.

A broken or cracked tooth

  • Rinse gently with warm water and save any pieces in milk or a clean, damp container.
  • Press clean gauze on any bleeding for about 10 minutes.
  • Hold a cold pack on your cheek for 15 to 20 minutes at a time to limit swelling.
  • Cover a sharp edge with dental wax from a pharmacy, or sugar-free gum, so it doesn't cut your tongue or cheek.
  • Chew on the other side, and skip very hot, very cold or sugary food that sets off the nerve.

Swelling

  • Check for warning signs first: fever, trouble swallowing or breathing, or swelling spreading under the jaw or toward the eye. Any of those means the ER, now.
  • Without those signs, use a cold pack on the outside of the cheek, on and off. Skip heat.
  • Don't try to pop or drain a bump on the gum.
  • An over-the-counter pain reliever, taken as the label directs and only if it is safe for you, can help until you are seen.
  • Swelling from a tooth infection usually doesn't clear up for good without treatment, even if it settles for a day or two, so get it checked.

A lost crown or filling

  • Keep the crown in a clean bag or container and bring it with you. If the tooth underneath is sound, it can sometimes be cleaned and recemented.
  • If the crown slides on easily, a little temporary dental cement from a pharmacy can hold it for a day or two. Never use household or super glue.
  • If it doesn't fit easily, don't force it. Leave it out and keep the area clean.
  • Brush gently around the exposed tooth, which may be sensitive, and chew on the other side.

Questions, answered

Should I go to the ER or a dentist for a toothache?

For a toothache on its own, a dentist is usually the right place, because the ER can ease pain but generally can't fix the tooth. Go to the ER if the toothache comes with facial swelling and fever, trouble breathing or swallowing, or swelling spreading toward your eye or neck.

How can I stop tooth pain until I can see a dentist?

Rinse with warm water, floss gently to clear anything stuck, and use a cold pack on the cheek. An over-the-counter pain reliever taken as the label directs, if it is safe for you, can help. Don't put a pill directly on the gum, and get the tooth checked even if the pain fades.

Is a swollen face from a tooth an emergency?

It can be. Swelling with fever, trouble swallowing or breathing, or swelling that spreads toward the eye or down the neck needs the ER right away. A small bump on the gum that doesn't hurt is less urgent but still a sign of infection that needs a dentist soon.

Is a cracked tooth an emergency?

A crack that causes sharp pain, bleeding or a loose piece should be seen the same day if possible. A small chip with no pain can usually wait a few days. Either way, avoid chewing on that side until a dentist has looked at it.

Can I put a crown back on myself?

If it slides on easily, temporary dental cement from a pharmacy can hold it for a day or two. Never use household or super glue, and don't force it if it doesn't fit. Bring the crown with you so the dentist can check whether it can be reused.

Does dental insurance cover emergency visits?

Many plans pay toward part of an urgent visit, but it depends on your plan, waiting periods and how much of your yearly maximum is left. Most major dental insurance is accepted here, so send us your plan details and we check your benefits for you.
