Signs you may have a cavity

A cavity is a spot where acid made by mouth bacteria has dissolved part of the tooth. Early on it often causes no feeling at all, because the outer enamel has no nerves. As it grows into the softer layer underneath, these are the signs people tend to notice:

  • A quick zing when you eat something sweet or drink something cold, which fades in a few seconds
  • A twinge when you bite down on one particular tooth
  • A dark spot, a small hole, or a rough edge your tongue keeps finding
  • Food that packs into the same gap every time, or floss that shreds in one spot
  • A chalky white patch near the gum line, which can be an early weak spot in the enamel

Plenty of cavities show none of these signs and are found only at a checkup. That is not bad luck; it is simply how decay starts. Pain that throbs, lingers after the cold is gone or wakes you at night is a different story and can mean the nerve is involved, so call us sooner rather than later.

Not every spot needs a filling

Some early weak spots in the enamel have not broken through to a real hole yet. Those can sometimes harden again with fluoride, better cleaning between the teeth and fewer sugary snacks. In that case we may suggest watching the spot and checking it again at your next visit rather than drilling it now.

Once decay has gone past the enamel, it doesn't heal on its own, and waiting usually means a larger filling later. We show you the image and explain which category your tooth is in, so the choice makes sense to you.

Composite and amalgam: an honest comparison

We place tooth-colored composite fillings. Composite is a resin mixed with very fine glass or ceramic particles. It is placed soft, shaped to the tooth and hardened with a blue curing light, and it bonds to the tooth surface. Because it sticks to the tooth, the dentist can often remove less healthy tooth structure than a traditional silver filling needs to stay in place.

Silver-colored amalgam has been used for well over a century. It is durable and forgiving to place, and it contains mercury bound into a metal alloy. The American Dental Association considers amalgam a safe, effective material. The FDA has suggested that certain higher-risk groups, such as people who are pregnant or nursing and young children, consider other materials when appropriate. If you have questions about your own situation, ask us and we will talk it through calmly.

Neither material lasts forever. How long a filling holds up depends more on its size, its position, your bite, grinding and your cleaning habits than on the label.

Replacing old fillings

Fillings wear out. Edges can chip, small gaps can open where the filling meets the tooth, and decay can start again underneath. Old silver fillings sometimes show fine crack lines in the tooth around them. Those are good reasons to replace a filling, and we can usually see them on the exam and X-rays before they cause pain.

What we don't recommend is drilling out sound, intact silver fillings only because of the material. Every time a filling is replaced, a little more healthy tooth goes with it. If you would like old fillings changed for appearance, for example a dark filling that shows when you smile, that is a fair choice; we'll explain the tradeoffs and how insurance usually treats a cosmetic replacement.

A very large old filling is sometimes better replaced with a crown, because a new filling would leave thin walls of tooth that can break. We'll tell you if your tooth is in that group.

Cavities between teeth

The spot where two teeth touch is where many adult cavities begin, and it is impossible to see in a mirror. Bitewing X-rays show it clearly, which is why we take them even when nothing hurts. Our post on cavities between teeth and X-rays explains why they hide so well.

Fixing a cavity between teeth takes a little extra care. A thin band is placed around the tooth so the composite can be built back up to touch its neighbor firmly. The goal is a contact that floss snaps through, with no ledge for food to catch on. A loose contact that traps food can lead to sore gums and a new cavity next door.

What happens at a filling visit

Most fillings are done in one visit. Here is the usual order:

  • Confirming the tooth. We check the tooth with the exam and X-rays and explain what we're about to do.
  • Numbing. A gel goes on the gum first, then local anesthetic. We wait until the tooth is fully numb.
  • Keeping it dry. Cotton rolls or a thin rubber sheet keep saliva away, because composite bonds poorly to a wet tooth.
  • Removing the decay. Only the soft, damaged part of the tooth is cleaned out.
  • Preparing the surface. A mild gel and a bonding liquid help the composite grip the tooth.
  • Building the filling. Composite is placed in thin layers, each hardened with a curing light, then shaped to look like a tooth.
  • Checking your bite. You bite on thin colored paper so we can find and smooth any high spot.
  • Polishing. The surface is polished smooth so it resists stains and feels natural to your tongue.

Time depends on the size of the filling and how many teeth we treat. A single small filling is often a short visit. Several fillings can be done together, or spread over visits if you prefer shorter appointments.

Numbing: what to expect

Most fillings are done with local anesthetic, and with it you should feel vibration and pressure but not pain. A few very shallow fillings can be done without numbing if you would rather skip it; we'll tell you honestly whether yours is one of them.

The numbness usually lasts a few hours. Until it wears off, don't chew on that side, be careful with hot drinks, and keep an eye on kids so they don't bite their lip or cheek. If you get nervous at the dentist, tell us. We go at your pace, explain each step in English or Spanish, and oral sedation is available for people who need more help relaxing.

Sensitivity after a filling

Some sensitivity after a filling is common, especially when the cavity was deep. The tooth may react to cold or to biting for a few days to a few weeks, and it should get a little better as time passes. A toothpaste for sensitive teeth can help. The gum around the tooth may also feel tender for a day where the band sat.

  • Your bite feels high or the tooth hurts when you chew: the filling may need a small adjustment. It is quick, and you shouldn't wait it out.
  • Cold sensitivity that is slowly fading: usually normal. Give it time and let us know if it isn't improving.
  • Throbbing, pain that lingers, or pain that wakes you up: call us. The nerve may be inflamed, and the tooth needs another look.

When a filling isn't enough

A filling works when there is enough healthy tooth around it to hold it and take the force of chewing. When the damage is bigger, a different treatment protects the tooth better:

  • A crown covers the whole tooth when a large cavity, a cracked cusp or a big old filling has left thin, weak walls. See dental crowns.
  • A root canal is needed when decay or a crack reaches the nerve inside the tooth, often with lingering or throbbing pain. The tooth is then usually finished with a crown. See root canal treatment.
  • An extraction is the last option, for a tooth too broken down to restore. See tooth extraction, including how the space can be replaced.

Our post on signs you need a filling or a crown walks through how that call is made. A chipped front tooth without decay is often a job for dental bonding instead, which uses the same tooth-colored material.

Fillings for kids

Cavities in baby teeth are worth treating. Baby teeth hold space for the adult teeth, help children chew and speak, and a cavity left alone can grow fast and become painful or infected. As a family practice, we see kids, teens and adults, so parents and children can often be seen at the same office.

With kids we explain things in simple words, go slowly, and let a parent stay close. Small cavities in children are often filled with the same tooth-colored material we use for adults. Sealants and fluoride help prevent the next one. Dr. Rana is a general dentist, so if a child needs extensive treatment or care that is better done in a children's dental office, we refer to a pediatric dentist. Read more on our kids dentist page.

What a filling costs

The price of a filling depends on the tooth and on the size of the repair, so we confirm the exact price in writing before treatment. The main things that change it:

  • How many surfaces of the tooth the filling covers, from one small surface to several
  • Which tooth: front teeth and back molars are built differently
  • How deep the decay goes and whether a protective liner is needed
  • Whether an old filling has to be removed first
  • How many teeth are treated in the same visit
  • Whether you choose oral sedation

The single biggest way to keep the cost down is to treat a cavity while it is small. A small filling now is usually far simpler than a crown or a root canal later. For larger plans, 0% financing is available on approved credit through CareCredit, Cherry and LendingClub; see financing.

Insurance and fillings

We accept most major dental insurance. Many plans cover preventive visits at a high percentage and treat fillings as basic services, paid at a lower percentage after your deductible. Some plans pay for a tooth-colored filling on a back tooth only at the rate of a silver filling, which leaves you a difference to pay. Many plans also have waiting periods for basic services and annual maximums that are often in the $1,000 to $2,000 range.

A filling replaced only for appearance may not be covered at all. Bring your insurance details to your visit and we check your benefits for you.

Preventing the next cavity

Fillings fix damage; habits prevent it. These are the steps that make the biggest difference for most people:

  • Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, and spit rather than rinse so the fluoride stays on your teeth
  • Clean between your teeth once a day with floss or small brushes, since that is where many adult cavities start
  • Watch how often you snack or sip; constant small amounts of sugar, juice, soda or sweetened coffee keep teeth under acid attack all day
  • Drink water, especially if your mouth often feels dry, since some medicines reduce saliva
  • Keep up regular checkups and teeth cleanings so small problems are caught early
  • Ask about sealants for children's back teeth and prescription fluoride if you get cavities often

Questions, answered

How do I know if I need a filling?

Common signs are brief sensitivity to sweets or cold, a twinge when biting, or a dark spot or hole you can see or feel. Many cavities cause no symptoms at all, especially between teeth, which is why an exam and X-rays are the reliable way to know.

Do fillings hurt?

The tooth is numbed with local anesthetic first, so most people feel pressure and vibration but not pain. Some soreness or sensitivity for a few days afterward is common. If you are nervous, tell us and we will go at your pace; oral sedation is also available.

How long do tooth-colored fillings last?

Many tooth-colored fillings last for years, and some much longer. How long yours lasts depends on its size, where it is, your bite, grinding, and how well the area is cleaned. Regular checkups let us spot wear early and repair it before the tooth is damaged.

Is it normal for a tooth to be sensitive after a filling?

Yes, mild sensitivity to cold or biting for a few days to a few weeks is common and usually fades. If your bite feels high, call us for a quick adjustment. Throbbing pain, pain that lingers, or pain that wakes you at night should be checked.

Should I replace my old silver fillings?

Not just because they are silver. The ADA considers amalgam safe, and removing a sound filling also removes some healthy tooth. We recommend replacing a filling when it is cracked, leaking, or has decay underneath, and you can choose to replace one for appearance after we explain the tradeoffs.

How much does a filling cost in Miami?

It depends on which tooth, how many surfaces need repair, and whether an old filling has to come out first. We confirm the exact price in writing before treatment. We accept most major dental insurance, and 0% financing is available on approved credit for larger plans.

Does insurance cover tooth-colored fillings?

Many plans cover fillings as a basic service, after the deductible and up to the annual maximum. Some plans pay for a tooth-colored filling on a back tooth only at the silver-filling rate, so you may owe the difference. We check your benefits for you.
