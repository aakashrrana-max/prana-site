Gingivitis or periodontitis: which one do you have?

Gum disease is inflammation and infection of the tissues around your teeth. It is driven by plaque, the sticky film of bacteria that forms on teeth every day. Plaque that stays put hardens into tartar (also called calculus), and a toothbrush can't remove tartar. The gums react by swelling, reddening and bleeding.

That first stage is gingivitis. It affects only the gum tissue, and the bone underneath is still intact. With a professional cleaning and steady home care, gingivitis usually clears up. Periodontitis is the later stage. The inflammation works below the gumline, the gum starts to pull away from the tooth, and the bone that anchors the tooth begins to break down. Lost bone generally doesn't grow back on its own, so treatment aims to stop the disease and keep it stable.

Most people can't tell which stage they are in by looking. Early gum disease rarely hurts, and bleeding can look the same at both stages. Our article on gingivitis vs periodontitis goes deeper into the difference.

Signs of gum disease to watch for

Gum disease often builds quietly for years. These are the signs that tell us it is time for a proper gum exam:

  • Bleeding when you brush or floss. Healthy gums don't usually bleed from normal brushing. Pink in the sink is the most common early sign, even when nothing hurts.
  • Red, puffy or tender gums. Healthy gum tissue is usually firm and pale pink and fits snugly around each tooth.
  • Receding gums. Teeth that look longer than they used to, or new dark triangles between teeth, can mean the gum and bone are pulling back.
  • Bad breath or a bad taste that keeps coming back. Bacteria in deep pockets produce odors that mints and mouthwash only cover for a while.
  • Pus between a tooth and the gum. This is a sign of active infection and should be checked soon.
  • Loose or shifting teeth. Teeth that move, spread apart, or a bite that suddenly feels different can mean bone support has been lost.
  • Sensitivity near the gumline. When gums recede, the root surface is exposed, and roots tend to react to cold.

Smoking reduces blood flow to the gums, so smokers can have active gum disease with little or no bleeding. That is one reason measurements matter more than how your gums look in the mirror.

The gum exam: what pocket measurements tell us

Between each tooth and the gum there is a small groove, called a sulcus or pocket. In healthy gums it is shallow. During a gum exam, a thin, blunt measuring tool called a periodontal probe is slid gently into that groove at several points around every tooth, and the depth is recorded in millimeters. Most people feel light pressure. Inflamed gums can feel tender, and we can numb an area if you need it.

Here is roughly how the numbers are read:

  • 1 to 3 mm: usually a healthy pocket, especially if it doesn't bleed when measured.
  • 4 mm: a warning sign. It may be swelling from gingivitis or the start of periodontitis, and X-rays help tell which.
  • 5 mm or more: a deeper pocket where tartar and bacteria sit below what brushing and a regular cleaning can reach.
  • Also recorded: which spots bleed, how far the gums have receded, whether any teeth are loose, and whether bone loss shows between the roots of back teeth.

Pocket depths alone don't show the bone. X-rays do, so a full diagnosis usually needs both. Your first visit is free and covers an exam, a digital 3D scan and a smile preview. X-rays are a separate step, and we confirm their cost with you before they are taken.

We show you your numbers and keep them on file. At later visits you can see for yourself whether each area is getting shallower, holding steady or getting deeper, which takes much of the guesswork out of deciding what comes next.

Scaling and root planing (deep cleaning), step by step

When pockets are deep and tartar has built up below the gumline, a routine polish can't reach it. The standard first treatment for periodontitis is scaling and root planing, usually called a deep cleaning. Dr. Rana performs it in our office as part of our general dentistry care. For how it differs from a routine visit, see deep cleaning vs regular cleaning.

1. Planning the visits

The mouth is divided into four sections called quadrants. Depending on how many need treatment, a deep cleaning is often split over two visits, one side of the mouth at a time, or done in one longer visit. Splitting it means you can still chew comfortably on the other side. We plan it around your schedule, including evenings and Saturdays once we open.

2. Numbing the area

The section being treated is numbed with local anesthetic, the same type used for a filling. Once it has taken effect, most people feel pressure, vibration and water rather than sharp discomfort. If dental visits make you anxious, oral sedation is available; our page on sedation dentistry explains how it works and who it suits.

3. Scaling

Using an ultrasonic scaler and fine hand instruments, plaque and tartar are removed from the tooth surfaces above and below the gumline, all the way to the bottom of each pocket. The ultrasonic tip vibrates deposits loose while a stream of water flushes them away.

4. Root planing

Next, the root surfaces are smoothed. Rough patches on a root give bacteria a place to cling. A smoother root makes it harder for plaque to settle back in and gives the gum a better chance to tighten up against the tooth as it heals.

5. Rinsing and instructions

The pockets are rinsed, and before you leave we go over what is normal over the next few days and how to clean the treated areas without irritating them. Each session usually takes about an hour, sometimes more for a heavily affected mouth.

6. The re-check

Gums need time to respond, so we measure again a few weeks later, often somewhere around four to eight weeks. In many people the pockets get shallower and bleeding drops a lot. Areas that haven't improved get a closer look, and that is the point where we talk about whether a referral makes sense.

After a deep cleaning: what's normal

Most people go back to work or their day right after a deep cleaning. Here is what to expect while your gums settle down:

  • Numbness usually wears off within a few hours. Wait until it does before eating, so you don't bite your cheek or tongue.
  • Gums are often sore or tender for a day or a few days. An over-the-counter pain reliever, taken as the label directs and if it is safe for you, is usually enough.
  • Sensitivity to cold is common for days to a few weeks, as root surfaces that were covered in tartar are now clean. A sensitivity toothpaste helps many people.
  • A little bleeding in the first day or two is expected.
  • As swelling goes down, the gums can look slightly lower on the teeth. That is the inflammation leaving, not new damage.
  • Warm salt water rinses can soothe the area. Keep brushing gently the same day, and go back to cleaning between your teeth as soon as it feels comfortable.
  • Smoking slows gum healing. If you can avoid it for at least the first few days, your gums will thank you.

Maintenance cleanings: keeping it under control

Periodontitis is managed over time, a bit like blood pressure. After a deep cleaning, most people move to periodontal maintenance visits, often every three to four months instead of every six. The bacteria behind gum disease tend to regroup in deep areas within a few months, and a shorter interval keeps them from getting a foothold again.

A maintenance visit includes cleaning above and below the gumline, re-measuring the areas we are watching, and checking how your home care is going. If your gums stay stable for a long stretch, the interval can sometimes be lengthened. If you only had gingivitis, a regular teeth cleaning schedule is usually enough.

Skipping maintenance is the most common reason gum disease comes back. A deep cleaning resets the clock, but it doesn't stop the clock.

Home care that actually helps your gums

What you do between visits matters as much as what we do in the chair. These habits make the biggest difference for most people:

  • Brush twice a day for two minutes with a soft brush, angling the bristles toward the gumline where plaque collects.
  • Clean between your teeth every day. Floss works for tight contacts. Small interdental brushes often work better where gums have receded and spaces have opened. A water flosser is a good extra, especially around bridges and crowns.
  • An electric toothbrush helps many people clean more evenly, but a manual brush used well works too.
  • An antimicrobial mouthrinse can be an add-on. It doesn't replace brushing and cleaning between teeth.
  • Don't stop flossing because your gums bleed. Bleeding usually improves within a week or two of steady, gentle cleaning. If it doesn't, tell us.
  • Replace your toothbrush or brush head about every three months, or sooner if the bristles splay.

Risk factors for gum disease

Plaque is the cause, but some things make gums more likely to break down or slower to heal:

  • Smoking and vaping. Smoking is one of the strongest known risk factors for periodontitis. It also hides bleeding and slows healing after treatment.
  • Diabetes. Poorly controlled blood sugar is linked with more severe gum disease, and gum inflammation may make blood sugar harder to manage. If you have diabetes, tell us how well controlled it is.
  • Hormone changes. Pregnancy and puberty can make gums more reactive to plaque.
  • Some medications. Medicines that cause dry mouth, and a few that cause gum overgrowth, can make plaque harder to control.
  • Family history. Some people are simply more susceptible and need closer monitoring.
  • Hard-to-clean spots. Crowded teeth and old fillings or crowns with rough edges trap plaque where a brush struggles to reach.

Having a risk factor doesn't mean you will get gum disease. It means regular measurements are worth it, so changes are caught early.

Gum disease and your overall health

Researchers have found associations between periodontitis and several health conditions, including heart disease, diabetes and some pregnancy complications. An association is not proof that one causes the other, and treating your gums is not a treatment for heart disease. What is reasonably well supported is that periodontitis is a long-lasting source of inflammation, and that diabetes and gum disease can make each other worse. Our article on gum disease and heart health covers what the research does and doesn't show.

Before treatment, tell us about your health conditions and every medication you take. If you have diabetes or a heart condition, are pregnant, or take blood thinners, we may coordinate with your doctor first.

When we refer you to a periodontist

Dr. Rana is a general dentist. He does gum exams, cleanings, deep cleanings and maintenance visits himself. A periodontist is a dentist with additional years of training focused on the gums and the bone around teeth, including gum surgery. We refer you to one when:

  • Pockets stay deep or keep bleeding after a deep cleaning and re-check
  • X-rays show advanced bone loss, or several teeth are noticeably loose
  • Gum surgery may help, such as procedures to reach deep pockets or to graft gum over exposed roots
  • Gum disease is moving fast despite good home care, or started at a young age
  • Your medical history makes the case more complex

If we refer you, we send your measurements and X-rays so you don't start from zero, and we tell you plainly why we think you need it. In many cases people go on to alternate maintenance visits between the two offices, if the periodontist agrees. If a tooth can't be saved, we talk through replacing it once the gums are healthy, from a bridge to dental implants.

What gum disease treatment costs in Miami

We can't quote a fair number before measuring your gums, because two people told they have gum disease can need very different care. One may need a single area treated; another may need the whole mouth, plus frequent maintenance for years. The exact price is confirmed in writing before any treatment. The main things that move the cost:

  • How many quadrants need deep cleaning. One or two areas cost less than all four.
  • How severe it is. Deeper pockets and heavier tartar take more time per area.
  • X-rays. Which images you need depends on what the exam shows and what you already have from another dentist.
  • Sedation. Oral sedation is optional and priced separately.
  • Maintenance. Visits every three or four months are an ongoing cost, so ask us to include a year of them when you compare plans.
  • Referral care. If you need a periodontist, their fees are set and billed by their office.

Gum disease questions, answered

Can gum disease be reversed?

Gingivitis, the early stage, can usually be reversed with a professional cleaning and good daily brushing and flossing. Periodontitis can't be fully reversed, because bone that has been lost generally doesn't grow back on its own. It can often be stopped and kept stable with a deep cleaning and regular maintenance visits.

Does a deep cleaning hurt?

The area is numbed with local anesthetic first, so most people feel pressure and vibration rather than pain during the cleaning. Gums are often tender for a day or a few days afterward, and teeth can be sensitive to cold for a while. Oral sedation is available if you are anxious about dental work.

Do I really need a deep cleaning, or can I just get a regular cleaning?

It depends on your measurements. A regular cleaning works above and just below the gumline, which is right for healthy gums and gingivitis. If you have pockets of 5 mm or more with tartar below the gumline, a regular cleaning can't reach it, and the disease is likely to keep progressing. We show you the numbers so you can see why we are recommending it.

How often do I need cleanings after a deep cleaning?

Many people need periodontal maintenance visits every three to four months, at least at first. The bacteria that cause gum disease tend to return to deep areas within a few months. If your gums stay stable for a long time, the interval can sometimes be stretched.

Why do my gums bleed when I brush?

The most common reason is inflammation from plaque along the gumline, which is gingivitis. Brushing too hard, a new flossing habit, hormone changes and some medications can also play a part. Bleeding that lasts more than a week or two of gentle, regular cleaning is worth a gum exam.

Can loose teeth from gum disease tighten up?

Sometimes, a little. When inflammation calms down after treatment, some slightly loose teeth firm up. Teeth that have lost a lot of bone usually stay loose, and some may need a periodontist's care or eventually need to be replaced.

Does insurance cover deep cleaning?

Many dental plans cover scaling and root planing as a basic service, usually at a lower percentage than preventive care and after your deductible. Plans can limit how often it is covered and how maintenance visits are counted, and annual maximums apply. We check your benefits for you and give you the price in writing before treatment.
