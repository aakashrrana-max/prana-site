A family dentist who treats kids and teens

Prana is a family practice: Dr. Rana treats kids, teens, parents and grandparents, often in the same visit. Kids dentist vs. pediatric dentist: what's the difference

If you've searched for a pediatric dentist in Miami, it's worth knowing the difference. A board-certified pediatric dentist completes additional specialty training focused entirely on infants, children and patients with special needs. Dr. Rana is a general (family) dentist who sees patients of all ages, including children, for routine and general dental care.

For most kids, that's exactly the right fit. The same gentle, unhurried approach we use with adults, adapted for a child's comfort and attention span. When a child needs specialist-level care, such as significant special needs or a complex case beyond routine treatment, we'll refer to a board-certified pediatric dentist so your child gets the right level of care. We're upfront about this distinction because it matters for your child's care, and we'd rather send you to the right specialist than take on a case outside what a general dentist should handle.

Your child's first dental visit

The ADA and the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry recommend a first dental visit by a child's first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth coming in, whichever comes first. That first visit is mostly about getting your child comfortable in the chair and giving us a chance to check early development, not about intensive treatment. Getting your child used to the sounds, the chair and the team early on tends to make every visit afterward easier, which is really the main goal of that first appointment.

Our guide to your child's first dental visit covers what to expect and how to prepare your child beforehand.

Cleanings, sealants and fluoride

Regular cleanings are just as important for kids as adults, helping catch small issues before they grow. Dental sealants and fluoride treatments are commonly recommended preventive options for children's back teeth and developing enamel, and Dr. Rana will let you know if they make sense for your child based on their specific teeth. We also talk with parents about brushing habits, diet and thumb-sucking or pacifier use, since those everyday habits affect a child's dental development as much as anything that happens in our chair.

Cavities and gentle treatment

Cavities are common in childhood, and finding one isn't cause for alarm. If your child needs a filling, we explain what's happening in age-appropriate terms and keep treatment as calm and quick as possible. We also talk with you about prevention going forward, since one cavity is often a sign it's worth adjusting brushing habits or snacking patterns at home.

Before and at the first visit

Guidance from the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry and the ADA points to a first dental visit around the first tooth or the first birthday. For many babies that is somewhere between 6 and 12 months, but every child is different, and a slightly later start is not a crisis. The point is to begin before there is a problem.

You can start well before that appointment. Wipe your baby's gums with a soft, damp cloth after feeds. Once the first tooth appears, brush it twice a day with a smear of fluoride toothpaste about the size of a grain of rice. Try not to put your baby to bed with a bottle of milk or juice, since liquid pooling around the teeth overnight is a common cause of early cavities.

For babies and toddlers, the visit itself is short. Your child usually sits in your lap, facing you, while Dr. Rana looks at the teeth and gums, checks how the teeth are coming in and talks with you about feeding, teething and brushing. For ideas on preparing at home, read about your child's first dental visit.

Why cavities in baby teeth still matter

Baby teeth fall out, so it is tempting to let a small cavity go. Some of them stay in place until around age 10 to 12, though, and they do real work in the meantime: chewing, clear speech and holding space for the adult teeth underneath. A baby tooth lost early to decay can let neighboring teeth drift and crowd the adult tooth coming in behind it.

Untreated decay can also reach the nerve, cause pain and infection, and in some cases affect the developing adult tooth. Treatment depends on the size and location of the cavity and on how soon the tooth is due to fall out. Small cavities can sometimes be watched or treated with fluoride, while larger ones often need a filling. Read more in why baby teeth cavities matter and what to expect when your child's first cavity is filled.

Sealants and fluoride, in practice

Fluoride varnish is painted onto the teeth in a minute or two. It tastes a little sweet, it sets quickly, and kids can usually go right back to their day, with soft foods and no brushing for a few hours afterward. At home, the toothpaste amount goes up from a rice-grain smear to a pea-size dab at about age three. For the details on how much is enough, see fluoride for kids: how much is too much.

Sealants are a thin coating brushed into the grooves of the back teeth and hardened with a light. No numbing or drilling is needed. They are most often placed on the first adult molars, which come in around age six, and the second adult molars, around age twelve. They can wear or chip over time, so we check them at each visit. Our post on whether dental sealants are worth it covers who benefits most.

Thumb sucking and pacifiers

Sucking a thumb, finger or pacifier is normal for babies and toddlers, and many children stop by about age three or four. The concern grows if a strong habit continues as the adult front teeth start coming in, which can push the top teeth forward or leave a gap between the upper and lower front teeth when your child bites.

Pressure and scolding tend to backfire. Gentle steps work better for most families: praise on good days, a sticker chart, keeping hands busy during screen time, and noticing when the habit happens, such as when your child is tired or bored. We watch how the bite is developing at each checkup and tell you if it is time to do more. More in our post on thumb sucking and pacifiers.

Mouthguards for sports

The ADA recommends a mouthguard for sports with contact or a risk of falls, such as soccer, basketball, football, baseball, martial arts, skateboarding and cycling. A guard can help protect the teeth, lips and tongue, and lowers the chance of a broken or knocked-out tooth.

There are three main kinds. Stock guards come ready to wear but often fit loosely. Boil-and-bite guards are softened in hot water and shaped at home, and fit better. Custom-fitted guards are made from a model of your child's teeth and usually fit most comfortably, which makes kids more likely to keep them in. Growing mouths change, so expect to replace a guard as new teeth come in. If your child wears braces, ask us which type works with them.

If a tooth is knocked out or broken during a game, our emergency dental page explains what to do in the first hour.

Helping anxious kids feel at ease

Many children are nervous about the dentist, often because of something they heard rather than anything that happened to them. We use a simple pattern: tell, show, then do. We tell your child what comes next in plain words, show the tool on a finger or a toy, and only then use it in their mouth. Kids are invited to raise a hand any time they need a break.

At home, keep the talk calm and simple. Skip words like shot, drill or hurt, and avoid promising that nothing will be felt. Reading a picture book about the dentist, or letting your child come along to a parent's cleaning first, helps many kids. Morning and Saturday appointments often go better than visits squeezed in at the end of a long school day.

If a young child needs a lot of treatment, or could only get through it with sedation, a pediatric dentist who focuses on that care is often the better fit, and we will refer you.

When we refer to a pediatric dentist or an orthodontist

Dr. Rana is a family dentist, and most kids' care, from checkups to small fillings, can happen right here. Some situations call for another dentist's training, and we tell you when that is the case.

  • Pediatric dentist: very young children with widespread decay, children who need treatment under sedation or general anesthesia, and children with medical, developmental or behavioral needs that call for a dedicated setting.
  • Orthodontist: the American Association of Orthodontists suggests a first orthodontic check by about age seven. We look for crossbites, severe crowding, jaw growth concerns and front teeth that stick out, and refer you when an evaluation makes sense. For some teens, clear aligners are an option; see Invisalign and Invisalign for teens.
  • Oral surgeon: for wisdom teeth that are deeply impacted or close to a nerve. Many routine cases can be handled here; see wisdom teeth removal.

After a referral, your child can keep coming to us for cleanings and checkups, and we coordinate with the other office.

More questions from parents

Do cavities in baby teeth need to be filled?

Often, yes, though it depends on the size of the cavity and how soon the tooth is due to fall out. Baby teeth hold space for adult teeth and some stay until about age 10 to 12. A very small cavity may be watched or treated with fluoride, while a larger one usually needs a filling to prevent pain and infection.

When should my child stop sucking their thumb?

Many children stop on their own by about age three or four. It becomes more of a concern if a strong habit continues as the adult front teeth come in, around age six. Gentle encouragement usually works better than punishment, and we can check whether the bite is being affected.

Does my child need a mouthguard for sports?

The ADA recommends a mouthguard for sports with contact or a risk of falls, including soccer, basketball, football and martial arts. A well-fitting guard can help protect against broken or knocked-out teeth. Custom-fitted guards usually fit most comfortably, and boil-and-bite guards are a reasonable option for many kids.

Can parents stay in the room during a child's dental visit?

Yes, parents are welcome. Babies and toddlers are often examined while sitting on a parent's lap. As kids get older, some prefer a few minutes on their own, and we ask what works for your family.

When should my child see an orthodontist?

The American Association of Orthodontists suggests a first orthodontic check by about age seven, when problems such as crossbites or crowding can often be spotted. Not every child needs treatment then. We watch your child's bite at each checkup and refer you when an evaluation makes sense.

How much toothpaste should a toddler use?

For children under three, a smear of fluoride toothpaste about the size of a grain of rice, twice a day. From about age three, a pea-size amount. An adult should brush or help brush until the child can do it well, and encourage spitting rather than swallowing.
