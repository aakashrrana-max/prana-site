What teeth grinding is

The medical name for grinding or clenching your teeth is bruxism. It comes in two main forms. Sleep bruxism happens at night, usually without you knowing, and is often noticed by a partner or a dentist first. Awake bruxism is more often clenching than grinding: holding the teeth together while you concentrate, drive, work at a screen or feel stressed.

Some grinding is common and harmless. It becomes a problem when it is frequent or forceful enough to wear teeth down, crack fillings, or leave your jaw muscles sore. That is the point where protecting the teeth, and looking at what is driving the habit, is worth doing. Signs you may be grinding or clenching

Because sleep grinding happens while you are asleep, most people find out from its effects. During an exam, Dr. Rana looks for clues like these:

  • Front teeth that look shorter or flatter than they used to, or edges that are thin and see-through
  • Flat, shiny wear spots on the back teeth that don't match the way you chew
  • Small chips on tooth edges, or fillings and crowns that crack or keep coming loose
  • Teeth that feel sensitive or a little loose without a clear cause like decay or gum disease
  • Tired, tight or sore jaw muscles when you wake up, or a dull headache at the temples
  • Larger, firmer chewing muscles at the angle of the jaw
  • A wavy, scalloped edge on the sides of the tongue, or a white ridge along the inside of the cheeks
  • A partner who hears grinding or clicking sounds at night

None of these proves grinding on its own, and some have other causes. Put together with what you tell us, they help us decide whether a guard is likely to help.

What can make grinding worse

Bruxism usually has more than one cause, and in many people the cause is never fully pinned down. Common contributors include:

  • Stress, anxiety and long, high-pressure workdays
  • Poor or broken sleep, including snoring and sleep-related breathing problems
  • Caffeine late in the day, alcohol and nicotine
  • Some medicines, including certain antidepressants; never stop a medicine on your own, but do mention it to your doctor and to us
  • A new filling or crown that sits high, or a bite that has changed

A guard protects the teeth, but it doesn't remove these triggers. Cutting back on late caffeine, working on sleep and stress, and noticing daytime clenching (lips together, teeth apart is a useful reminder) can all help alongside it.

TMJ: the basics

The temporomandibular joints, or TMJs, are the hinges in front of each ear that let your jaw open, close and slide. Problems with the joints or the muscles around them are often called TMD. Common signs include jaw pain, clicking or popping, a jaw that feels stuck for a moment, pain when chewing, and headaches or ear discomfort with no ear problem behind it.

Grinding and clenching can contribute to jaw muscle pain, and for many people a well-fitted guard, along with soft food for a while, short-term over-the-counter pain relief taken as the label directs, and simple jaw care, can ease symptoms. But TMD has many possible causes, and a guard doesn't help everyone. Clicking without pain often needs no treatment at all.

We refer complex cases. If your jaw locks open or closed, if pain is severe or has lasted for months, if it followed an injury, or if your bite has suddenly changed, Dr. Rana will refer you to a dentist who focuses on orofacial pain, an oral surgeon or your physician for further evaluation. Custom vs store-bought night guards

Pharmacy guards come in two kinds: one-size stock trays, and boil-and-bite guards you soften in hot water and bite into. They can be a reasonable stopgap for a short time. A custom guard is made from a scan of your own teeth and adjusted to your bite in the chair.

A guard that doesn't fit well can rock, fall out at night or press unevenly on some teeth. If you use a store-bought one, stop if it makes your teeth or jaw sore, and bring it to your visit so we can see how you wear it.

Types of custom guards

Hard guards

Made of rigid acrylic, usually for the upper teeth. They tend to last longest and let the jaw glide smoothly, which suits many heavy grinders.

Soft guards

Flexible and gentle for light or occasional clenching. Some people chew on soft material, so they aren't always the right choice for heavy grinding.

Layered (dual) guards

A firm outer surface with a softer lining against the teeth. A common middle ground when comfort and durability both matter.

Upper or lower

Most night guards cover the upper teeth. A lower guard may suit people with a strong gag reflex or certain bites. Dr. Rana suggests one and explains why.

Your visit: from exam to fitting

Getting a custom guard usually takes two short visits.

  • Exam and conversation. Dr. Rana asks about your sleep, headaches, jaw pain, stress and medicines, then checks your teeth for wear, your jaw muscles and joints for tenderness, how far you can open, and how your teeth meet.
  • Digital 3D scan. Instead of a tray of impression putty, a small handheld scanner records your teeth and bite in a few minutes. The scan also gives a baseline, so future scans can show whether wear has slowed.
  • A plan in writing. If a guard makes sense, you hear which type and why, alongside anything else worth fixing, such as worn edges or a cracked filling. The cost is given in writing before anything is made.
  • Made in our in-house lab. The guard is designed from your scan and made by our own lab team, without sending your case to an outside lab. We tell you at your visit when it will be ready.
  • Fitting. At the second visit, Dr. Rana checks that the guard seats fully, isn't tight on any one tooth, and lets your teeth touch evenly. Small adjustments are made in the chair, and you learn how to put it in, take it out and clean it.

Your first visit at Prana is free: an exam, a 3D scan and a smile preview. It is a good starting point if you want to know whether grinding is behind what you are noticing.

Getting used to your guard

Most people need a few nights to a couple of weeks to get used to sleeping with a guard. Extra saliva, a slightly odd bite in the first few minutes after you take it out, and a feeling of fullness are common at first and usually settle. Pain, a guard that feels tighter on one tooth, or a bite that still feels off an hour after you take it out are not normal; call or text and we will adjust it.

Caring for your night guard

  • Brush and floss before you put it in, so you aren't sealing food against your teeth overnight.
  • Each morning, rinse it and brush it gently with a soft toothbrush and cool water. Mild, unscented soap works; regular toothpaste can scratch it.
  • Don't use hot water or leave it in a hot car, which can warp it.
  • Let it dry, then keep it in its vented case. Keep it away from dogs, which love to chew guards.
  • Bring it to your checkups and cleanings so we can look at the wear marks and the fit.
  • Replace it if it cracks, gets thin, smells even after cleaning, or stops fitting snugly. After dental work like a new crown, we may need to adjust it or make a new one.

Sports mouthguards

A sports mouthguard is a different appliance from a night guard. It is thicker, cushions the teeth, lips and jaw against impact, and is made for wearing while you are awake and breathing hard. A night guard isn't designed to protect against a ball or an elbow, and a sports guard isn't meant for sleeping in.

Dental groups recommend mouthguards for contact and collision sports such as football, basketball, hockey, soccer, martial arts and boxing, and for skateboarding and similar activities. Custom sports guards made from a 3D scan tend to stay in place and let you speak and breathe more easily than generic ones. Kids and teens outgrow them as their teeth and jaws change, so we check the fit as they grow; see our kids dentistry page. If a tooth does get knocked out, follow our guide to the first 30 minutes.

A note on snoring and sleep apnea

Grinding and sleep-related breathing problems sometimes go together. A night guard isn't a treatment for snoring or sleep apnea, and for some people with sleep apnea a standard guard may not be the right appliance. If you snore loudly, stop breathing or gasp in your sleep, wake up unrefreshed or feel very sleepy during the day, please talk to your physician, who can arrange a sleep evaluation. Dr. Rana doesn't diagnose sleep apnea, but he will mention signs he notices and refer you to your physician. More in our post on sleep apnea and oral health.

Repairing teeth that grinding has already worn

A guard protects what is left, and it is usually the first step before repairing worn teeth, so new work isn't ground down too. Small chips and worn edges can often be rebuilt with dental bonding. Teeth that are cracked or heavily worn may need crowns, made in our in-house lab. Severe wear across many teeth needs a careful, step-by-step plan, and we talk through options and priorities with you rather than doing everything at once.

Questions, answered

Do I really need a night guard?

Not everyone who grinds needs one. A guard is usually suggested when grinding is wearing your teeth, damaging fillings or crowns, or leaving your jaw sore. An exam can show whether the wear pattern points to grinding, and Dr. Rana will tell you honestly if a guard isn't needed yet.

Is a custom night guard worth it compared to a store-bought one?

For regular nightly use, many people find a custom guard fits better, is thinner and more comfortable, and lasts longer, which makes them more likely to keep wearing it. A store-bought guard costs less upfront and can work as a short-term stopgap. Stop using any guard that makes your teeth or jaw hurt.

How long does a night guard last?

It depends on the material and how hard you grind. Soft guards may wear out within a year or so for heavy grinders, while hard acrylic guards often last several years with good care. Bring your guard to checkups so we can see when it needs replacing.

Can a night guard fix TMJ?

A guard can ease jaw muscle pain linked to grinding or clenching for many people, but it doesn't cure every jaw joint problem. TMJ disorders have many causes. Complex cases, such as a jaw that locks or pain lasting months, are referred for further evaluation.

Should a night guard go on the top or bottom teeth?

Most night guards are made for the upper teeth, but a lower guard can suit people with a strong gag reflex or certain bites. Either can protect the teeth when it is made and adjusted properly. Dr. Rana recommends one based on your bite and comfort.

Can I wear a night guard if I have sleep apnea?

Talk to your physician first. A standard night guard doesn't treat sleep apnea, and for some people with sleep apnea it may not be the right appliance. If you have been diagnosed or have symptoms like loud snoring and gasping at night, tell us so we can plan with your physician's advice.

Does dental insurance cover night guards?

Some plans pay part of the cost of a night guard for grinding, often with conditions like a documented diagnosis or a limit on how often it is replaced, and some don't cover them. Most major dental insurance is accepted here, and we check your benefits for you.
