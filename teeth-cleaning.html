What's included in a cleaning visit

A standard visit starts with an exam from Dr. Rana, checking your teeth, gums and bite for anything that needs attention. Digital X-rays are taken as needed to look below the gumline and between teeth for issues you can't see or feel yet. From there, we remove plaque and tartar buildup, polish your teeth, and walk you through anything worth watching before your next visit. We also check your bite, look for early signs of wear, and screen soft tissue in the mouth as part of every routine visit.

Everything is explained in plain language as we go. What we're seeing, why it matters, and what (if anything) needs to happen next. You'll never leave wondering what just happened or why. See our full range of general dentistry care for how routine visits fit into the bigger picture of your health.

Regular cleaning vs. deep cleaning

Most patients just need a regular cleaning: removing plaque and tartar above and slightly below the gumline to keep gums healthy. If gum disease has taken hold, deeper pockets, bleeding, bone loss on X-rays, Dr. Rana may recommend scaling and root planing, often called a deep cleaning. It's a more thorough cleaning below the gumline, sometimes done in stages with local numbing for comfort.

We won't recommend a deep cleaning unless your gums actually need it, and we'll explain exactly what we're seeing and why before any treatment starts.

How often you should come in

For most people, a professional cleaning every six months is the right rhythm. Enough to stay ahead of plaque and catch small problems early. Some patients do better with more frequent visits, including those with a history of gum disease, smokers, or anyone managing a condition that affects healing. Dr. Rana will recommend a schedule based on your actual mouth, not a one-size-fits-all rule. Skipping visits for a year or more doesn't save time in the long run. Small issues caught early are almost always simpler and less costly to treat than the same problem left to grow.

Regular cleaning or deep cleaning: how the call gets made

The choice comes down to measurements, not a hunch. With a thin, marked probe we check the small space between each tooth and the gum around it. Healthy gums usually measure about 1 to 3 millimeters, and they don't bleed much when touched. Readings of 4 millimeters or more, bleeding, loose teeth or bone loss on X-rays point toward gum disease.

If your gums are healthy or only a little inflamed (gingivitis), a regular cleaning plus better home care is usually enough to settle them. If the pockets are deeper and tartar has built up on the roots (periodontitis), a deep cleaning, called scaling and root planing, cleans below the gumline area by area, usually with numbing. Our gum disease treatment page covers that process, and deep cleaning vs regular cleaning goes further into the difference.

Once a deep cleaning is done, the usual next step is a maintenance cleaning every three or four months or so, which helps keep the pockets from deepening again. If your gums need surgery or the disease is advanced, we refer you to a periodontist and keep seeing you for routine care.

What happens at a cleaning, step by step

A routine adult cleaning with an exam often takes about 45 minutes to an hour, longer if it has been years or X-rays are due. Here is the usual order:

  • Health check-in: any new medicines, blood thinners, diabetes, pregnancy or recent surgery, since these can change how we clean and what we watch for.
  • A look around: teeth, gums, tongue, cheeks and the floor of the mouth, plus a check for signs of grinding.
  • X-rays, if due: only the images your history and exam call for.
  • Gum measurements: pocket depths around each tooth, recorded so changes show up next time.
  • Scaling: hand instruments and, for many people, an ultrasonic tool that uses vibration and a water spray to loosen hardened tartar.
  • Flossing and polishing: to clear what is left between teeth and smooth the surfaces so plaque has a harder time sticking.
  • Fluoride, if it helps: a quick varnish for people with a higher cavity risk.
  • The wrap-up: what Dr. Rana found, what to watch, and when to come back.

Dental X-rays: why and how often

X-rays show what an exam can't: cavities forming where two teeth touch, bone levels around the roots, infections at the tip of a root and teeth that haven't come in. Our post on cavities between teeth shows why those spots are so easy to miss.

ADA and FDA guidance says X-ray timing should be based on your own risk, not a fixed calendar. As a rough idea, many adults with healthy teeth and no recent cavities get bitewing X-rays every year or two, sometimes longer, while people with frequent cavities or active gum disease may need them more often. A full set or a panoramic image is usually taken every few years, or when there is a specific reason. Digital X-rays use a low dose of radiation.

If you are pregnant or might be, tell us. Routine X-rays are often postponed, though images needed for a dental problem can still be taken with care. If another office took X-rays in the last year or two, ask them to send copies, since that may save you a repeat.

Why teeth get sensitive, and what helps

Most sensitivity during a cleaning comes from exposed root surfaces, where gums have pulled back, or from enamel worn thin by grinding, acidic drinks or hard brushing. Recent whitening can make teeth touchy for a while too.

A few things help. Brushing with a sensitivity toothpaste for two or three weeks before your visit can calm things down. Tell us which areas bother you, so we can use a topical numbing gel there, change the water temperature or switch from ultrasonic to hand instruments. If a deep cleaning is planned, local anesthetic is normal for that. If one tooth is sharply sensitive, mention it, because it can be a sign of a crack or cavity rather than gum recession.

Cleanings for kids and teens

Children's cleanings are usually shorter and gentler, with plenty of explaining and a chance to see and touch the tools first. Many kids also get fluoride varnish and, when their back adult molars come in, sealants. Our kids' dentist page explains how we approach younger patients.

Teens bring their own challenges. Braces and clear aligners trap plaque around brackets and under trays, sports and energy drinks bathe teeth in sugar and acid, and wisdom teeth start to show on X-rays in the later teen years. A cleaning is a good time to check all three. If your teen is in Invisalign or braces, tell us at check-in.

Aftercare: the rest of the day and the months between

After a regular cleaning

You can usually eat and drink right away. If you had fluoride varnish, follow the instructions you are given, which usually means soft foods and no brushing for a few hours. Gums may feel a little tender and bleed slightly when you brush that evening. That generally settles within a day or two.

After a deep cleaning

Wait until the numbness wears off before eating, so you don't bite your cheek. Some soreness and sensitivity to cold is common for a few days. A warm salt-water rinse and a soft toothbrush help. Call or text us if you have swelling, fever or pain that is getting worse instead of better.

Until your next visit

Brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste, clean between your teeth daily and replace your toothbrush every three or four months. Notice if your mouth often feels dry, since dry mouth raises cavity risk; our post on dry mouth and your teeth explains why. Gums that bleed most days are worth a call before your next cleaning is due.

More teeth cleaning questions

Is a cleaning included in the free first visit?

No. The first visit covers an exam with Dr. Rana, a 3D scan and a smile preview. Cleanings and X-rays are scheduled as a separate appointment, and the exact price is confirmed in writing beforehand, whether you use insurance or pay yourself.

How long does a teeth cleaning take?

A routine cleaning with an exam often takes about 45 minutes to an hour. It can take longer if X-rays are due, if there is heavy tartar or if it has been several years. A deep cleaning is usually split into two or more visits.

How often do I need dental X-rays?

It depends on your risk. Many adults with healthy teeth get bitewing X-rays every year or two, while people with frequent cavities or gum disease may need them more often. Dr. Rana only orders the images your exam and history call for.

Why do my gums bleed when I get my teeth cleaned?

Bleeding during a cleaning usually means the gums are inflamed, most often from plaque along the gumline. Light bleeding that improves with regular brushing and flossing is common with gingivitis. Heavy bleeding, deep pockets or bleeding that never stops between visits can point to gum disease and is worth checking.

Can I eat after a teeth cleaning?

After a regular cleaning, usually yes, right away. If fluoride varnish was applied, you may be asked to stick to soft foods and skip brushing for a few hours. After a deep cleaning with numbing, wait until feeling comes back before you eat.

How much is a teeth cleaning without insurance in Miami?

It depends on whether you need a regular or deep cleaning, which X-rays are due and how much buildup there is, so we don't quote one flat number. You get the exact price in writing after your exam and before the cleaning. Financing on approved credit is available for treatment.
