When a tooth has to come out, and when it can be saved

A tooth that hurts is not automatically a tooth that has to go. Many painful teeth can be kept with a filling, a crown or a root canal, and your own tooth, when it can be restored well, is usually worth keeping. Saving it keeps the root in the bone, keeps your bite stable and avoids the cost and time of replacing it later.

Still, saving a tooth is not always the wiser path just because it is technically possible. These are the situations where removal usually makes more sense:

  • The tooth is cracked or split down into the root, below the gum line, where it can't be sealed
  • Decay has destroyed so much of the tooth that there is nothing solid left for a crown to hold on to
  • Advanced gum disease has taken away much of the bone around it and the tooth is loose
  • An infection keeps coming back and a root canal or retreatment is unlikely to succeed
  • A baby tooth won't fall out and is blocking the adult tooth underneath
  • Teeth are removed as part of an orthodontic plan to make room for crowded teeth
  • A wisdom tooth is causing pain, infection or damage to the molar next to it

When both options are reasonable, we lay out the tradeoffs side by side: how long each is likely to last, what each costs now and later, and what happens to the space. Then you choose. Extractions are one part of our general dentistry care, so the same office that removes a tooth can also restore or replace it.

Simple and surgical extractions

Dentists sort extractions into two broad groups. Which one you need depends on how much of the tooth is showing, the shape of the roots, and how firmly the tooth is held in the bone. Your exam and X-rays usually answer that before the day of treatment.

Simple extraction

A simple extraction is for a tooth that is fully visible above the gum. After numbing, the tooth is gently loosened in its socket with a small instrument and then lifted out. There is usually no cut in the gum. Front teeth and many premolars come out this way, and for many people it is quicker than they expected.

Surgical extraction

A surgical extraction is needed when the tooth has broken off at the gum line, the roots are curved or spread wide, or part of the tooth is still under the gum. A small flap of gum may be lifted, a little bone may be smoothed away, and a back molar may be divided into sections so each root comes out on its own. That sounds like a lot, but it is often gentler on the bone than forcing a whole tooth out in one piece. Stitches are placed when needed.

When we refer to an oral surgeon

Dr. Rana is a general dentist, not an oral surgeon. He removes the teeth that can be taken out safely in a general dental office, which includes many surgical extractions. Some cases belong with an oral surgeon, and we will say so clearly:

  • Teeth deeply held in the bone, or roots sitting right against the nerve in the lower jaw or the sinus above the upper teeth
  • Medical situations that make the procedure more involved, such as a bleeding disorder, some bone-strengthening or cancer medicines, or past radiation to the jaw
  • Patients who need IV sedation or general anesthesia, since we offer oral sedation only
  • Several difficult teeth that are better removed together in a surgical setting

If you are referred, you leave with your images and a plain explanation of why, so you are not starting from zero somewhere else. When the tooth is out, you can come back to us to plan the replacement.

Comfort: numbing and oral sedation

Every extraction starts with local anesthetic, and we test the area before we begin. You should feel pressure and some pushing, but not sharp pain. If you feel anything sharp, raise your hand and we stop and give more anesthetic. Infected teeth can be harder to numb, and we take extra time with them.

If the thought of an extraction makes you tense, oral sedation is available. It is a prescribed pill taken before the visit that helps you feel calm and drowsy while you stay awake and able to respond. You will need someone to drive you home, and we give you written instructions about eating and medicines beforehand. Our team speaks English and Spanish, and we tell you what is happening before each step, so nothing comes as a surprise.

Before your extraction: health history and imaging

A few minutes of history makes the procedure safer. Please tell us about every medicine and supplement you take, and especially about:

  • Blood thinners or aspirin taken daily
  • Medicines for osteoporosis or cancer that act on bone
  • Heart conditions, artificial heart valves or joint replacements
  • Diabetes, pregnancy, or a weakened immune system
  • Smoking or vaping, which slows healing and raises the risk of dry socket

Don't stop any prescribed medicine on your own before an extraction. When it matters, we coordinate with your physician.

New patients start with a free first visit: an exam, a digital 3D scan and a smile preview. To see the roots and the bone around them, we also take dental X-rays, and for some teeth a CBCT, a 3D X-ray. Those images are separate from the first visit, and their cost is confirmed with you before they are taken.

What happens at the visit, step by step

Most people feel calmer once they know the order of things. A typical extraction appointment goes like this:

  • Check-in and a final look. We go over your health history and images again and answer any last questions. If you chose oral sedation, it is already working by now.
  • Numbing. A gel goes on the gum first, then the local anesthetic. We wait until the tooth and gum are fully numb.
  • Loosening the tooth. The tooth is gently rocked and widened in its socket. This is the part where you feel pressure.
  • Removal. The tooth is lifted out, in one piece or in sections for a surgical extraction.
  • Cleaning the socket. The socket is cleaned and checked. If you are planning an implant, a bone graft may be placed now to help keep the bone for later.
  • Stitches and gauze. Stitches are placed if needed, and you bite on gauze to help a clot form.
  • Instructions. You go home with written aftercare in English or Spanish and a way to reach us with questions.

Time in the chair varies. A simple extraction is often over quickly, while a surgical one takes longer. Plan to keep the rest of the day light.

Aftercare and avoiding dry socket

Good healing depends on one small thing: the blood clot that forms in the empty socket. It protects the bone and nerve underneath while new tissue grows in. Most of the aftercare rules are really about keeping that clot in place.

The first 24 hours

  • Bite firmly on the gauze for about 30 to 45 minutes, and use a fresh piece if oozing continues
  • Rest, and keep your head raised when you lie down
  • Hold a cold pack on the cheek for short stretches to limit swelling
  • Skip rinsing, spitting, straws, smoking and vaping
  • Choose cool or lukewarm soft food and chew on the other side

The next several days

  • After the first day, rinse gently with warm salt water a few times a day, especially after meals, and let it fall from your mouth rather than spitting
  • Brush your other teeth as usual and go softly near the socket
  • Keep to soft foods such as eggs, yogurt, rice, mashed plantains or soup that isn't hot, and add firmer food as it feels comfortable
  • Mild soreness and some swelling for two or three days is normal and should ease a little each day

Dry socket: what to watch for

Dry socket happens when the clot comes loose or breaks down too early and the bone is left exposed. The tell-tale sign is pain that gets worse around day two to four instead of better, often spreading toward the ear, sometimes with a bad taste or a socket that looks empty. It is more common after lower back teeth and in people who smoke. It is very treatable: call us and we clean the socket and place a soothing dressing. Our guide on how to prevent dry socket goes deeper on the habits that protect the clot.

When to call right away

  • Bleeding that keeps going after an hour of firm pressure on gauze
  • Fever, pus, or swelling that grows after the third day
  • Numbness that hasn't worn off by the next day
  • Trouble breathing or swallowing, or swelling spreading toward the eye or neck: go to the ER

Replacing the tooth afterward

Not every pulled tooth needs to be replaced. Wisdom teeth and teeth removed for braces usually don't. For most other teeth, though, a gap changes things over time: the neighbors tend to tip into the space, the tooth above or below can drift toward it, chewing shifts to the other side, and the bone where the root used to be slowly shrinks. That is why we talk about the replacement before the extraction, not months later.

Planning ahead can also change how the tooth comes out. If an implant is likely, we take care to protect the bone during removal and may suggest a graft in the socket. In some cases an implant can go in on the same day as the extraction; more often the site heals first. It depends on your bone, any infection and your health.

What a tooth extraction costs

We don't quote an extraction price before seeing the tooth, because two extractions can be very different jobs. You get the exact price in writing before any treatment. These are the things that move it:

  • Simple or surgical: a tooth that needs sectioning, a gum flap or stitches takes more time and skill
  • Which tooth: a single-rooted front tooth is usually simpler than a three-rooted molar
  • The imaging needed, such as X-rays or a CBCT
  • Whether you choose oral sedation
  • A bone graft in the socket, if you are planning an implant
  • The replacement tooth afterward, which is a separate cost
  • A referral to an oral surgeon, whose office sets its own fees

Insurance and extractions

We accept most major dental insurance. Many plans treat a needed extraction as a basic service and pay a share of it after your deductible, while some group surgical extractions with oral surgery, which can have its own percentage or a waiting period. Many plans also have annual maximums that are often in the $1,000 to $2,000 range.

Bring your insurance details and we check your benefits for you.

Questions, answered

Can a tooth be saved instead of pulled?

Often, yes. A tooth with deep decay or an infected nerve can frequently be kept with a root canal and a crown. Extraction usually makes more sense when the tooth is split below the gum, too broken down to restore, or loose from advanced gum disease. An exam and X-rays tell us which applies to your tooth.

Does getting a tooth pulled hurt?

The tooth and gum are fully numbed first, so you should feel pressure but not sharp pain. If you are anxious, oral sedation is available. Some soreness for a few days afterward is normal and usually eases with over-the-counter pain relief as directed.

How long does it take to heal after a tooth extraction?

Most people feel much better within a few days, and the gum closes over the socket in a few weeks. The bone underneath keeps filling in for a few months. Surgical extractions and back teeth usually take a little longer than simple ones.

How much does a tooth extraction cost in Miami?

It depends on whether the extraction is simple or surgical, which tooth it is, the imaging needed and whether you choose sedation. We confirm the exact price in writing before treatment. We accept most major dental insurance, and 0% financing is available on approved credit.

From Monday, November 16, 2026, we offer same-day appointments Monday to Saturday, 8am to 8pm; call or text (786) 579-0521 first. Many teeth can be removed at that visit, while some are better treated after swelling settles or are referred to an oral surgeon. For spreading swelling, fever or trouble breathing or swallowing, go to the ER.

What can I eat after a tooth extraction?

Stick to soft, cool or lukewarm food for the first day or two, such as yogurt, eggs, soup that isn't hot, mashed potatoes or rice. Chew on the other side and avoid straws, crunchy snacks and seeds for about a week. Most people are back to normal food within a week or so.

How soon after an extraction can I get a dental implant?

It depends on your bone, any infection and your overall health. Sometimes an implant can be placed the same day as the extraction, but more often the socket heals for a period first, sometimes with a bone graft. We plan the timing with you before the tooth comes out.
