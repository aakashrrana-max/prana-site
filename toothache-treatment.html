What usually causes a toothache

A tooth hurts when something irritates the nerve inside it or the tissue around it. Most of the time the cause is one of a handful of familiar problems, and many of them are easy to treat when they are caught early. Here are the ones we see most often in adults and kids:

  • Tooth decay. A cavity that has worked through the hard outer enamel into the softer layer underneath often makes a tooth sensitive to sweets and cold. If it keeps going, it can reach the nerve.
  • An inflamed or infected nerve. Deep decay, a crack or an old, large filling can let bacteria reach the pulp, the soft core of the tooth. This is the kind of pain that throbs or keeps you up.
  • A cracked or broken tooth. Cracks can be too small to see and still hurt sharply when you bite or release.
  • An abscess. A pocket of infection at the root tip or in the gum, often with swelling, tenderness or a bad taste. See our page on dental abscess treatment.
  • Gum problems. Food wedged between teeth, inflamed gums or a gum pocket around one tooth can feel a lot like tooth pain.
  • Exposed roots. When gums pull back, the root surface can react to cold air, cold drinks or brushing.
  • A wisdom tooth coming in. Gum that is swollen over a partly erupted back tooth can ache and make it hard to open wide.
  • Grinding or clenching. Heavy force at night can leave several teeth sore in the morning, along with tired jaw muscles.
  • Sinus pressure. The roots of the upper back teeth sit close to the sinuses, so a cold or sinus infection can make several upper teeth ache at once.
  • Recent dental work. Some sensitivity for a few days after a filling or crown is common. Pain that is getting worse, or a bite that feels high, should be checked.

What different kinds of tooth pain can mean

The way a tooth hurts gives useful clues, but it is only a clue. Two people with the same kind of pain can have different problems, and some serious problems hurt very little. Use this table to understand what you are feeling and how soon to call, not to diagnose yourself.

Safe relief until you can be seen

Home care can make the wait easier, but it doesn't treat the cause. If the pain is worst at bedtime, our post on toothache at night has a few extra tips. These steps are generally safe for most adults. If you are pregnant, take other medicines, have stomach, kidney or liver problems, or take a blood thinner, ask a pharmacist or your doctor which pain reliever is safe for you.

What can help

  • An over-the-counter pain reliever, taken as directed on the label. Stick to the dose and timing on the package, even if the pain is bad. Taking more than the label says doesn't fix the tooth and can cause real harm.
  • A cold compress on the outside of the cheek. Wrap ice or a cold pack in a thin cloth and hold it on for 15 to 20 minutes, then take a break. It can calm throbbing and limit swelling.
  • A gentle rinse. Swish lukewarm water, or water with a little salt dissolved in it, to clear food and soothe the gum.
  • Careful flossing. A seed or a piece of popcorn shell stuck at the gumline can cause a surprising amount of pain. Floss gently around the sore tooth once.
  • Chew on the other side, and keep away from very hot, very cold or sugary food and drinks that set the tooth off.
  • Numbing gel, for adults only. An over-the-counter oral gel can dull the area for a short time if you follow the label. Don't use these products on babies or toddlers.

What to avoid

  • Don't put an aspirin tablet, or any crushed pill, on the gum or tooth. It doesn't work that way and can burn the tissue.
  • Don't put heat on a swollen face or jaw.
  • Don't poke at the tooth or gum, or try to open a swelling yourself.
  • Don't take antibiotics left over from another illness or prescribed for someone else. They may be the wrong drug or dose, and they don't fix the source of the pain.
  • Don't wait it out because the pain eased. A tooth that stops hurting can still be infected.

When a toothache is an ER problem

Most toothaches belong with a dentist. Some don't. Go to the ER now, or call 911, if a toothache comes with any of these:

  • Swelling that is spreading toward your eye or down your neck
  • A fever along with the tooth pain or swelling
  • Trouble breathing or trouble swallowing
  • Bleeding from the mouth that firm pressure doesn't stop
  • A blow to the face, such as from a fall, a crash or a sports hit

The ER can treat a spreading infection and check for injuries, and you will still need a dentist afterward to fix the tooth itself. What happens at your toothache visit

The goal of the first visit is to find the cause, relieve the pain, and give you clear options. A typical visit goes like this:

  • Your story. Dr. Rana asks what the pain is like, how long you have had it, and about your health and medicines.
  • A close look. He checks the tooth, the gums, your bite and the teeth around it, and looks for swelling or signs of a crack.
  • Simple tests. A touch of cold, gentle tapping, or biting on a small instrument can show which tooth is involved and how the nerve is doing.
  • Imaging if needed. X-rays show decay between teeth, infection at the root tip and bone around the tooth. Our digital 3D scan can also help show cracks, wear and how the teeth fit together.
  • A plain explanation. You hear what is wrong, what your options are, and what happens if you wait.
  • Cost in writing. Before any treatment starts, you get the options and their cost in writing, and you decide.
  • Relief. When it is safe and you agree, we often start treatment that same visit to take the pressure off the tooth. If more time is needed, we set a temporary fix and book the rest.

If dental visits make you anxious, tell us when you call. Oral sedation is available for suitable patients, and local anesthetic is used to numb the area for treatment.

Possible treatments, depending on the cause

There is no single toothache fix. What Dr. Rana recommends depends on what the exam and X-rays show, how much healthy tooth is left, and what you prefer.

A filling or bonding

When decay hasn't reached the nerve, removing it and placing a tooth-colored filling often settles the pain. A small chip or a worn spot near the gumline can sometimes be covered with dental bonding to calm sensitivity.

Root canal treatment

If the nerve is inflamed beyond recovery or infected, a root canal removes the damaged pulp, cleans and seals the inside of the tooth, and keeps your natural tooth in place. Back teeth usually need a crown afterward to protect them, which our in-house lab makes.

Extraction

Some teeth are too broken down or cracked too deeply to save. In that case a tooth extraction removes the source of pain. We then talk through whether and how to replace the tooth, for example with a bridge or a dental implant, so the teeth around the gap stay stable.

Other fixes

  • Cleaning out a gum pocket or trapped food and treating the inflamed gum
  • Adjusting a filling or crown that sits too high
  • Desensitizing treatment for exposed roots
  • A custom night guard if grinding is behind the soreness
  • Referral to your physician when the cause looks like a sinus problem rather than a tooth

After treatment: what to expect

Most people feel better soon after the cause is treated, but the area can stay tender for a few days, especially after a root canal or extraction. Wait until the numbness wears off before you eat, so you don't bite your cheek or tongue. Chew on the other side for a day or so and keep brushing and flossing gently around the tooth.

If you had a root canal, keep the temporary filling in place and come back for the crown when we recommend, because a back tooth without one is more likely to crack. If you had a tooth removed, follow the aftercare sheet we give you, which explains how to protect the healing socket and lower the risk of dry socket. Call or text us if pain is getting worse instead of better after two or three days, if swelling grows, or if you develop a fever.

Preventing the next toothache

Many toothaches start as small problems that didn't hurt yet. A few habits make a real difference:

  • Brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste and clean between your teeth once a day.
  • Keep sugary drinks and snacks to mealtimes rather than sipping or grazing all day.
  • Come in for regular cleanings and checkups, where small cavities and cracks can be found before they reach the nerve.
  • Get sensitivity checked early instead of living with it.
  • Wear a night guard if you grind, and a mouthguard for contact sports.
  • Replace old fillings or crowns that are chipped, leaking or loose, when your dentist recommends it.

Questions, answered

Can a toothache go away on its own?

Pain from a minor irritation, like food stuck in the gum or brief sensitivity after a filling, can settle by itself. Pain from decay, a crack or an infection usually comes back, and it can fade only because the nerve has died. If a toothache lasts more than a day or two, or keeps returning, have it checked.

Why does my tooth hurt when I bite down?

Pain on biting is often linked to a crack in the tooth, a filling or crown that sits a little high, or inflammation around the root tip. It can also come from a sore gum pocket. Chew on the other side and have it examined within a day or two, since cracks can spread with use.

Can a sinus infection cause a toothache?

Yes. The roots of the upper back teeth are close to the sinuses, so sinus pressure can make several upper teeth ache at once, often worse when you bend over. If the ache stays after the sinus clears, or it centers on one tooth, a dentist should check that tooth.

Will I need a root canal for a toothache?

Not always. Many toothaches are fixed with a filling, a gum treatment or a bite adjustment. A root canal is usually recommended when the nerve is inflamed beyond recovery or infected, which tests and X-rays help show. Dr. Rana explains which applies to you before anything is done.

Can a dentist pull a tooth on the same day?

Often, yes, when an extraction is the right choice and it can be done safely in the office. Some cases need a different plan first, for example when there is a spreading infection or the tooth is deeply impacted, and complex surgical cases are referred to an oral surgeon. Call or text first so we can plan the time.

Will the dentist give me antibiotics for a toothache?

Only if the dentist decides they are appropriate, for example when an infection is spreading or you have certain health conditions. Antibiotics don't fix the cause of a toothache, so the tooth still needs treatment. Please don't take leftover antibiotics or someone else's prescription.

Does dental insurance cover toothache treatment?

Many plans pay toward the exam, X-rays and treatment such as fillings, root canals or extractions, but how much depends on your plan, deductibles, waiting periods and your remaining annual maximum. Most major dental insurance is accepted here, and we check your benefits for you.
