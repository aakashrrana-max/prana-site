Teeth whitening in Miami: what to expect

Teeth whitening works best on natural, healthy tooth enamel, so it isn't always the very first step. If you have untreated cavities, gum disease or built-up tartar, that gets addressed first, often as simply as a teeth cleaning and exam, so the whitening gel can work evenly once your mouth is healthy. Most adults with generally healthy teeth and gums are good candidates, and your free consult is where Dr. Rana confirms that's the case for you before any whitening plan is scheduled.

Results vary from person to person. Some teeth brighten more dramatically than others depending on their starting shade and the type of staining involved, and Dr. Rana will give you a realistic idea of what to expect at your free consult rather than promising a set number of shades. Some sensitivity is common with any whitening treatment, but it's manageable. The process is supervised so gel stays off your gums, and a desensitizing step is built in for anyone whose teeth tend to react.

One thing teeth whitening can't do is change the color of crowns, veneers or fillings, since it only works on natural enamel. If your smile includes any restorations, mention that at your consult so your plan accounts for it, whether that means whitening your natural teeth to match, or looking at veneers or the fuller range of cosmetic dentistry options for a more uniform result. Either way, nothing is scheduled without a written plan and price first, so you know exactly what you're getting before you commit.

In-office, take-home or store-bought whitening: how they compare

All three rely on a peroxide gel that breaks down stain molecules inside the enamel. What changes is the strength of the gel, how well it fits your teeth, who is watching, and how quickly you see a difference.

Who whitening won't work for

Whitening only lightens natural tooth structure. In a few situations a different plan, or a different order of steps, makes more sense:

  • Crowns, veneers, bonding and tooth-colored fillings. These stay the shade they were made in. When one sits right at the front of your smile, lightening the teeth around it can make it look darker by comparison, so we talk through whether it would need replacing afterward before you start.
  • Tetracycline and other deep internal stains. Gray or banded discoloration from certain antibiotics taken while teeth were forming, or from heavy fluoride exposure in childhood, sits deep inside the tooth. Whitening may soften it slowly but often not evenly, and veneers or bonding are usually more predictable.
  • One tooth that turned dark. A tooth that darkened after an injury or a root canal often doesn't respond to surface whitening. It needs an exam and X-rays first, and sometimes a different approach for that single tooth.
  • Exposed roots. Root surfaces don't lighten the way enamel does, and they tend to be more sensitive.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding. Because whitening is elective and hasn't been well studied in pregnancy, most dentists suggest waiting.
  • Children and younger teens. Whitening is generally put off until the permanent teeth are fully in and the nerves inside them have matured.

Sensitivity: why it happens and how to keep it down

Peroxide passes through the enamel to reach stains, and on the way it can briefly irritate the nerve inside the tooth. That is why some people feel short, sharp zings during treatment or for a day or two afterward. For most people it fades on its own and leaves no lasting effect.

A few things tend to help:

  • Brushing with a sensitivity toothpaste for a couple of weeks before you start
  • Using a lower-strength gel, or shorter sessions spaced further apart
  • Taking a day or two off the trays if your teeth feel sore
  • Skipping very hot, very cold and acidic food and drink for a day or so
  • Telling us about any existing sensitivity, so we can rule out a cavity, a cracked tooth or worn enamel first

If the sensitivity is strong, or still there after a few days, call or text us and we'll take a look. More in sensitive teeth after whitening.

How long whitening results last

Whitening isn't permanent, because teeth keep meeting the coffee, tea, red wine, dark sauces and tobacco that stained them in the first place. Many people find their shade holds for many months, and some go a couple of years before wanting a refresh, but it varies a great deal from one person to the next. Diet and smoking make the biggest difference.

Keeping your custom trays makes upkeep simple: a short top-up every so often stops the shade drifting back. Rinsing with water after a dark drink, using a straw for iced coffee and keeping your regular cleanings all help. See how long whitening lasts and why results vary for more.

Whitening before veneers or bonding

If veneers, bonding or new crowns are in your plans, order matters. Porcelain and composite are matched to the color of your natural teeth at the time they're made, and they won't lighten later. So if you'd like the rest of your smile brighter, whiten first.

After whitening, the shade usually settles over a week or two, and bonding materials don't grip as well on freshly whitened enamel. For both reasons, many dentists wait around two weeks before matching and bonding new restorations. Your own timeline depends on your plan.

Your digital smile preview helps with this, because you can see lighter natural teeth and new restorations together before committing to either one. Our post on veneers vs whitening helps if you're unsure which you need.

What drives the cost of whitening

Whitening prices vary because the treatment itself varies. You'll have the exact price in writing before anything is done. The main factors:

  • In-office treatment, custom take-home trays, or a combination
  • How many sessions or gel refills your starting shade is likely to need
  • Whether a cleaning, fillings or gum care are needed before whitening
  • Whether an existing crown, filling or bonding will need replacing afterward to match
  • Whether you want a gentler, slower plan with extra desensitizing steps

Hold on to your trays once they're made. Buying gel for later touch-ups usually costs less than starting over from scratch.

Questions, answered

Is in-office or take-home whitening better?

Neither is better for everyone. In-office whitening gives a faster, more noticeable change in one visit, while custom take-home trays work more gradually and are often easier on sensitive teeth. Many people do a session in the office and then use trays to deepen and maintain the result.

Why didn't whitening strips work on my teeth?

Strips use a milder gel and lie flat, so they can miss the curves of your teeth and the areas near the gums. Some stains, such as gray or banded discoloration, respond poorly to any whitening. Crowns, veneers and fillings don't change color at all, which can make results look uneven.

Can tetracycline stains be whitened?

Sometimes partly, but results are slow and often uneven, because the discoloration sits deep inside the tooth. Gray and banded stains are the hardest to lighten. Veneers or bonding are often a more predictable way to cover them, and an exam will show which makes sense for you.

Does insurance cover teeth whitening?

Usually not, because dental plans treat whitening as cosmetic. The exam, cleaning or fillings you may need first can be covered under preventive or basic benefits, depending on your plan. We check your benefits for you.

How soon after whitening can I get veneers or bonding?

Many dentists wait about two weeks. That gives the new shade time to settle, so restorations are matched to your real final color, and it lets bonding materials grip the enamel properly. Your timeline depends on your plan.

What should I avoid after teeth whitening?

For the first day or two, go easy on coffee, tea, red wine, dark sauces, berries and tobacco, since teeth can pick up stain more easily right after treatment. Very hot, cold or acidic food and drink can also trigger sensitivity during that time. Rinsing with water after anything dark helps.
